Topic and objective

TOPIC: Verbal Aptitude — Section 1 of the General Aptitude syllabus, which is common to every GATE paper including CS. Basic English grammar (tenses, articles, adjectives, prepositions, conjunctions, verb-noun agreement, other parts of speech); basic vocabulary (words, idioms and phrases in context); reading and comprehension; narrative sequencing.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide a grammar question from the feature that actually governs it — the head noun for agreement, the first sound for an article, the kind of time expression for a preposition, the fixed particle for an idiom — instead of from the word nearest the blank. Take a word's meaning from the sentence that holds it rather than from memory. Answer a comprehension question by entailment, striking any option that adds a word the passage never uses. And solve a jumbled paragraph as a graph problem: find the sentences that point backwards, order them, and check the result against the options.

I read the four syllabus clauses as four kinds of decision: a feature check (grammar), a substitution check (vocabulary), an entailment check (comprehension), and an ordering check (sequencing). Every previous-year item worked in this lesson is one of the four, and the animation is built around the four decisions rather than around a list of rules.

Prerequisites

  • School English. No linguistics and no formal grammar terminology beyond "noun", "verb", "subject" and "preposition" — each term is glossed the first time it is used.
  • Nothing from the CS syllabus. This section is deliberately independent of the rest of the paper, which is why it is the cheapest 15 marks to protect.
  • Quantitative aptitude — the neighbouring General Aptitude lesson. Its habit of naming the base before computing has an exact analogue here: name the word the blank depends on before choosing.
  • Analytical aptitude — owns deduction and induction as logic. This lesson uses the same entailment test on prose, and the two lessons share the "true but not stated" trap.
  • Graphs and connectivity — the sequencing half of this lesson is a topological ordering of a directed graph. That lesson supplies the vocabulary; this one applies it to sentences.

The four decisions, in one line each

  • Feature. Find the word that governs the blank, then check one feature of it.
  • Substitution. Put the option in the sentence and ask whether the sentence still means one thing.
  • Entailment. Strike any option that adds a word — all, only, must — the passage never uses.
  • Order. Every sentence that points backwards must follow the sentence it points to.

Intuition first: a sentence is a machine with slots

Think of a sentence as a machine with slots in it, and of a grammar question as a question about which part is allowed in a slot. The slot does not ask "which word sounds best here" — it asks for one feature. The article slot asks for a sound. The agreement slot asks for a number. The preposition slot asks for a kind of time expression. The idiom slot asks for a fixed particle. Educational interpretation Under that picture, the commonest wrong answer in this section is the right answer to a neighbouring slot: "are" is correct English, just not for this subject; "since" is correct English, just not with a length of time.

The other half of the section is not grammar at all. Comprehension and sequencing ask what a passage commits to, and in what order its sentences must stand. Both are questions about structure, which is why a picture of the structure answers them faster than a reading of the prose does.

Verb–noun agreement: the head noun decides

The subject is the noun phrase the verb is about. Its head is the one noun that the phrase cannot lose — everything else in the phrase modifies it. Agreement is a match in number (singular or plural) between that head and the verb.

the list of items is on the desk
head = list (singular) → is"of items" is a prepositional phrase modifying "list". It sits between the head and the verb, and it changes nothing about the number of the subject.

Mathematical derivation The rule is a function from the head's number to the verb's form. Two candidate functions are available — head number, and nearest-noun number — and they agree on most sentences, which is exactly why the trap works. They disagree when a plural noun sits between a singular head and its verb, and the exam builds its distractor out of that disagreement.

The two quantifier phrases that flip the head

"A number of" and "the number of" look identical and take opposite verbs, because the head is different in each.

a number of applicants have applied
the number of applicants has risenIn "a number of", the whole phrase is a determiner meaning "many", so the head is "applicants" — plural. In "the number of", "number" is the head — singular — and "of applicants" modifies it.

Mathematical derivation The test is substitution: replace the phrase with "many" or with "the count". "Many applicants have applied" keeps the meaning; "the count of applicants has risen" keeps it too. Only the first is plural, so the two phrases cannot share a verb.

Articles: chosen by sound, not by letter

"A" and "an" are the two indefinite articles. "The" is the definite article and does not vary. The choice between a and an is decided by the first sound of the next word.

a university (first sound /j/, a consonant sound)
an honourable man (h silent, first sound /ɒ/, a vowel sound)
an hour, a European, an MPSpelling is a poor guide: "university" begins with a vowel letter and a consonant sound; "honourable" begins with a consonant letter and a vowel sound.

Mathematical derivation Because the rule reads one feature — vowel sound or not — it can be checked on three words and counted. On the three words of the 2025 paper item, a letter-based rule gets one right and a sound-based rule gets three. That count is computed on the animation tab.

Prepositions of time: a span, a point, a deadline

Time expressions come in three shapes, and each preposition takes one shape.

for + a length (for three weeks, for six months)
since + a starting point (since 2019, since Monday)
within + a deadline (within six months)A length answers "how long"; a point answers "from when"; a deadline answers "by when". "The last three weeks" is a length, so it takes "for".

Mathematical derivation This is a typing rule: each expression has a type and each preposition accepts one type. "Since the last three weeks" fails because a length is not a point, however natural the phrase sounds. The same typing explains "for" with the present perfect — the action fills the span, so the span must be named.

Idioms: the particle is part of the word

An idiom is a fixed group of words whose meaning is not the sum of its parts. A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle — a small word such as down, out, on, for — and the particle changes the meaning.

keep your head down — stay out of trouble
head out — leave
have a head for batting — have a talent for it
a cool head on your shoulders — composureEach of the four is a different idiom that happens to share the word "head". The blank is not a choice of meaning; it is a choice of one fixed word.

Mathematical derivation If each idiom has exactly one legal particle, a four-blank question is a conjunction of four independent tests, and an option tuple is right only when it passes all four. Counting violations per tuple turns a judgement into arithmetic, and the animation does that count.

Vocabulary in context: a scale, or a clue

Two shapes cover almost every vocabulary question. The first is a scale: words that differ only in intensity, so the answer is decided by direction, not by meaning. The second is a context clue: the sentence itself defines the word, usually through a contrast.

dry → arid → parched (rising lack of moisture)
diet → fast → starve (rising lack of food)The arrow means "less of the thing, more intensely". Reversing it — diet → fast → feast — keeps the grammar and destroys the relation, which is why "feast" is a plausible wrong answer rather than a silly one.

Mathematical derivation A scale question is a monotonicity question: the three words must have strictly increasing intensity. That is checkable — rank the words and test the ranks are increasing — and it is what the model does before drawing the ladder.

Comprehension: entailment, not truth

A comprehension question asks which statement the passage entails: which one must be true if the passage is true. Truth in the world is irrelevant, and an option that is true but unstated is wrong.

entailed = true in every situation the passage allowsThe practical test is the absolute word. "All", "only", "must", "never", "the first" make an option stronger than almost any passage. If the passage does not itself use such a word, the option that does is not entailed.

Mathematical derivation This is the same contrapositive reasoning as the analytical-aptitude lesson, applied to prose: from "if the teacher is in the room then all students stand" you may conclude "if some student is not standing then the teacher is not in the room", and nothing about the converse. The animation counts how many options carry an absolute word the passage lacks.

Narrative sequencing: a paragraph is a directed graph

A sentence that points backwards — with a pronoun (he, she, it, they), a demonstrative (this event, these figures), a definite article where the first mention was indefinite (a village … the village), a connector (however, therefore, thus) or a time word (later, finally) — cannot come before the sentence it points to. Write each such obligation as a directed edge, and a valid paragraph order is a topological order of that graph: an order in which every edge points forward.

edge a → b means "a must come before b"
valid order = a permutation respecting every edgeWith four sentences there are only 4! = 24 orders, and four options. So the question is decided by finding enough edges to leave one option standing — not by feeling the paragraph.

Mathematical derivation Three edges chosen well usually leave a single order. In the 2023 paper's item, three edges leave exactly one of the twenty-four permutations; in the 2024 paper's item, three edges again leave exactly one. Both counts are computed by brute force on the animation tab, and the surviving order is then checked against the printed options. A diagram is not a proof that a paragraph is coherent — the edges come from reading — but the count is a proof that only one option is consistent with those edges.

Example 1 — an article, decided by sound

GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, GA Question 1 (one mark). Ravi had ______ younger brother who taught at ______ university. He was widely regarded as ______ honorable man. Select the sequence of articles.

Step 1. Read the first sound, not the first letter. "Younger" begins /j/, a consonant sound. "University" begins /juː/, also a consonant sound. "Honorable" has a silent h, so it begins with a vowel sound.

Mathematical derivation The sequence is a, a, an. Verified fact The published key marks the option "a; a; an". The page also runs the letter-based rule on the same three words and gets "a; an; a", which is wrong twice — the count is shown on the animation tab.

Example 2 — prepositions of time, typed

GATE CSE 2020, GA Question 1 (one mark). Raman is confident of speaking English ______ six months as he has been practising regularly ______ the last three weeks.

Step 1. The second blank carries "the last three weeks" — a length. A length takes for. That alone eliminates every option whose second word is since or in.

Mathematical derivation The first blank is a deadline, so it takes within: within, for. Verified fact The official key for GATE 2020 CS gives "within, for". Note that "for six months" is perfect English — it just means a duration, not a deadline, which is why it is the tempting wrong first blank.

Example 3 — four idioms, four fixed particles

GATE CSE 2024 Set 1, GA Question 6 (two marks). Steve was advised to keep his head (i) ______ before heading (ii) ______ to bat; for, while he had a head (iii) ______ batting, he could only do so with a cool head (iv) ______ his shoulders.

Step 1. Four idioms, not one: keep one's head down (stay out of trouble), head out (leave), a head for something (a talent for it), a cool head on one's shoulders (composure).

Mathematical derivation The tuple is down, out, for, on. Verified fact The published key marks that option. The page counts violations for all four tuples the paper printed: 3, 2, 0 and 3, so exactly one survives. Two of the distractors are grammatical everywhere — "a head on batting" is meaningful — and only idiomatic usage separates them.

Example 4 — a scale of intensity

GATE CSE 2024 Set 1, GA Question 1 (one mark). If the arrow denotes increasing order of intensity, then the meaning of [dry → arid → parched] is analogous to [diet → fast → ______].

Step 1. Name the scale: less and less moisture, more and more severely. Step 2. Name the second scale: less and less food.

Mathematical derivation The third term must be the most extreme lack of food: starve. Verified fact The published key marks "starve". The model ranks both ladders and checks that each is strictly increasing, so the answer is the unique word of maximum rank among the options rather than a matter of taste.

Example 5 — a word defined by its own sentence

GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, GA Question 2 (one mark). The CEO's decision to downsize the workforce was considered myopic because it sacrificed long-term stability to accommodate short-term gains. Replace "myopic" without changing the meaning.

Step 1. The because clause is a definition: the decision gave up the long term for the short term. That is the meaning of "myopic" here — not the eye condition.

Mathematical derivation Shortsighted. Verified fact The published key marks "shortsighted". The other three options — visionary, progressive, innovative — all describe looking forward, so all three have the polarity of the opposite of the clue. The model records that polarity and checks that exactly one option matches it.

Example 6 — a passage, and what it entails

GATE CSE 2023, GA Question 6 (two marks). A country's working population is largely ill; health experts conclude that a complete lack of physical exercise is one of the leading causes; the government decides to give money to those who cycle to work. Based only on this, which statement can be inferred with certainty?

Step 1. Write the premises: (1) more than 75 per cent have serious health issues; (2) lack of exercise is one of the leading causes; (3) the government will incentivise cycling to work.

Mathematical derivation Only one option follows: the government believes that cycling is a form of physical exercise — otherwise the measure would not address the stated cause. Verified fact The published key marks that option. The page checks the three rejected options and finds each carries an absolute or an unsupported claim — everyone will cycle, all health issues will vanish, the experts demanded compulsion — none of which appears in the premises.

Example 7 — two jumbled paragraphs, solved by counting

(a) GATE CSE 2023, GA Question 8 (two marks). Four sentences about Mina: (i) once on the terrace … she notices the man right away; (ii) she begins to pant by the time she has climbed all the stairs; (iii) Mina has bought vegetables and rice at the market, so her bags are heavy; (iv) he was leaning against the parapet.

Edges. (iii) → (ii): the heavy bags explain the panting. (ii) → (i): the stairs come before the terrace. (i) → (iv): "the man" is introduced before "he". Three edges, four sentences.

(b) GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, GA Question 6 (two marks). S: many millennia ago a chunk of the Antarctic Plate collided with the Eurasian Plate; P: this fortuitous geological event generated heat and the rocks rose; R: the cooling of this massive edifice absorbed carbon dioxide; Q: thus geophysicists think of the Himalayas as an active event.

Mathematical derivation The first paragraph's only consistent order is (iii), (ii), (i), (iv); the second's is S, P, R, Q. Verified fact The published keys are D for the 2023 item and the option reading S, P, R, Q — marked C in the 2024 Set 2 key — for the second. The page tries all 24 orders for each, reports that exactly one survives its edges, and then checks that the surviving order is the keyed option. That is a check on the reasoning, not on the paragraph's beauty.

The boundaries: where each rule stops being safe

Each card names a condition the rule depends on and says what happens when it fails. Where a size can be computed, it is.

Agreement: collective nouns and "one of the … who"

"The committee has decided" treats the committee as one body; "the committee have disagreed" treats it as its members. British usage allows both, so a well-set question avoids the case. "One of the students who were late was fined" is not ambiguous: the relative pronoun "who" takes its number from "students", while the main verb after "one of" is singular.

Mathematical derivation Two verbs, two different subjects: "who" is about the students, "was fined" is about the one. Matching both to the same noun is the error, and it is the reason this pattern is a favourite.

Articles: acronyms and silent letters

"An MP" because the letter M is pronounced "em"; "a UN report" because U is pronounced "you". The rule is about the sound of the first letter, not the letter. "An hotel" is old-fashioned British usage and is not the expected answer in an Indian exam paper; "a hotel" is.

Mathematical derivation Pronounce the word, then classify the first sound. Any rule that reads spelling fails on university, European, one-off and hour — four common words, all in the opposite class from their first letter.

Prepositions: "since" with a span is the classic failure

"Since" needs a point: since 2019, since Monday, since the merger. "The last three weeks" is a span, so it takes "for". "In the last three weeks" is also grammatical and means roughly the same as "for", which is why an option using "in" cannot be eliminated on the second blank alone.

Mathematical derivation Eliminate on the blank that is unambiguous, then confirm on the other. In the 2020 item the first blank does the work: "within" is the only option that gives a deadline, and the second blank then agrees.

Idioms: regional and dated usage

Some idioms differ between British and American English ("a quarter of" versus "a quarter to" for time), and some are dated. An exam paper uses idioms that are current in Indian English, which follows British usage closely.

Community observation Coaching discussions of the 2024 idiom item note that two of its four blanks admit a second reading ("head down to bat" is also said in cricket), and that the option set, not the sentence, decides. That is a reported difficulty, not an official statement.

Comprehension: an option can be true and still wrong

"Zombieland's working population is largely ill" is in the passage; "Zombieland should have built cycle lanes" is sensible and absent. The question asks what follows, so the second is wrong however true it is.

Mathematical derivation Entailment is a relation between the passage and the option, not between the world and the option. Any option containing a word the passage lacks — a universal, a superlative, a "must" — is not entailed unless the passage says it.

Sequencing: when the edges do not decide

Three edges may leave two orders standing. Then a second kind of edge is needed: general before particular, cause before effect, or the sentence whose noun is indefinite ("a village") before the one whose noun is definite ("the village").

Mathematical derivation A graph with several topological orders has no unique paragraph, so the paper must supply something more — usually a fixed first or last sentence. If the options differ only in a part you cannot decide, the question is defective; that happens rarely, and guessing the "most natural" reading is the only remaining move.

A counterexample to "the verb agrees with the nearest noun"

"The list of items is on the desk." The nearest noun to the verb is "items", which is plural; the verb is singular. The nearest-noun rule predicts "are" and is wrong.

Mathematical derivation The head-noun rule predicts "is" and is right. Because the two rules disagree here, one sentence is enough to refute the nearest-noun rule — and because they agree everywhere else, the rule survives in most people's practice until a paper tests it. The same counterexample, with a plural head, is "the boxes of paper are heavy": now the nearest noun is singular and the verb is plural.

Six beliefs that cost marks, and the smallest thing that corrects each

Each entry gives the belief as it is usually held, the correction, and a counterexample small enough to check in the exam hall.

1. "The verb agrees with the noun next to it."

Correction. The verb agrees with the head of the subject. A prepositional phrase between them is a modifier and carries no number of its own.

Counterexample. "The list of items is on the desk." Nearest noun plural, verb singular. Mathematical derivation The two rules disagree only in this configuration, which is why the belief survives ordinary writing.

2. "A word beginning with a vowel letter takes 'an'."

Correction. The article follows the first sound.

Counterexample. "A university", "a European", "an hour", "an MP". Verified fact The 2025 Set 1 item is built on exactly this: "a university" and "an honorable man" in one sentence, and the letter rule fails both. GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Q1, key "a; a; an".

3. "Since and for are interchangeable with a period of time."

Correction. "For" takes a length; "since" takes a starting point.

Counterexample. "For the last three weeks" is right; "since the last three weeks" is not. Verified fact GATE CSE 2020 GA Q1 is decided by this, with "within, for" as the key.

4. "Any grammatical phrase is acceptable in an idiom."

Correction. An idiom has one fixed particle. Grammaticality is not the test; fixedness is.

Counterexample. "He had a head on batting" parses, and means something, but is not the idiom. Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q6, key "down, out, for, on".

5. "A true statement answers a comprehension question."

Correction. Only an entailed statement does. The question is about the passage, not the world.

Counterexample. From the Zombieland passage, "everyone will now cycle to work" is a sensible hope and is not entailed by a subsidy. Verified fact GATE CSE 2023 GA Q6 rejects exactly that option and keys the "government believes cycling is exercise" option.

6. "A paragraph can be built by reading the options."

Correction. Build the edges first, then compare. Options are a check on your order, not a source of it — four options invite pattern-matching on the first letter.

Counterexample. In the 2023 Mina item, the option that begins with (i) looks natural because (i) reads like an opening, and it is wrong: (i) contains "once on the terrace", which presupposes the climb in (ii). Verified fact GATE CSE 2023 GA Q8, key (iii), (ii), (i), (iv).

Closely confused pairs, side by side

Agreement versus proximity. The head noun decides the verb; the nearest noun only tempts. "The list of items is" against "the boxes of paper are".

Letter versus sound. "A university" (consonant sound) against "an umbrella" (vowel sound), both spelled with a vowel letter.

Length versus point versus deadline. "For three weeks" (length), "since March" (point), "within three weeks" (deadline). Only the middle one is a moment in time.

Synonym versus intensity step. "Arid" is not a synonym of "dry"; it is the next step. A scale question is about direction and order, which a thesaurus cannot supply.

Entailment versus plausibility. "The subsidy will not work" is plausible; "the government believes cycling is exercise" is entailed. Only the second answers the question.

Reference versus sequence. A pronoun creates an ordering constraint; a time word creates a different one. "He" must follow "the man"; "later" must follow whatever it is later than. Both are edges, and both must hold at once.

How this section is actually asked

What follows describes the shapes these questions take in the papers named, with a source for each. The paper does not label its questions by syllabus heading, so the grouping is mine and rests on what each question tests. Nothing here is a forecast of the 2027 paper.

Shape 1 — the multi-blank grammar item

Two to four blanks in one sentence or a short passage, each blank testing a different feature: an article here, a preposition there, a particle in the third. Two marks. The work is one decision per blank, and a tuple is right only if every blank is right.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q6 (two marks, four idiom blanks) and GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Q1 (one mark, three article blanks) are both this shape. Community observation A public archive files 26 GATE CSE questions under "English grammar" and 11 under "grammatical error" across 2010–2026; the tagging is the archive's.

Shape 2 — the word in its context

A single sentence with one highlighted word, and four options of which one can replace it without changing the meaning. The sentence always contains the definition, usually in a because, a despite or an appositive.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Q2 ("myopic" replaced by "shortsighted"). GATE CSE 2013 and 2012 asked the same shape with "nadir" and "mitigate". Community observation The archive lists 138 GATE CSE items under "most appropriate word", which is its largest verbal tag.

Shape 3 — the relation between two words

An analogy, either as a scale of intensity ("dry → arid → parched") or as a pair relation ("bird : nest"). One mark. The work is to name the relation before looking at the options.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q1 (intensity scale, key "starve") and GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Q1 (the same shape with "bothered → … → daunted"). The relation must be named: "increasing intensity" is not "same meaning".

Shape 4 — the passage and its inference

Three or four sentences of fact, then "based only on the above, which can be inferred with certainty". Two marks. The work is to write the premises down and reject any option that adds a universal, a cause the passage does not name, or a recommendation.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2023 GA Q6 (the Zombieland passage, key: the government believes cycling is exercise). GATE CSE 2022 GA Q6 and GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 GA Q61 are the same shape on other subjects.

Shape 5 — the jumbled paragraph

Four sentences labelled P–S or (i)–(iv), four orders offered. Two marks. The work is to find the opener and one mandatory pair, which usually eliminates three options without reading for style.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2023 GA Q8 (Mina, key (iii), (ii), (i), (iv)) and GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Q6 (Himalayas, key S, P, R, Q). Community observation The archive files five GATE CSE items under "sentence ordering", and narrative sequencing appears in the GA syllabus for 2027 exactly as quoted in the Overview.

What to expect, stated carefully

In the papers this lesson checked, verbal aptitude supplied between two and six of the fifteen General Aptitude marks: two questions in each of 2024 Set 1, 2024 Set 2 and 2025 Set 1, and four in 2023. That is a count taken from an archive's tagging of those papers, so it is a Community observation and not an official distribution. The 2027 paper may differ in either direction.

Two patterns are worth acting on. First, the recent papers prefer multi-blank items to single-word ones, so a candidate who can decide only one of three blanks still loses the mark: practise whole tuples. Second, comprehension and sequencing are the two-mark questions, so they are worth roughly twice as much as the vocabulary items, and they reward a procedure rather than a vocabulary list. If time is short, learn the entailment test and the opener-and-pair routine before learning any word list.

MCQGATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Q1

Ravi had ______ younger brother who taught at ______ university. He was widely regarded as ______ honorable man. Select the option with the correct sequence of articles to fill the blanks.

  1. a; a; an
  2. a; an; a
  3. an; a; an
  4. an; an; a

Answer: A — a; a; an

"Younger" begins with the consonant sound /j/, "university" with /juː/; both take "a". "Honorable" has a silent h, so it begins with a vowel sound and takes "an".

Why B appears. It applies the letter rule to "university". Why C and D appear. They apply it to "younger" as well, treating the letter y as a vowel.

The rule. Pronounce the word, then classify the first sound. Never classify the first letter.

MCQGATE CSE 2020 GA Q1

Raman is confident of speaking English ______ six months as he has been practising regularly ______ the last three weeks.

  1. during, for
  2. for, since
  3. for, in
  4. within, for

Answer: D — within, for

"Within six months" states a deadline. "For the last three weeks" states a length, and "for" is the preposition for a length.

Why B appears. "Since" takes a point in time ("since Monday"), not a length. Why A appears. "During six months" means throughout a period, not by the end of it. Why C appears. "For six months" is a duration, so it cannot state when the skill will arrive.

The rule. Type the time expression first — length, point or deadline — then pick the preposition that accepts that type.

MCQGATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q1

If the arrow denotes increasing order of intensity, then the meaning of the words [dry → arid → parched] is analogous to [diet → fast → ______]. Which one of the given options is appropriate to fill the blank?

  1. starve
  2. feast
  3. eat
  4. cook

Answer: A — starve

Both ladders rise in intensity: less moisture, then less food. "Starve" is the extreme of lack of food.

Why B appears. "Feast" is the opposite end of the scale — it is grammatically perfect and reverses the direction. Why C and D appear. Both are neutral verbs with no intensity at all, so they cannot occupy the third rung of a ladder.

The rule. Name the relation before reading the options. Here the relation is direction, and only one option points the same way.

MCQGATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Q6

Sequence the sentences into a coherent passage. P: This fortuitous geological event generated a colossal amount of energy and heat that resulted in the rocks rising to an average height of 4 km across the contact zone. Q: Thus, the geophysicists tend to think of the Himalayas as an active geological event rather than as a static geological feature. R: The natural process of the cooling of this massive edifice absorbed large quantities of atmospheric carbon dioxide. S: Many millennia ago, a breakaway chunk of bedrock from the Antarctic Plate collided with the massive Eurasian Plate.

  1. QPSR
  2. QSPR
  3. SPRQ
  4. SRPQ

Answer: C — SPRQ

S introduces the collision. P's "this fortuitous geological event" is that collision. R's "this massive edifice" is the risen rock. Q's "Thus" concludes.

Why A and B appear. Both open with "Thus", which can only conclude — a sentence with a concluding connector can never be first. Why D appears. S then R breaks the chain: "this massive edifice" has nothing to refer to until the rocks have risen in P.

The rule. Every demonstrative ("this event", "this edifice") and every connector ("thus", "however", "therefore") is an edge. Place the edges, then read the options.

MCQGATE CSE 2023 GA Q8

Which one of the following sentence sequences creates a coherent narrative? (i) Once on the terrace, on her way to her small room in the corner, she notices the man right away. (ii) She begins to pant by the time she has climbed all the stairs. (iii) Mina has bought vegetables and rice at the market, so her bags are heavy. (iv) He was leaning against the parapet, watching the traffic below.

  1. (i), (ii), (iv), (iii)
  2. (ii), (iii), (i), (iv)
  3. (iv), (ii), (i), (iii)
  4. (iii), (ii), (i), (iv)

Answer: D — (iii), (ii), (i), (iv)

(iii) introduces Mina and the heavy bags; (ii) explains the panting on the stairs; (i) puts her on the terrace, where she sees the man; (iv) says what "he" was doing.

Why A appears. It starts with "once on the terrace" before she has climbed anything. Why C appears. It starts with "he", whose antecedent has not been named. Why B appears. The market purchase comes after the climb.

The rule. Find the sentence with no backward reference: it is the opener. Here only (iii) qualifies.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct? Select all that apply.

  1. The list of items is on the desk.
  2. A number of applicants has applied for the post.
  3. The number of applicants has risen this year.
  4. Each of the two proposals has merit.

Answer: A, C, D

A: head "list" is singular, so "is". C: head "number" is singular, so "has". D: "each" is singular, so "has".

Why B is wrong. "A number of" is a determiner meaning "many", so the head is "applicants" and the verb must be "have". It is the mirror image of C, which is why the pair is worth memorising together.

The rule. Find the head, then match the verb. "A number of" and "the number of" differ in head, not in meaning alone.

MSQOriginal practice

A passage states: "The city banned cars from the market street on weekdays. Shopkeepers reported a fall in sales within a month." Based only on this, which statements can be inferred with certainty? Select all that apply.

  1. Sales in the market street fell after the ban began.
  2. The ban caused the fall in sales.
  3. All shopkeepers in the city lost money.
  4. Shopkeepers reported the fall.

Answer: A, D

The passage states that sales fell within a month of the ban, and that shopkeepers reported it. Both are stated.

Why B is wrong. "Reported a fall … within a month" gives a time order, not a cause. Causal language — caused, because, led to — is an addition. Why C is wrong. "All" and "in the city" both exceed the passage, which speaks of one street's shopkeepers.

The rule. Time order is not causation, and a universal is never entailed by a particular.

MCQOriginal practice

Sequence the sentences into a coherent passage. P: However, the sensors drifted within a month and the readings became unreliable. Q: The team therefore switched to a cheaper sensor that needed weekly recalibration but stayed accurate. R: The initial design used high-precision sensors that were expected to run for a year without maintenance. S: A start-up built a low-cost air-quality monitor for schools in Nagpur.

  1. SRPQ
  2. RSPQ
  3. SPRQ
  4. RPSQ

Answer: A — SRPQ

S introduces the start-up and the product with full nouns. R gives the initial design. P's "However" contrasts with R's expectation of a year. Q's "therefore" answers the drift in P.

Why B and D appear. Both open with R, which contains "the initial design" — a definite phrase that needs the design to have been introduced. Why C appears. It puts P before R, so "However" contrasts with nothing.

The rule. Two edges — expectation before contrast, problem before response — eliminate three options. This question is written for the lesson, not taken from a paper.

Revision sheet — the whole section on one screen

Rules, decision procedures and traps, in the order you would want them in the last hour before the paper.

Grammar in six lines

agreement: verb matches the HEAD noun, not the nearest
a / an: by the first SOUND (a university, an hour)
for + length · since + point · within + deadline
idiom: one fixed particle (head down, head out, a head for)
tense: present perfect for a span reaching now
modifier: a participle attaches to the subject it describes

Each line is one feature, and a question tests one feature at a time. Find the feature before you find the word.

Decision procedures

  • Agreement. Delete every prepositional phrase between subject and verb. What is left is the head; match the verb to it.
  • Article. Say the word aloud, classify the first sound, then choose.
  • Preposition. Type the expression — length, point, deadline — then pick the preposition that accepts that type.
  • Idiom. Say the whole phrase with each option and ask which one you have actually heard. Meaning beats grammar here.
  • Vocabulary. Name the relation (scale, contrast, cause) from the sentence, then test options against the relation, not against the dictionary.
  • Comprehension. Write the premises in one line each. Strike any option with a universal, a cause, or a recommendation the passage lacks.
  • Sequencing. Find the opener, then one mandatory pair, then check the closer. Read the surviving option once.

Traps, in one line each

  • The nearest noun is not the subject.
  • "A university" and "an hour": letter is not sound.
  • "Since the last three weeks" is a length with a point's preposition.
  • An idiom's particle is not a free choice.
  • A grammatical phrase can still be unidiomatic.
  • "Feast" completes the ladder and reverses it.
  • True in the world is not entailed by the passage.
  • "All", "only" and "must" in an option are almost always additions.
  • A sentence starting with Thus, However or Therefore cannot open a paragraph.
  • Reading the options first is how the wrong opener looks natural.

Three questions to ask before answering anything here

Which word does this blank depend on — the head noun, the next word's sound, the time expression, or the idiom? What is the option adding that the sentence or passage does not contain? And if this is a sequencing item, which sentence could not possibly stand first?

Those three questions settle most of this section. The rest of the library covers what is next to it: quantitative aptitude for the numerical half of General Aptitude, analytical aptitude for deduction and analogy as logic, spatial aptitude for the figure questions, and graphs and connectivity for the topological ordering that a jumbled paragraph is.

  • Standard school-grammar treatments of subject–verb agreement by head noun, of the indefinite article as a function of the following sound, and of the prepositional typing of time expressions (for a duration, since a point, within a deadline). These are the derivations on the Build-up tab.

Community discussion, labelled as such

The mark counts and the shape counts on the GATE patterns tab come from public question archives that tag GATE CSE General Aptitude items by topic and year, and from worked-solution discussions of the same questions. That tagging is the archive's, not the exam's: the paper does not label its questions, and different archives file the same question under different headings. The lesson treats such tagging as a Community observation and uses it only to show that a shape recurs.

Where two published solutions to the same item disagree — as they do about the second blank of the 2020 preposition item, where "in the last three weeks" is defensible — the lesson says so and eliminates on the blank that is unambiguous rather than pretending the other blank decides. Items dated 2026 are named nowhere in this lesson, because a reconstruction of a recent paper cannot be checked against the paper itself here.

What is computed rather than cited

Every count on the canvas is computed by this page's model and re-checked against the definition it came from: the head noun and the nearest noun of each agreement sentence are found by rule and the two verdicts compared; the article rule is run twice, once on letters and once on sounds, and the two scores compared; the time expressions are typed and each preposition tested against its type; the four idiom tuples are scored by counting violations against a table of fixed particles; the two intensity ladders are ranked and checked to be strictly increasing; the polarity of each vocabulary option is compared with the clue's polarity; the comprehension options are tested for absolute words the premises do not contain; and both jumbled paragraphs are solved by trying all twenty-four orders against their reference edges, with the count of surviving orders and the surviving order itself reported. The count of passing checks is printed under the canvas.
