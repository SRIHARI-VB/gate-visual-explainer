Topic and objective

TOPIC: Ambiguity of a context-free grammar — one string with two parse trees — and inherent ambiguity of a context-free language, where every grammar for it is ambiguous. The page also owns the decision procedure for one string, the reduction that makes the general question undecidable, and the two repairs that actually work.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State ambiguity as two distinct parse trees (equivalently two distinct leftmost derivations) and say why two derivation orders are not two trees; count the trees of a given string with a span table and read the count as a proof; repair an ambiguous grammar by levelling precedence and by the matched/unmatched split, checking that the language did not change; recognise an inherently ambiguous language and name the theorem that settles it; and sort the four decision problems into decidable and undecidable.

I read the request as ambiguity as a decision problem, not another tour of grammars. Context-free grammars and push-down automata owns the definition of a grammar and already prints the tree counts 2 and 5 for two strings; Post’s correspondence problem owns the reduction in the abstract. This page owns what neither does: a general counter that decides any string against any grammar, the two dangling-else repairs with the one that fails, the standard grammar for the inherently ambiguous language counted out, and the reduction run on two instances.

1. Intuition, and 2. the formal definition

Intuition. “I saw the man with the telescope.” One sentence, two readings: I used a telescope, or the man had one. The words are identical and the structure is not. A grammar that permits both readings has built the sentence two ways.

Formal. A parse tree for a string $$w$$ in the language of a grammar $$G$$ has the start symbol at the root, one internal node per rule application, and $$w$$ as its leaves read left to right. $$G$$ is ambiguous when some $$w$$ in $$L(G)$$ has two distinct parse trees. Equivalently, when some $$w$$ has two distinct leftmost derivations — derivations that always rewrite the leftmost nonterminal. VERIFIED FACT

Each leftmost derivation corresponds to exactly one tree, and each tree to exactly one leftmost derivation. That bijection is why the two statements are the same statement. (Columbia CSTheory handout, read 2026-11-05.)

3. The object that is counted

Not a derivation sequence. A tree's independent branches may be expanded in either order, so one tree can be written as several sequences.

Computed on this page: i+i has 1 tree and 2 sequences; i+i+i has 2 trees and 16 sequences. A test built on sequences would call both ambiguous. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

4. The decision procedure for one string

Fill a table cell for every piece of the string, shortest first. A cell holds the number of trees that build that piece from one nonterminal. One-letter cells come from the rules that rewrite straight to a terminal; longer cells are a sum over rules and over the ways to cut the piece among the rule’s parts.

About $$n^2/2$$ cells, about $$n \cdot |R|$$ work each, so cubic. A count of 1 proves unambiguity for that string and a count of 2 proves ambiguity. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

5. Boundaries: when a repair exists

Precedence and associativity are always repairable by splitting one nonterminal into a level per rank. Dangling else is repairable by the matched/unmatched split, and only by the version whose else branch is itself unmatched.

Nothing repairs an inherently ambiguous language: Ginsburg and Ullian proved in 1966 that no grammar for {aibjck : i=j or j=k} escapes it. VERIFIED FACT

6. Edge cases in one line each

Ambiguity on exactly one string: S → A | B, A → a, B → a gives 2 trees for a and none for aa.

Infinitely many trees: S → SS | a | ε has no finite count for the empty string.

Epsilon rules create trees without consuming a character, so a “count” can diverge. The counter reports that instead of returning a number.

Example 1: one string, counted cell by cell

Grammar: E → E + E | E * E | i. String: i+i+i, five characters.

The six non-empty pieces that an E can build are [0,1), [2,3), [4,5), [0,3), [2,5) and [0,5). Their counts, computed: 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 and 2. The pieces [1,2) and [3,4) are the operator +, which no E builds, so they hold 0 and are not shown.

The last cell is the answer: two trees, one with the first + at the top, one with the second. The grammar is ambiguous. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 2: the two trees of i+i*i

(i+i)*i — root is *, left child is the sum. At i = 2 this is 8.

i+(i*i) — root is +, right child is the product. At i = 2 this is 6.

The string is one string. The value is not one value. That is the whole cost of an ambiguous grammar, and it is why a compiler refuses one. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 3: the repair, and its audit

E → E + T | T, T → T * F | F, F → i | ( E ).

The six counts fall from 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42 to 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1.

The language is unchanged: all 363 strings over the three symbols up to length five were counted in both grammars and none changed sides, and all 15 strings the language contains up to length seven have exactly one tree. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 4: two repairs of the dangling else

Witness ictictxex — “if c then if c then x else x”.

Repair that fails: U → ictS | ictMeS gives 2 trees, because the statement after else can still be read as matched or unmatched.

Repair that works: U → ictS | ictMeU gives 1. Across 782 candidate strings the two grammars agree on the language and the repaired one never exceeds 1.

Example 5: the reduction, run twice

Dominoes (a | aa) and (ab | b). The grammar built from them has 10 rules.

The two halves share 2 strings; each carries 2 trees; the shortest shared string is aabdc. The 90 strings in only one half carry 1 tree.

Replace the second domino with (ab | bb) and the halves share 0 strings; the largest count over the 77 one-sided strings is 1. Ambiguity appears exactly when a match exists.

Counterexample: a grammar ambiguous on exactly one string

S → A | B, A → a, B → a. The language is the single string a.

Computed: a has 2 trees (through A and through B), aa has 0, b has 0. The grammar is ambiguous, and the ambiguity lives on exactly one string of a one-string language.

This is the smallest possible ambiguous grammar, and it kills the belief that ambiguity needs a long string or a recursive rule. Two unit rules that reach the same string are enough.

Infinitely many trees

S → SS | a | ε gives the empty string infinitely many trees: any tree for ε can be wrapped in another S → SS with an ε beside it.

The counter on this page reports growth without bound rather than a number. GATE CSE 2003 Question 51 uses S → aSb | SS | ε, which behaves the same way.

Epsilon rules change the count

An ε rule lets a nonterminal vanish, so a rule can take a piece of the string with some of its parts contributing nothing. Those empty contributions are still tree nodes.

Consequence: two grammars with the same rules except for one ε alternative can disagree about ambiguity. S → aS | ε is unambiguous; S → aS | Sa | ε has 32 trees for aaaaa.

The empty string

If ε is in the language, the question “how many trees does the empty string have” is legitimate and the answer may be infinite. A grammar for a non-empty language can still be infinitely ambiguous on ε alone.

Practical rule: when a question mentions ε, count trees for ε separately. It is the one string with no characters to constrain the tree.

Bounded languages are the exception

For a grammar whose language is bounded — contained in w1* w2* … for fixed words — ambiguity is decidable; that is one of the four results in Ginsburg and Ullian (1966). Every language over a one-letter alphabet is bounded.

So “ambiguity is undecidable” is a statement about general grammars, not about every grammar. VERIFIED FACT

Where the boundary sits

Ambiguity of one string against one grammar: decidable, cubic.

Ambiguity of one grammar: undecidable in general, decidable for regular, bounded and LL/LR grammars.

Inherent ambiguity of one language: undecidable, and for specific languages settled case by case with Ogden’s lemma.

1. “Two derivations, so it is ambiguous”

Wrong. Two derivation sequences are usually just one tree written in two orders. i+i has 1 tree and 2 sequences; i+i+i has 2 trees and 16 sequences.

Right. Two distinct trees, or two distinct leftmost derivations. Those two agree by construction.

2. “This grammar is ambiguous, so the language is too”

Wrong. Ambiguity of a grammar is a fact about those rules. The expression grammar is ambiguous and its language is not: the levelled grammar generates the same strings with one tree each.

Right. Inherent ambiguity needs a proof about every grammar, usually through Ogden’s lemma.

3. “Left recursion makes a grammar ambiguous”

Wrong. E → E + T | T is left recursive and unambiguous, with 1 tree for every string. S → aS | ε is right recursive and unambiguous.

Right. Left recursion causes a problem for a particular parsing method, not ambiguity. Count the trees.

4. “Undecidable means nobody knows the answer”

Wrong. Undecidable means no single algorithm answers every instance. For each fixed grammar and string the answer is a finite computation, and this page runs it.

Right. Separate the instance from the class. One string is decidable; all grammars is not.

5. “The matched/unmatched repair always works”

Wrong. With U → ictS | ictMeS the repaired grammar still has 2 trees for ictictxex. Only U → ictS | ictMeU gives 1.

Right. Check the count. The difference is one symbol and it is invisible by eye.

6. “A longer string must have more trees”

Wrong. The count is not monotone in length. anbncn has exactly 2 trees for every n, while aaaaa under S → aS | Sa | ε has 32.

Right. Count for the string you were given; do not extrapolate from a neighbour.

The four shapes, and the procedure for each

Shape 1 — which of these grammars is ambiguous? For each option, look for a short string with two trees. Try the smallest: one letter, then a repeated letter, then a mixed string. Two rules that reach the same string are enough.

Shape 2 — how many parse trees does this string have? Fill the span table. For an operator grammar with k operands the answer is the Catalan number at k−1: 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42.

Shape 3 — is this language inherently ambiguous? A grammar example never answers it. The known inherently ambiguous families are {aibjck : i=j or j=k} and {aibjckdl : i=j and k=l} ∪ {i=l and j=k}. Shape 4 — what is decidable? Use the three-object table.

The table to memorise

one string, one grammar — decidable, cubic

one grammar, all strings — undecidable

one language, all grammars — undecidable

regular grammar — decidable, product construction

bounded grammar — decidable (Ginsburg and Ullian)

What a question is really testing

Almost every wrong answer on this topic answers a different question from the one asked. Before writing anything, underline the object: a string, a grammar, or a language.

If the option says “the grammar is ambiguous because…”, the reason must be a string with two trees. If it says “the language is ambiguous”, the reason must be about every grammar.

Fast routes, and their assumptions

Catalan applies only to a grammar with no precedence written in, where every binary tree is legal.

“Left and right recursion on the same nonterminal means ambiguous” is a community rule of thumb. It is true for S → aS | Sa (32 trees for aaaaa) but it is not a definition and it does not hold in general. COMMUNITY OBSERVATION

Never use a shortcut as the answer’s justification when a two-tree witness is one line away.

MSQ GATE CSE 2026 Set 2, Q19

Which of the following grammars is/are ambiguous?

I. S → aSb | ε
II. E → E+E | E*E | id
III. S → aS | Sa | ε
IV. S → aS | ε

  1. I and II only
  2. II and III only
  3. I, II and III
  4. II, III and IV

Answer: (2), II and III. Computed by this page’s counter: I has 1 tree for every string tested (62 strings up to length five); II reaches 2 trees at i+i+i; III reaches 32 trees at aaaaa; IV has 1 tree for every string.

III is the one people miss. a alone already has two trees, one through aS and one through Sa, so the count for an is 2n. IV has only one recursion direction and is unambiguous.

The question text and the key are from GATE Overflow’s page for the question, read 2026-11-05. The answer is reproduced by counting, not quoted.

MCQ GATE CSE 2003, Q51

Let G = ({S}, {a,b}, R, S) be a context-free grammar with R = {S → aSb | SS | ε}. Which of the following statements is true?

  1. G is not ambiguous
  2. There exist x, y ∈ L(G) such that xy ∉ L(G)
  3. There is a deterministic push-down automaton that accepts L(G)
  4. We can find a deterministic finite state automaton that accepts L(G)

Answer: (3). L(G) is the balanced-parenthesis language, with a as an opening bracket; a deterministic push-down automaton pushes on a and pops on b.

Option 1 is false and the counter shows it: the count for the empty string and for a grows without bound, because any tree can be wrapped in a further S → SS with an ε beside it. Option 2 is false because L(G) is closed under concatenation by the rule S → SS. Option 4 is false because a finite automaton cannot count nesting.

Note what option 1 shows: “ambiguous” and “not deterministic context-free” are independent. An ambiguous grammar can generate a language that a deterministic push-down automaton accepts.

NAT original practice

The grammar is E → E + E | E * E | i. How many distinct parse trees does the string i + i + i + i have?

Answer: 5. Four operands, and the count is the Catalan number at k−1 = 3, which is 5. The counter on this page returns 5, and the Catalan closed form agrees.

The three wrong answers to expect are 4 (counting the places to put the top operator), 8 (doubling for associativity) and 14 (using the Catalan number at 4, which is the answer for five operands).

MCQ original practice

Which of the following languages is inherently ambiguous?

  1. { anbn : n ≥ 1 }
  2. { aibjck : i = j or j = k, i,j,k ≥ 1 }
  3. { anbncm : n,m ≥ 1 }
  4. { w ∈ {a,b}* : w has as many a as b }

Answer: (2). The overlap strings anbncn belong to both families, and Ginsburg and Ullian (1966) proved no grammar escapes that. The grammar on this page shows the mechanism: 2 trees for every anbncn, 1 off the overlap.

(1) has the unambiguous grammar S → aSb | ab. (3) has S → aSb | A, A → cA | c. (4) is not even context-free, so it cannot be an inherently ambiguous context-free language: the question is about context-free languages.

Option (4) is the trap for anyone who reads “inherently ambiguous” as “not obviously unambiguous”. Membership in the class comes first.

MSQ original practice

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

I. Ambiguity of a given string with respect to a given grammar is decidable.
II. Every regular language has an unambiguous grammar.
III. If a grammar is unambiguous, its language is deterministic context-free.
IV. If a language is inherently ambiguous, no grammar for it is unambiguous.

  1. I, II and IV only
  2. I and II only
  3. II, III and IV only
  4. All four

Answer: (1). I: the span table decides it in cubic time. II: a deterministic finite automaton gives a right-linear grammar with one tree per string, computed here on 510 strings with a maximum of 1. IV is the definition of inherent ambiguity.

III is false, and it is the interesting one. Unambiguity of a grammar does not give determinism of the language: an unambiguous grammar can still be non-deterministic, and the direction that does hold is the other one — every deterministic context-free language has an unambiguous grammar, not conversely.

MCQ original practice

Take S → i c t S | i c t S e S | x, and repair it with S → M | U, M → i c t M e M | x, U → i c t S | i c t M e S. What is the number of parse trees of i c t i c t x e x in the repaired grammar?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4

Answer: (2). This repair does not work. The inner statement i c t x e x can be built as M or as U, so the outer statement has two trees. The page computes 2 for exactly this grammar.

Replacing the last rule by U → i c t S | i c t M e U gives 1, because an unmatched statement may only appear where an unmatched statement is required. Option 1 is the answer for the correct repair, which is the point of the question.

NAT original practice

For the grammar S → A | B, A → a, B → a, how many strings of L(G) have more than one parse tree?

Answer: 1. The language is the single string a, and it has two trees, one through A and one through B. Computed: a gives 2, aa gives 0.

A tempting answer is 0, on the reasoning that a one-string language must be trivial. Trivial languages can still be generated ambiguously; what makes this language not inherently ambiguous is that another grammar, S → a, has one tree.

The five facts to carry

1. A grammar is ambiguous when one string in its language has two distinct parse trees, equivalently two distinct leftmost derivations. Derivation sequences are not the test.

2. The trees of one string against one grammar are counted by a span table in cubic time, so that question is decidable and the count is a proof.

3. A language is inherently ambiguous when every grammar for it is ambiguous. {aibjck : i=j or j=k} is the standard example (Ginsburg and Ullian, 1966).

4. Ambiguity of a grammar is undecidable, by reduction from Post’s correspondence problem (Cantor 1962; Floyd 1962; Chomsky and Schützenberger 1963). 5. Regular grammars are decidable, and every regular language has an unambiguous grammar.

The three objects

string + grammar — decidable, cubic

grammar — undecidable

language — undecidable

regular grammar — decidable; bounded grammar — decidable

The repairs

precedence: one nonterminal per rank, E → E+T | T

dangling else: U → ictS | ictMeU, never ictMeS

nothing repairs an inherently ambiguous language

Numbers from this page
