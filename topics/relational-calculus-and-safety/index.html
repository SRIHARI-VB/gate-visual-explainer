Topic and objective

TOPIC: Relational calculus — the tuple calculus and the domain calculus, their atoms, quantifiers and free variables, the safety condition that makes them answerable, and the Codd equivalence that makes them the same language as the basic relational algebra.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read any tuple-calculus or domain-calculus expression and say what relation it denotes; translate a calculus expression into relational algebra and back; decide whether an expression is safe by binding its free variables and by the growth test; and decide all four options of the previous-year items in this topic without tracing anything.

I read this as the declarative half of the relational model. The prerequisite lesson owns the three-notation overview — one scene of calculus inside a lesson about the algebra and SQL. This one owns the calculus itself: quantifiers, binding, safety, and where the language stops.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Section 9 of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, published by IIT Madras, reads: “ER-model. Relational model: relational algebra, tuple calculus, SQL. Integrity constraints, normal forms. File organization, indexing (e.g., B and B+ trees). Transactions and concurrency control.”

Educational interpretation Class A by name. “Tuple calculus” is named verbatim, so this is not an extension of the syllabus; it is a named clause. Domain calculus is not named, but the 2026 paper used its notation, so it is treated here as part of the same clause.

Community observation Calculus has appeared in the papers as a one-mark item on safety or on equivalence. That is an observation about the papers read for this lesson, not a forecast for 2027.

Prerequisites, and what is assumed

The six relational-algebra operators, the notion of a relation as a set of tuples, and the difference between the algebra and SQL are built in Relational Algebra, Tuple Calculus and SQL. The connectives, the quantifiers, and free against bound variables are in First-Order Logic. Sets, Cartesian products and subsets are in Sets and Set Operations.

Assumption Every relation is finite, every attribute domain is infinite unless a question says otherwise, and a tuple is a total function on the schema (no NULLs) except where the 2017 item is discussed, which flags its NULL explicitly.

The five ideas

1. Calculus describes the answer; the algebra computes it.

2. A shared variable between two relations is a join.

3. A universal quantifier is a search for a counterexample, and over an empty relation it succeeds vacuously.

4. Safety is one condition: every value in the answer comes from dom(P).

5. Safe calculus, safe domain calculus and the basic algebra are one language — and recursion is outside it.

Intuition first: two colleagues and one filing cabinet

A filing cabinet holds index cards. The first colleague gives you instructions: open the drawer, keep the cards with SA written on them, then copy out only the course column. The second writes a description on a slip of paper: “all courses c such that some card exists with SA and c on it”. Both produce the same pile of cards. The first is relational algebra. The second is relational calculus.

Educational interpretation The description has one property the instructions do not: it can say all. “Every course in the Biology list” is one clause on the slip, and it is a division, a double difference, or a counting argument when you must write it as instructions. That single word is why the calculus exists, and it is also where its danger lies.

The tuple calculus, precisely

An expression is { t | P(t) }: the set of all tuples t for which the formula P is true. t is a tuple variable; t[A] is the value of t on attribute A; t ∈ r says t is a tuple of relation r.

A formula is built from three atoms and nothing else: s ∈ r; s[x] Θ u[y] for one of the six comparisons; and s[x] Θ c for a constant. From atoms: ¬P, P ∧ Q, P ∨ Q, P ⇒ Q, and the quantifiers ∃s ∈ r (P) and ∀s ∈ r (P).

Verified fact This is the standard definition: Silberschatz, Korth and Sudarshan give the three atoms and the two quantifier forms exactly this way in the formal-relational-query-language chapter of Database System Concepts. Note the deliberate absence of a ∉ atom.

Free, bound, and the schema of the answer

A variable is bound when a quantifier captures it and free otherwise. The answer tuple is built from the free variables, in the order they are written. In { ⟨u⟩ | ∃v ∃w ( ⟨u,v⟩ ∈ R ∧ ⟨v,w⟩ ∈ S ) }, the variable u is free and v, w are bound, so the answer has one column.

Mathematical derivation Deciding the schema first is not a stylistic preference. An equivalence question compares two expressions as sets of tuples, so a wrong arity or a wrong column order makes the comparison meaningless before any row is examined.

The domain calculus, precisely

An expression is { ⟨x1, …, xn⟩ | P(x1, …, xn) }, where each xi is a domain variable ranging over attribute values rather than over tuples. Membership is positional: ⟨x1, …, xn⟩ ∈ r means the first component is r’s first attribute.

Verified fact The domain calculus is the theoretical basis of query-by-example, in the same way the algebra is the basis of SQL. The two calculi are equivalent in power once both are restricted to safe expressions — the statement of Codd’s relational completeness, and the answer to GATE CSE 2006’s expressive-power item.

Safety, precisely

The domain of a formula P, written dom(P), is the set of values mentioned in P plus every value appearing in any relation P names. An expression { t | P(t) } is safe when every value in its answer is a value of dom(P).

For the domain calculus the definition needs two more clauses: an existential subformula is true exactly when some value in the domain witnesses it, and a universal subformula exactly when every value in the domain satisfies it. Without those clauses a quantifier would have to test infinitely many values.

Mathematical derivation The three-clause form is not decoration. Clause one bounds the answer; clauses two and three bound the search, and they are what make an expression evaluable at all.

The growth test, and why it is the same condition

The operational form used throughout this page: evaluate the expression over dom(P), then over dom(P) with one value added that appears in no relation and in no constant of the query. A safe expression returns exactly the same set both times; an unsafe one grows, because the new value has become available to appear in the answer.

Mathematical derivation The two forms agree for the expressions in this lesson, and the reason is one line: if every value of the answer comes from dom(P), then enlarging the ambient domain adds no candidate that the predicate can accept, since the predicate can only compare against values it mentions. The converse fails in general — there are expressions that are domain-independent without being safe in the syntactic sense — which is why the lesson says “the test used here” rather than “the definition”.

Educational interpretation For exam purposes the two forms never disagree: every option in a GATE safety question is settled by the same reasoning either way, and the growth test is the faster of the two to apply.

Codd’s equality

Three languages express exactly the same queries: the basic relational algebra (selection, projection, Cartesian product, union, difference, renaming); the tuple calculus restricted to safe expressions; and the domain calculus restricted to safe expressions.

Verified fact This is Codd’s relational-completeness result, and it is stated in the same form by Silberschatz, Korth and Sudarshan and in the standard lecture notes on the topic. GATE CSE 2006 asked for it directly.

Where the power stops

The three languages cannot express transitive closure — “there is a path from u to w” with no bound on the length. A calculus expression contains a fixed number of quantifiers, and a chain can be longer than that number of hops.

Mathematical derivation This page makes the claim concrete instead of quoting it: on a five-node chain there are ten reachable ordered pairs, and the expression with two nested existentials finds seven of them. Add a node and the gap widens, which is a measurement of the failure rather than a restatement of it.

Worked examples, page 1 of 2 — the two previous-year items

E1. Calculus to algebra (GATE CSE 1999 Q1.19)

The item. The relational-algebra expression equivalent to { t | t ∈ r ∧ (t[A] = 10 ∧ t[B] = 20) } is (A) σA=10 ∨ B=20(r), (B) σA=10(r) ∪ σB=20(r), (C) σA=10(r) ∩ σB=20(r), (D) σA=10(r) − σB=20(r).

Step 1 — the answer schema. The only free variable is t, and the formula uses t[A] and t[B], so the answer is a set of (A, B) tuples: the same schema as r.

Step 2 — the atom that binds it. t ∈ r is the positive membership atom. Without it the expression would be unsafe, which is why it is written first.

Step 3 — read the conjunction. Both conditions must hold of the same tuple, so the answer is the intersection of the two selections: option C.

Computed. On r = {(10,20), (10,30), (20,20)} the calculus answer is {(10,20)}, the intersection is {(10,20)}, and the disjunction is all three rows — so A, B and D each return a different set. The page reports all four comparisons as checks.

E2. A shared variable is a join (GATE CSE 2026 Set 1 Q43)

The item. With R(P, Q) and S(X, Y), let E = { ⟨u⟩ | ∃v ∃w ⟨u,v⟩ ∈ R ∧ ⟨v,w⟩ ∈ S }. Which algebra expression is equivalent? The four options join P with X, X with Q, P with Y, and Y with Q respectively.

Step 1 — where each variable sits. u is R’s first attribute (P). v is R’s second (Q) and S’s first (X). w is S’s second (Y).

Step 2 — the shared variable. Only v appears twice, so the join condition is R.Q = S.X. That is option B.

Step 3 — the projection. The free variable is u, which is P, so the expression projects onto P. The join column is not projected.

Computed. On R = {(1,2),(2,3),(3,4)} and S = {(2,7),(3,8),(9,5)} the calculus answer is {1, 2}; option B gives {1, 2}, option A gives {2, 3}, and options C and D give the empty set. Exactly one option matches, and that is a check on the page.

Worked examples, page 2 of 2 — safety and vacuity

E3. Which expression is not safe (GATE CSE 2001 Q2.24)

The four options. A: {t | ∃u∈R1 (t[A]=u[A]) ∧ ¬∃s∈R2 (t[A]=s[A])}. B: {t | ∀u∈R1 (u[A]="x" ⇒ ∃s∈R2 (t[A]=s[A] ∧ s[A]=u[A]))}. C: {t | ¬(t ∈ R1)}. D: {t | ∃u∈R1 (t[A]=u[A]) ∧ ∃s∈R2 (t[A]=s[A])}.

The method. Do not classify by shape; bind the output. In A, B and D the output attribute t[A] is equated with a value of R1 or R2, so no value outside dom(P) can enter. In C nothing constrains t at all.

Computed. On R1 = {1, 2, x} and R2 = {2, 3, 4}, the four answers have sizes 2, 0, 2, 1. Add one fresh value: they become 2, 0, 3, 1. Only C moves.

The subtlety worth keeping. B is safe here only because no tuple of R1 has A = “x”, which makes its universal quantifier vacuously true and its answer empty. Run the same expression on R1 = {1, 2, 3} and it returns 5 values, then 6 with a fresh value — unsafe. The page computes both instances, and the published key for the paper is C.

E4. The vacuous universal, and its repair

The query. “Students who have taken every Biology course”: {t | ∀c∈COURSE (c[Dept]="Bio" ⇒ ∃e∈ENROL (e[S]=t[S] ∧ e[C]=c[C]))}.

What goes wrong. If no course is a Biology course, the implication is true for every course and therefore true for every value of t — including values that are not students. On the lesson’s instance with no Biology course the answer is 9 rows, which is the size of the whole domain, and it becomes 10 when one fresh value is added.

The repair. Add ∃e∈ENROL (e[S]=t[S]) as a conjunct outside the quantifier. The answer becomes the four enrolled students, and stays four when the fresh value is added.

The check that it is a repair and not a change of meaning. On an instance that does offer Biology courses, the naive and guarded forms return the same two students. The guard costs nothing except the unsafe case, which is exactly what the textbook’s treatment of this query says.

E5. The same query in both calculi

Tuple form. { t | ∃u∈EMP ( t[EmpName]=u[EmpName] ∧ ∃v∈DEPT ( u[DeptId]=v[DeptId] ) ) }.

Domain form. { ⟨n⟩ | ∃i,d,dn ( ⟨i,n,d⟩ ∈ EMP ∧ ⟨dn,d⟩ ∈ DEPT ) }.

On the lesson’s instance — three employees, two departments, the third employee in a department that does not exist — both return {Asha, Bilal}. The page compares the two sets, not their sizes, and reports the comparison as a check.

Mathematical derivation The translation is mechanical: replace each tuple variable by the list of its attributes, replace t[A] by the position of A, and keep the formula. The free variables of the tuple form become the components of the answer tuple, in order.

E6. Where a fixed quantifier depth runs out

The expression. { ⟨u,w⟩ | E(u,w) ∨ ∃v ( E(u,v) ∧ E(v,w) ) } — a path of one or two hops.

Computed on a chain. A three-node chain has three reachable ordered pairs, all at distance at most two, and the expression finds all three. A five-node chain has ten, and the expression finds seven: every pair at distance one or two, missing the three pairs that need three hops.

Why this is the standard counterexample. The number of quantifiers is fixed when the expression is written, and a chain can be made longer than that number of hops. Adding nodes widens the gap, so no fixed expression can be equivalent to reachability — which is why recursion needs a fixed-point operator or a recursive SQL query rather than another quantifier.

Edge cases and boundary conditions

Each of these is a place where a formula behaves differently from the English sentence beside it. The rule that covers all of them is the same one: ask what the answer’s values are bound to.

An empty relation under a universal quantifier

∀u ∈ r (P) is true when r is empty, whatever P says. A universal over nothing has no counterexample to find.

Mathematical derivation This is the definition of the universal quantifier as ¬∃u ∈ r (¬P): the inner existential over an empty relation is false, so the outer negation is true. It is not a quirk of databases, and it is the mechanism behind E4.

An empty relation under an existential quantifier

∃u ∈ r (P) is false when r is empty. The answer is then empty, not everything — the two quantifiers fail in opposite directions on empty input, and that asymmetry is what the 2001 options A and D exploit.

Educational interpretation A useful mnemonic: an existential is a filter that can only remove rows, so an empty relation removes everything; a universal is a filter that can only fail to remove, so an empty relation removes nothing.

The same value in two positions

Two existential quantifiers over the same relation may pick the same tuple unless the formula forbids it. “Two different boats” needs b1 ≠ b2 written explicitly; without it the query answers “at least one boat” and looks correct.

Trap This is the most common silent error in a hand-written calculus query, and it is invisible in the answer when every sailor happens to own two boats.

NULL in the instance

The calculus as defined here compares values, and a comparison with NULL is unknown rather than true or false. A tuple whose department id is NULL therefore satisfies neither t[DeptId] = v[DeptId] nor its negation, which is the fact the 2017 item’s note about NULL is pointing at.

Verified fact The 2017 paper says explicitly that DeptId may be NULL in EMP. Under three-valued logic that sentence changes which tuples satisfy the conditions, which is why the item is discussed in the Misconceptions tab rather than treated as routine.

Arity and order in the domain calculus

A domain expression whose angle brackets list the wrong number of components is a syntax error. The same expression with two components swapped is a semantic error that still runs and answers a different question.

Educational interpretation Tuple calculus is safer against this particular slip, because attribute names appear in the expression. That is a usability argument, not a power argument: the two languages are equal in expressive power.

Duplicates, and what an answer set is

A calculus answer is a set, so two tuples that agree on the free variables are one row. A query that joins three relations and projects one attribute returns the distinct values, not one row per matching triple.

Mathematical derivation The evaluator on this page enforces it: candidates are enumerated, but the answer is deduplicated by the free-variable values before it is counted, which is why the 2026 example returns two rows from two join pairs and not from the six pairs a careless reading would count.

Safety is not a property of an instance

An expression can return a finite answer on the instance in front of you and still be unsafe. Option B of the 2001 item is exactly that: safe-looking on the paper’s instance, unbounded on a variant that differs only in one stored value.

Mathematical derivation Safety quantifies over all instances of the schema. A single example can refute a claim of safety; it can never establish one.

Which domain the growth test uses

The definition of dom(P) is per formula, and that makes the naked complement empty by construction: its own domain is exactly the relation it complements. So the 2001 table evaluates all four options over the whole question’s domain, which is what a reader does, and the page computes the per-formula verdict as well and checks that the two agree on which option moves.

Mathematical derivation Both readings identify C: over the shared domain its answer is 2 rows then 3, and over its own domain 0 then 1. The growth, not the size, is the signal — which is the point of using a test rather than a table of numbers.

A constant is part of the domain

dom(P) includes the constants written in P. So {t | t[A] = 5} has 5 in its domain, but the answer still contains every tuple whose A is 5 over an infinite universe — the constant bounds the value, not the tuple.

Educational interpretation This is why the lesson’s growth test uses a value that appears in no relation and in no constant: adding a constant that the query already mentions cannot change anything, and would be a useless test.

Misconceptions, each with the smallest counterexample

Six beliefs that survive a first reading and fail on an instance. Every counterexample below was evaluated by the page’s model.

“A negation makes an expression unsafe.”

False. Options A and D of the 2001 item both contain a negation, and both are safe: what matters is whether the output is bound by a positive atom. {t | ∃u∈R1 (t[A]=u[A]) ∧ ¬∃s∈R2 (t[A]=s[A])} is safe and negated.

Mathematical derivation Computed: 2 rows on the domain, 2 rows with a fresh value added. No growth, so no dependence on the universe.

“A universal quantifier is always safe because it ranges over a relation.”

False. The quantifier ranges over a relation, but the answer tuple may not. In the 2001 option B the quantifier is vacuous on one instance and the answer is then every value of the domain.

Mathematical derivation Computed on the variant instance: 4 rows, then 5 with a fresh value. The quantifier was bounded and the expression was still unsafe.

“The domain calculus is more powerful, because its variables range over values.”

False. Restricted to safe expressions, the two calculi express exactly the same queries, and so does the basic algebra. The difference is notation, not power.

Verified fact This is Codd’s theorem, and it is the published key to GATE CSE 2006’s expressive-power item: all three options named in the question are equivalent.

“Two expressions with the same number of rows are equivalent.”

False. In the 2026 item options C and D both return the empty set while the answer is {1,2} — equal sizes, different sets. Equality of relations is equality of sets of tuples, attribute by attribute.

Mathematical derivation The page’s checks compare sets, not sizes, for exactly this reason; a size comparison would have passed two wrong options on a different instance.

“GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q41 says I and II are unsafe, so the key is wrong.”

Careful. The published key is that all three are safe. The community thread on the item argues both readings at length. This page’s growth test finds that I and II move when a fresh value is added (27→30 and 33→36 on the lesson’s instance) while III does not (6→6).

Community observation Both claims are reported, and neither is presented as the other. In an exam the published key governs; the strict test is what explains why the item is argued about at all.

“If a query runs and returns rows, it is a valid query.”

False. The unsafe complement runs, returns rows, and is excluded from the language. Validity is not executability: an expression is admitted only when its answer is guaranteed to come from the stored values.

Educational interpretation This is the one place in the syllabus where “it gives an answer” is explicitly not the test of correctness, and it is worth remembering as a category, not as a formula.

The four question patterns, and the procedure that settles each

Calculus appears in the papers in a small number of shapes. Recognising the shape decides the method; the arithmetic is then short.

Pattern 1 — is this expression safe?

Ask one question: does a positive atom bind every attribute of the answer? If yes, safe. If no, ask whether a negation or a vacuous universal lets a value that appears nowhere in the formula into the answer.

Seen as: GATE CSE 2001 Q2.24 (answer C), GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q41 (answer: all three).

Pattern 2 — which algebra expression is equivalent?

Decide the answer schema from the free variables, then turn each shared variable into an equality and each membership atom into a relation. The projection is decided by the free variables, not by the join column.

Seen as: GATE CSE 1999 Q1.19 (answer C), GATE CSE 2026 Set 1 Q43 (answer B).

Pattern 3 — which expressions are equivalent to each other?

Compare on an instance, not on shape. Build the smallest instance that separates the candidates — usually two rows with a shared value and one without — and evaluate. A candidate that differs on any instance is not equivalent.

Seen as: GATE CSE 2008 (a four-way equivalence item on a tuple-calculus expression).

Pattern 4 — expressive power

The answer is almost always “all of them are equal” when the calculi are qualified by safe, and “strictly more” when they are not. Read the qualifier before reading the options.

Seen as: GATE CSE 2006 (answer D, all three).

The procedure, in the order that saves time

1. Schema. List the free variables. Write down the arity and the order of the answer. This alone eliminates options in most equivalence questions.

2. Shape. Is it a join (an existential with an equality) or a “for every” (a universal with an existential inside)? A join is answered by a projection of a join; a “for every” by division, a double difference, or a count.

3. Binding. For every attribute of the answer, name the positive atom that binds it. If you cannot name one, the expression is unsafe — that is the whole safety test, and it takes seconds.

4. Growth. If the question turns on safety and the binding is ambiguous, add a value that appears in no relation and no constant, and ask whether the answer could change. If it could, the expression is unsafe.

Shortcuts, and the assumption each one needs

“Every free variable inside a membership atom means safe.” True here, and it is the shortcut this lesson uses. It assumes the quantifiers are bounded by relations, which is true of the tuple calculus but not of the domain calculus, where an unconstrained ∃z is legal and needs the extra safety clauses.

“A negation is safe if the atom it negates is bound elsewhere.” True, and it is why options A and D of the 2001 item are safe. It assumes the binding atom is positive; a negated atom binds nothing.

“Conjunction is intersection.” True for two selections on the same relation, which is what the 1999 item asks. It assumes both selections read the same relation instance; over different relations the analogue is a join, not an intersection.

MCQGATE CSE 2001, question 2.24

Which of the following relational calculus expressions is not safe?

  1. {t | ∃u∈R1 (t[A] = u[A]) ∧ ¬∃s∈R2 (t[A] = s[A])}
  2. {t | ∀u∈R1 (u[A] = "x" ⇒ ∃s∈R2 (t[A] = s[A] ∧ s[A] = u[A]))}
  3. {t | ¬(t ∈ R1)}
  4. {t | ∃u∈R1 (t[A] = u[A]) ∧ ∃s∈R2 (t[A] = s[A])}

Answer: C

Nothing binds t in option C, so the answer is every tuple over the ambient domain that is not in R1. When the domain is infinite the answer is infinite, and even for a finite domain it grows with the domain rather than with the data. This page measures the growth: 2 rows on dom(P), 3 rows with one fresh value added.

Why A fails. ∃u∈R1 (t[A] = u[A]) binds the output attribute to a stored value, so no fresh value can enter; measured 2 then 2. Why B fails. On this instance no tuple of R1 has A = “x”, so the implication is vacuously true and the answer is empty — measured 0 then 0. It is safe here, and the solution to question 3 shows what happens on another instance. Why D fails. Both membership atoms bind the output; measured 1 then 1.

Provenance note. The question text and the answer C are taken from public archives of the GATE CSE 2001 paper. The instance used to evaluate the options is this page’s own.

MCQGATE CSE 1999, question 1.19

The relational algebra expression equivalent to {t | t ∈ r ∧ (t[A] = 10 ∧ t[B] = 20)} is

  1. σ(A=10 ∨ B=20)(r)
  2. σ(A=10)(r) ∪ σ(B=20)(r)
  3. σ(A=10)(r) ∩ σ(B=20)(r)
  4. σ(A=10)(r) − σ(B=20)(r)

Answer: C

Both conditions must hold of the same tuple, and the intersection of the two selections keeps exactly the tuples that satisfy both. On the lesson’s instance r = {(10,20), (10,30), (20,20)} the calculus answer is {(10,20)} and the intersection is {(10,20)}.

Why A fails. The disjunction keeps all three tuples: conjunction is intersection, not union. Why B fails. The union also keeps all three, and it is the algebraic form of the disjunction. Why D fails. The difference keeps {(10,30)} — the tuples with A = 10 that do not have B = 20, which is the negation of the query rather than the query.

Provenance note. GATE CSE 1999 Q1.19, answer C in the published key. All four options were evaluated on the instance and the four comparisons are model checks on the page.

MSQGATE CSE 2006, question 24

Which of the following relational query languages have the same expressive power?

  1. Relational algebra
  2. Tuple relational calculus restricted to safe expressions
  3. Domain relational calculus restricted to safe expressions
  4. Tuple relational calculus without the safety restriction

Options I, II and III are the paper’s three; the fourth is added here as a distractor, so read this as a variant of the original rather than as the original.

Answer: A, B, C

The paper’s question is exactly options I, II and III, and its published key is D — all three. That is Codd’s theorem: the basic algebra, safe TRC and safe DRC denote the same class of queries.

Why D is false. Without the safety restriction the tuple calculus can express the complement of a relation, which the algebra cannot express as a finite relation. The restriction is what makes the three languages equal, so dropping it makes the calculus strictly stronger.

Provenance note. The paper’s item is GATE CSE 2006, marks 1, key D (all three of its options). The fourth option above is this lesson’s addition and is labelled as such.

MCQGATE CSE 2026 Set 1, question 43

With R(P, Q) and S(X, Y), let E = { ⟨u⟩ | ∃v ∃w ⟨u,v⟩ ∈ R ∧ ⟨v,w⟩ ∈ S }. Which one of the following relational algebra expressions is equivalent to E?

  1. ΠP(R ⋈R.P = S.X S)
  2. ΠP(S ⋈S.X = R.Q R)
  3. ΠP(R ⋈R.P = S.Y S)
  4. ΠP(S ⋈S.Y = R.Q R)

Answer: B

The variable v is the only one that appears in both membership atoms: it is R’s second attribute Q and S’s first attribute X. So the join condition is S.X = R.Q, and the free variable u is R’s first attribute P, so the projection is onto P. Option B is exactly that.

Why A fails. It joins P with X, which are different variables. Why C fails. It joins P with Y. Why D fails. It joins Y with Q. On the lesson’s instance the calculus answer is {1,2}, B gives {1,2}, A gives {2,3}, and C and D both give the empty set — each of the three wrong options returns a different set, which the page checks.

Provenance note. GATE CSE 2026 Set 1 Q43, published final key B. The instance is this page’s own.

NATOriginal practice

Let R1(A) = {1, 2, 3} and R2(A) = {2, 3, 4}, and take the expression E = {t | ∀u∈R1 (u[A] = "x" ⇒ ∃s∈R2 (t[A] = s[A] ∧ s[A] = u[A]))}. Evaluate E over the domain dom(P), then over dom(P) with one fresh value added. How many more tuples does the second evaluation return than the first?

Answer: 1

No tuple of R1 has A = “x”, so the implication is true for every u in R1 and the universal quantifier is vacuously true for every t. The answer is therefore the whole domain: 5 tuples over dom(P) = {1,2,3,4,x}, and 6 after one fresh value is added.

Why this is the point of the question. The expression looks safe — it quantifies over a relation and it mentions two relations — and it is unsafe on this instance precisely because the quantifier is vacuous. Add the constant “x” to R1 and the same expression returns the empty set and is safe. Safety of this shape depends on the data, which is exactly what makes it a trap.

The page’s model evaluates the paper’s instance (which contains “x”) and this variant, and both results are checks. This question is original practice, written for this lesson.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following are true of the relation between the relational calculi and the basic relational algebra?

  1. Every expression of the basic algebra has an equivalent safe tuple-calculus expression.
  2. Every safe tuple-calculus expression has an equivalent expression in the basic algebra.
  3. Transitive closure is expressible in the tuple calculus but not in the algebra.
  4. An unsafe tuple-calculus expression can be strictly more expressive than the algebra.

Answer: A, B, D

A and B are the two directions of Codd’s theorem, and they are the reason the three languages are called equal. D is why the word safe cannot be dropped: the complement of a relation is expressible in the unsafe calculus and is not a finite relation the algebra can produce.

Why C is false. Transitive closure is expressible in neither. A calculus expression has a fixed number of quantifiers and a chain can be longer; the page measures the failure — a two-hop expression finds 7 of the 10 reachable pairs on a five-node chain. Recursion needs a fixed-point operator, not another quantifier.

Original practice question written for this lesson; the 7-of-10 figure is a model check on the page.

NATOriginal practice

A tuple-calculus expression has an answer of arity 3, and its formula mentions one relation with 4 tuples over two attributes plus the constant 7. What is the largest number of candidate tuples the evaluator can be asked to test, before any tuple is rejected?

Answer: 125

The domain holds the values of the relation plus the constant. Four tuples over two attributes can supply at most eight values, and the constant adds one, so |dom(P)| = 5 at most (fewer if values repeat, which only lowers the count). Candidates for arity 3 are 5³ = 125.

Why 64 appears. That is 4³, using the number of tuples instead of the number of values. Why 512 appears. That is 8³, forgetting the constant — and it is an over-count in the other direction only if the eight values are all distinct.

The page reports the same bound for its own expressions: |D|^arity is printed beside the largest answer, and the check asserts that no answer exceeds it. Original practice question written for this lesson.

Revision sheet

Definitions, the four mappings, the two shapes, the procedure and the traps. Everything here is stated in the lesson above with its reasoning.

Definitions in one line each

Tuple calculus: {t | P(t)}, the tuples for which P holds.

Domain calculus: {⟨x1,…,xn⟩ | P}, variables ranging over values.

Free variable: one no quantifier captures; the answer is built from these.

dom(P): the constants in P plus every value in the relations P names.

Safe: every value in the answer is a value of dom(P).

The four mappings, algebra to calculus

Selection σθ(r) → a conjunct t ∈ r ∧ θ(t).

Projection ΠA(r) → the free variable is A, bound by ∃u∈r (t[A]=u[A]).

Product r × s → two membership atoms, two distinct tuple variables.

Join → two membership atoms with one shared variable, and the shared variable is the equality.

The two shapes, and what answers them

Join shape. An existential over the second relation with an equality between tuple variables. Answer with a projection of a join.

“For every” shape. A universal whose body contains an existential. Answer with division, a double difference, or a count comparison — and remember that the existential guarding the answer is not optional.

Numbers computed on this page

The 1999 answer is 1 tuple and the disjunction is 3. The 2026 answer is {1,2} and only one of four options matches. The 2001 answers have sizes 2, 0, 2, 1 and grow only for C. The vacuous query returns 9 of 9 domain values, or 4 students once guarded. Reachability: 3 of 3 pairs on a three-node chain, 7 of 10 on a five-node chain.

The four sentences to carry in

1. The answer’s schema comes from the free variables; the join comes from the shared one.

2. A universal quantifier is a search for a counterexample, and over an empty relation the search fails vacuously.

3. An expression is safe when a positive atom binds every attribute of the answer; the growth test is how you check it.

4. The safe calculi and the basic algebra are one language, and recursion is outside all three.
