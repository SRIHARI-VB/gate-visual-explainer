Topic and objective

TOPIC: Conditional expectation — the mean of a variable once part of the situation is known — and the law of total expectation, which recovers the ordinary mean by averaging those conditional means.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Compute E[X | A] as an ordinary mean over the outcomes inside A. Compute E[X | Y = y] for every value of y and read the result as a random variable. Prove and use E[E[X | Y]] = E[X] in both the variable form and the partition form. Turn a mean into an equation by conditioning on the first step, and solve it — the wait for two consecutive heads is 6. Split a variance with the total-variance law, condition on a continuous variable through its density, and average a binomial mean over a random parameter without carrying the fixed-parameter variance across.

I am reading this microtopic as one habit: before computing a mean, ask what is already known, and average inside the known part. Everything else in the lesson — the tower property, the recursion, the variance split, the mixture — is that habit applied four ways.

Prerequisites

  • Probability, random variables and distributions — sample spaces, the definition of expectation, and conditional probability as a ratio of probabilities.
  • Linearity of expectation — the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, with no independence needed. Used once, in the variance split.
  • Mean, median, mode and standard deviation — the mean as a weighted average, and variance as the mean squared deviation.
  • Counting and combinatorics — needed only for the block counts in scenes 2 and 3.
  • Two-variable (joint) tables, and the idea that a joint probability divided by a marginal gives a conditional one.

No calculus beyond one integral over the unit interval, and that integral is checked numerically on the page.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A conditional mean is an ordinary mean over a smaller set. The formula does not change; the probabilities do.
  • Conditioning on a variable gives one mean per value. The answer is a function of the condition, not a number.
  • Average the conditional means and the ordinary mean returns. E[E[X | Y]] = E[X].
  • Condition on the first step and the mean becomes an equation. That is how an unbounded wait is computed.
  • Mixing keeps the mean and changes the variance. Total variance = inside + between.

Intuition first: the average inside the part, then the average of the parts

A college reports the average salary of its graduates, and also the average for each branch separately. If you know the branch averages and how many graduates each branch had, you can recover the college average: weight each branch average by that branch's share of graduates, and add. Nobody needs the individual salaries.

That is the whole of the law of total expectation. The branch is the condition; the branch average is the conditional mean; the branch's share of graduates is the probability of the condition. Educational interpretation The college picture carries the weighting and nothing else — it says nothing about how randomness enters, and a branch average is a fixed number while a conditional mean is a random variable when the condition is random.

Formal vocabulary, every term in words

Event — a set of outcomes. "The sum is at least 10" is an event; it holds on 6 of the 36 throws.

Conditional probability P(x | A) — the probability of outcome x, rescaled by dividing by P(A) so that the outcomes inside A add up to one.

Conditional mean E[X | A] — the sum of x times P(x | A) over the outcomes inside A. In words: the ordinary mean, computed on A alone.

Conditional mean given a variable, E[X | Y = y] — the same thing, with A being the event that Y equals y. One such number for each value of y.

E[X | Y] — the random variable that takes the value E[X | Y = y] when Y equals y.

Partition — a list of events that never happen together and that together cover every outcome.

The definition, and the tower property

For an event A with positive probability:

E[X | A] = sum over x of x · P(X = x | A)The ordinary mean formula, with the conditional probability in place of the ordinary one. If P(A) = 0 the definition does not apply, and that is a real restriction, not a technicality.

The law of total expectation, in its two forms:

E[X] = E[ E[X | Y] ] E[X] = Σ E[X | A_i] · P(A_i)The first averages over the values of a variable; the second averages over the parts of a partition. They are the same identity written two ways.

Mathematical derivation Expand E[E[X | Y]] as the sum over y of E[X | Y = y] times P(Y = y). Expand the inner mean as the sum over x of x times P(X = x | Y = y). Multiply by P(Y = y) and use the definition of conditional probability in reverse — the product is the joint probability P(X = x, Y = y). Swap the two finite sums and sum over y first: what remains is the marginal P(X = x). The outer sum is then E[X] by definition. The only move that needs care is the swap, which is legal for a finite sum.

What conditioning does to a mean, in one picture

Take the 36 throws of two dice and the larger face as X. Its mean over everything is 161/36 = 4.4722. Now reveal the smaller face. If it is 1, the larger face averages 3.7273; if it is 6, it averages exactly 6.

Six numbers, not one. Weighted by how often each condition holds — 11/36 for the first, down to 1/36 for the last — they average back to 4.4722. Mathematical derivation Each of those six figures and the recovery of 161/36 are computed on the page from the 36 pairs, and the recovery is checked as an exact rational identity.

The recursion form, which is the same law

When the process has a small number of states, conditioning on the first step gives one equation per state:

E from state s = 1 + Σ (probability of the next state) · (mean from that state)The 1 is the step just taken. The sum is the tower property over the partition formed by "which state do we move to".

For the wait until two heads in a row: a = 1 + (1/2)a + (1/2)b and b = 1 + (1/2)a, giving a = 6 and b = 4. Mathematical derivation Substituting b into a gives (1/4)a = 3/2, so a = 6. The exact distribution F(n−1)/2^n summed from n = 2 upward gives 6.000000 to six decimal places, so the equation is not an approximation.

Why this is worth a whole lesson

Every question of the form "expected number of tosses until …", "expected marks if she attempts … first", "a machine is chosen at random and then …" is a conditional-expectation question in disguise. The distribution of the answer is usually infinite or enormous, so no table can be averaged; the two-stage route is the only feasible one.

The second half of the lesson is the part the exam tests hardest: knowing that the tower property preserves the mean and does not preserve the variance. An answer that carries a variance across a random parameter is wrong by exactly the between-blocks term, and that term is often the larger of the two.

Five worked items, each with its arithmetic shown

Items 2, 3 and 4 are previous-year questions with the paper named; each answer was recomputed here from the question text rather than copied from a key. Items 1 and 5 are worked examples written for this lesson. Every intermediate number is printed, so a wrong final answer can be traced to the step that produced it.

Example 1 — two coins, one chosen at random

One coin is fair and one lands heads with probability 0.2. A coin is picked with equal probability and tossed twice. How many heads on average?

The condition: which coin was picked. The two parts are disjoint and cover everything, so they form a partition.

Given the coin, the count is binomial with two trials: mean 2 × 0.5 = 1 for the fair coin, and 2 × 0.2 = 0.4 for the biased one. Weighting: 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.7 heads.

Note what was not done: the overall distribution of the count was never written down. It exists — the count is 0, 1 or 2 with probabilities 0.34, 0.5 and 0.16 — but the mean did not need it.

Example 2 — the wait for two consecutive heads

Paper: GATE ECE 2015 Set 2. A fair coin is tossed until two heads appear in a row. The expected number of tosses is ______.

Two states carry everything: no head in hand, with mean a; one head in hand, with mean b. Conditioning on the next toss:

a = 1 + (1/2)a + (1/2)b, because a tail returns to the same state and a head advances. b = 1 + (1/2)·0 + (1/2)a, because a head finishes and a tail falls back.

Answer: 6. Substituting gives (1/4)a = 3/2, so a = 6 and b = 4. The page checks the same 6 by summing the exact distribution F(n−1)/2^n over n from 2 to 200, which gives 6.000000. Scope note This is a GATE question from the ECE paper, not the CSE paper. It is included because the technique is identical; it is not presented as a CSE previous-year question.

Example 3 — which question to attempt first

Paper: GATE CSE 2021 Set 2, Q16 (numbering as filed by a question catalogue). A student answers A first or B first. If the first answer is wrong she scores nothing; if the first is right and the second wrong she keeps only the first; if both are right she keeps both. A is worth 10 marks with success probability 0.8; B is worth 20 with success probability 0.5. Which order maximises expected marks?

A first: she keeps 10 with probability 0.8, and keeps a further 20 with probability 0.8 × 0.5. Expected marks = 8 + 8 = 16.

B first: she keeps 20 with probability 0.5, and a further 10 with probability 0.5 × 0.8. Expected marks = 10 + 4 = 14.

Answer: A first, 16 marks. The structure is a partition on the first attempt's outcome, with the second attempt's mean inside each part. Both totals are computed on the page and checked.

Example 4 — the piece that holds 0.4

Paper: GATE CSE 2025, Q65 (numbering as filed by a question catalogue). A point is chosen uniformly in (0,1) and divides it into two subintervals. What is the expected length of the subinterval containing 0.4?

The condition: the cut u. If u is above 0.4 the piece is the left one, of length u; if u is at most 0.4 the piece is the right one, of length 1 − u.

The cut is above 0.4 with probability 0.6, and there the length averages (1 + 0.4)/2 = 0.7. The cut is at most 0.4 with probability 0.4, and there the length averages (1 + 0.4)/2 = 0.7 as well — the two averages coincide because the length function is symmetric about 0.5.

Answer: 0.74, from 0.4 × 0.8 + 0.6 × 0.7. The direct integral of the length over u gives 0.7400 as well, and the page checks the two against each other.

Example 5 — a coin whose bias is itself random

The bias p is drawn uniformly from (0,1), then the coin is tossed three times. This is a mixture: a binomial whose parameter is random.

The mean: given p the mean is 3p, so the overall mean is 3 × E[p] = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5. The mean passes straight through the mixing.

The variance: given p the variance is 3p(1 − p), whose mean over a uniform p is 0.5. The conditional means 3p have variance 9 × (1/12) = 0.75. Total: 0.5 + 0.75 = 1.25.

Compare a fixed fair coin: mean 1.5, variance 0.75. The mixing left the mean alone and added 0.75 of variance. Both figures are checked on the page by integrating over p on a 20000-point grid.

What the five items have in common

Each one names a condition — the coin, the state, the first attempt, the cut, the bias — computes an ordinary mean inside that condition, and then weights by how likely the condition is. None of them writes the full distribution of the answer, and two of them have distributions with infinitely many values. The only real decision in each is which condition to use, and the safest choice is the one after which the rest of the problem becomes a fixed, ordinary calculation.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Seven boundaries, each of which turns a correct-looking calculation into a wrong answer. The rule stated in each card is the one the exam tests, and the example is the smallest one that shows the difference.

1. The parts must be disjoint and exhaustive

The law over a partition needs both properties. Overlapping parts count some outcomes twice and inflate the answer; missing parts leave outcomes out and deflate it.

Arithmetic check: the weights must add to exactly one. If they add to 1.2 or 0.9, the partition is wrong and no amount of care in the conditional means will fix it.

2. Conditioning on an independent variable changes nothing

If X is independent of Y then E[X | Y = y] = E[X] for every y, so the conditional mean is a constant and its variance is zero. On the page, conditioning the first die's face on the second die's face gives 3.5 six times, with a largest deviation of 0 to machine precision.

So a constant answer is right exactly when the variables are independent, and wrong otherwise. It is never right "because conditioning does not affect the mean" in general.

3. P(Y = y) can be zero

For a continuous Y the probability of any single value is zero, so E[X | Y = y] cannot be defined as a ratio of probabilities. It is defined as a limit over shrinking intervals, which equals an integral against the conditional density.

Exam consequence: a question that says "uniformly distributed" and asks for a conditional mean is an area or integral question, not a counting question. Dividing by a probability there produces zero over zero.

4. The mean survives mixing, the variance does not

E[E[X | P]] = E[X] always, so a random parameter never changes the mean. Var(X) = E[Var(X | P)] + Var(E[X | P]), and the second term is positive unless the conditional mean is constant.

For the random-bias coin: mean 1.5 either way, variance 1.25 against 0.75 for a fixed fair coin. Answering 0.75 to a random-parameter question is the classic error.

5. A conditional mean need not be an attainable value

The conditional mean of the larger face given the smaller face is 3.7273 when the smaller face is 1, and 4.2222 when it is 2. Neither is a possible value of the larger face, which is an integer.

So never eliminate an option for being fractional or unattainable. Eliminate it for being wrong.

6. A recursion needs the process to restart identically

Writing "a tail from the start returns to the start" assumes the future from that point is a fresh copy of the original problem. That holds for independent tosses of one coin, and it fails if the bias drifts, if the coin is drawn without replacement, or if there is a time limit.

Check before writing the equation: after the reset, is the situation exactly the original one? If not, the recursion needs more states.

7. The mean must be finite for the tower property

E[E[X | Y]] = E[X] compares two numbers. If E[X] is infinite, both sides are infinite and the identity carries no information; if the conditional means are infinite in a way that does not cancel, the algebra is meaningless.

GATE questions avoid this by giving a finite sample space or a distribution with a finite mean. The boundary matters when a question involves an unbounded number of trials, where you should confirm the mean is finite before using the law.

The one boundary to carry into the exam

The tower property is about the mean and about nothing else. Before using it, name the quantity: if it is a mean, average the conditional means freely; if it is a variance, add the between-blocks term; if the condition is continuous, integrate against the density; if the parts are not a partition, stop and fix the parts first.

Six beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each belief below is stated as a student would state it, then broken by the smallest counterexample that fits on one line. The last card is the single distinction that separates the correct uses from the incorrect ones.

1. "Conditioning does not change the mean"

Why it feels right: it is true for independent variables, and independence is the case students meet first.

Counterexample: two dice, sum at least 10. The unconditional mean of the sum is 7; the conditional mean is 10.67. Knowing the sum is large raises every outcome inside the condition.

Repair: conditioning changes the mean whenever the variable and the condition are dependent. Independence is the special case, not the rule.

2. "E[E[X | Y]] equals E[X | Y]"

Why it feels right: the two expressions look like the same thing written twice, and the inner brackets look removable.

Counterexample: with the dice, E[X | Y] takes six values from 3.73 to 6; its own mean is the single number 4.4722, which is E[X]. A six-valued object is not a single number.

Repair: the inner object is a random variable and the outer E averages it. The law says that averaging removes the conditioning, leaving E[X].

3. "The weights are the conditional probabilities"

Why it feels right: conditional probabilities are the weights used inside a part, so they seem to be the weights of the parts too.

Counterexample: in the two-coin problem the parts have weights 0.5 and 0.5. Using the conditional probabilities of heads inside each part — 0.5 and 0.2 — would give 0.5 × 1 + 0.2 × 0.4 = 0.58, not 0.7.

Repair: the outer weights are the probabilities of the parts, and they must add to one. The conditional probabilities are used one level in.

4. "Var(X) is the mean of the conditional variances"

Why it feels right: the tower property works for means, so it seems to work for any average of a conditional quantity.

Counterexample: the dice give E[Var(X | Y)] = 1.5176 but Var(X) = 1.9715. The missing 0.4539 is the variance of the conditional means.

Repair: use the full law, Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y]). The second term is zero only when the conditional mean is constant, which is the independence case.

5. "A random parameter can be replaced by its mean"

Why it feels right: the mean of the mixture does equal the mixture at the mean parameter — 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 either way.

Counterexample: the variance is 1.25 for the random bias against 0.75 at p = 0.5. Substituting the mean parameter loses 0.75 of variance.

Repair: the substitution is valid for linear quantities and invalid for anything nonlinear. A variance is quadratic in p, so it feels the randomness of p.

6. "Dividing by P(Y = y) is always the formula"

Why it feels right: it is exactly the discrete formula, and it appears in every worked example with a table.

Counterexample: for a uniform cut in (0,1), P(u = 0.4) = 0. The formula gives zero over zero; the correct answer 0.74 comes from an integral.

Repair: check whether the condition is a value of a discrete variable or of a continuous one. Discrete: divide by the probability. Continuous: integrate against the density.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

The conditional mean is a random variable; the law of total expectation is a statement about its average.

If a question asks for E[X | Y], the answer is an expression in Y, unless X and Y are independent and it collapses to a constant. If a question asks for E[X] and gives you conditional information, the answer is the weighted average of the conditional means, with weights that add to one. Every other rule in this lesson — the variance split, the recursion, the mixture — is one of those two sentences applied carefully.

How this clause gets tested, and what each pattern demands

Four patterns, in the order they appear across papers. Each card says what the question looks like, what it is really asking for, and the single step that decides the answer.

Pattern 1 — a two-stage experiment

Shape: "A machine is chosen at random, then …", "A coin is selected and tossed …", "She attempts one question first, then …".

Really asking: the partition form of the law. The first stage is the condition; the second stage is an ordinary mean inside it.

The deciding step: weight by the first stage's probabilities, not by anything from the second stage. Check the weights add to one.

Pattern 2 — a wait, answered as a NAT

Shape: "the expected number of tosses until …", "the expected number of draws before …", with a numerical field.

Really asking: a recursion built by conditioning on the next step, solved as a small linear system.

The deciding step: name the states, write one equation per state, and count the step just taken in every equation. The usual error is to omit that 1, which lowers every answer by one.

Pattern 3 — a statement set on the tower property

Shape: an MSQ offering "E[E[X | Y]] = E[X]", "E[X | Y] is a random variable", "Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)]", and asking which are true.

Really asking: whether you can separate the mean statement from the variance statement, and the random variable from its average.

The deciding step: the first two are true; the third is false as written, because the between term is missing. Any statement that conditions a variance needs both terms.

Pattern 4 — a mixture, or a conditional variance

Shape: "the parameter is uniformly distributed", "the number of defects is Poisson with a random rate", asking for a variance or a probability.

Really asking: whether you know that the mean passes through the mixing and the variance does not.

The deciding step: write the conditional mean and the conditional variance as functions of the parameter, then average both. The answer is the average of the variances plus the variance of the means.

The four-step procedure, and where each step can go wrong

Step 1 — name the condition. Wrong: conditioning on too little, so the conditional mean is still hard. The right condition makes the rest ordinary.

Step 2 — compute one mean inside each part. Wrong: forgetting that the pair y-y is one outcome while y-k and k-y are two, which breaks the counts.

Step 3 — weight by the probability of each part. Wrong: using conditional probabilities from inside the parts as the outer weights.

Step 4 — read the answer back. Wrong: reporting a mean as if it were a variance, or a six-valued function as if it were a number.

Where marks are actually lost

Three losses dominate. First, omitting the step just taken in a recursion, which produces an answer one below the truth and is the single commonest error on wait questions. Second, answering a random-parameter variance with the fixed-parameter formula, losing exactly the between-blocks term. Third, answering "what is E[X | Y]" with a number when the question wanted an expression in Y — or the reverse, giving an expression when the question said the variables are independent and the answer is a constant.

MCQGATE DA 2025, Q1

Suppose X and Y are random variables, and the conditional expectation of X given Y is written E[X | Y]. Then E[E[X | Y]] equals

  1. E[X | Y]
  2. E[X] / E[Y]
  3. E[X]
  4. E[Y]

Answer: C — E[X]

This is the law of total expectation. Write the outer mean as the sum over y of E[X | Y = y] times P(Y = y), expand the inner mean, multiply by P(Y = y) to recover the joint probability, and sum over y first. What remains is the marginal probability of X = x, so the total is E[X].

Why A appears. It drops the outer average and leaves the random variable standing; the inner object takes six different values on the dice example, so it cannot equal a single number. Why B appears. It treats the double expectation as a ratio, which no rule permits. Why D appears. It reads the outer expectation as being taken over X rather than over Y.

Paper note: this is a Data Science and Artificial Intelligence paper question, not a CSE paper question. It is included because the syllabus clause is identical in the two papers.

NATGATE ECE 2015 Set 2

A fair coin is tossed until two heads appear consecutively for the first time. The expected number of tosses is ______.

Answer: 6

Condition on the first toss. With no head in hand, a tail leaves the state unchanged and a head advances: a = 1 + a/2 + b/2. With one head in hand, a head finishes and a tail falls back: b = 1 + a/2. Substituting gives a/4 = 3/2, so a = 6.

Why 4 appears. It is b, the mean from the state where one head is already in hand — the answer to a different question. Why 8 appears. It doubles the correct answer, which happens when a tail from the one-head state is treated as advancing rather than resetting.

The exact distribution F(n−1)/2^n, summed from n = 2 upward, gives 6.000000, which is checked on the page.

MCQGATE CSE 2021 Set 2, Q16

A student may choose the order in which to attempt two questions. A wrong first answer scores zero; a right first answer with a wrong second keeps only the first; both right keeps both. Question A is worth 10 marks with success probability 0.8; question B is worth 20 with success probability 0.5. Which order maximises the expected marks, and what are they?

  1. A first, then B: 14 marks
  2. B first, then A: 14 marks
  3. B first, then A: 22 marks
  4. A first, then B: 16 marks

Answer: D — A first, then B, for 16 marks

A first: 10 with probability 0.8, plus 20 with probability 0.8 × 0.5, giving 8 + 8 = 16. B first: 20 with probability 0.5, plus 10 with probability 0.5 × 0.8, giving 10 + 4 = 14.

Why the 14 options appear. 14 is the expected marks of the wrong order, and it is a genuine value — which is why the question offers it twice, once with each ordering. Why 22 appears. It adds both full marks without weighting by the second success probability.

NATOriginal practice

Two fair dice are thrown. Given that the sum of the faces is at least 10, what is the expected value of the sum? Give your answer to two decimal places.

Answer: 10.67

Six throws satisfy the condition: (4,6), (5,5), (5,6), (6,4), (6,5) and (6,6), with sums 10, 10, 11, 10, 11 and 12. Their total is 64, so the conditional mean is 64/6 = 10.6667.

Why 7 appears. That is the unconditional mean; the condition has not been applied. Why 11 appears. That is the median of the surviving sums, not their mean. Why 9 appears. It averages 10, 11 and 12 with equal weights, forgetting that three of the six surviving throws have sum 10.

NATOriginal practice

A fair die is rolled once. A second fair die is then rolled repeatedly until it shows the first die's value. What is the expected total number of rolls of the second die?

Answer: 6

Given the first die's value, each roll of the second die matches it with probability 1/6, and the rolls are independent. So the conditional mean is 6 for every possible first value. Every conditional mean equals 6, so the overall mean is 6.

This is the case where conditioning changes nothing: the conditional mean is constant, so its variance is zero and the tower property returns the same 6.

Why 7 appears. It counts the first die's roll as well; the question asks only about the second die. Why 3.5 appears. It is the mean of one roll, not the mean of a count of rolls.

MSQOriginal practice

Let X and Y be random variables with finite means and variances. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. E[E[X | Y]] = E[X]
  2. E[X | Y] is a random variable
  3. Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)]
  4. If X and Y are independent then E[X | Y] = E[X]

Answer: A, B and D

A is the law of total expectation. B is true by definition: E[X | Y] takes the value E[X | Y = y] when Y equals y, so it is a function of Y. D is true because independence makes every conditional mean equal to the unconditional one.

Why C is false. The full law is Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y]). Statement C drops the between term, which is 0.4539 on the dice example and 0.75 in the random-bias mixture — never negligible in general.

This is the single most examinable distinction in the topic: the mean statement holds with no hypothesis, the variance statement needs its second term.

Revision sheet

Everything on this tab fits on one screen and is meant to be read the morning of the exam. Each line is a definition, an identity with its hypothesis, or a trap.

Definitions

E[X | A] — the sum of x times P(X = x | A). An ordinary mean over the outcomes inside A. Needs P(A) above zero.

E[X | Y = y] — the same with A being the event Y = y. One number per value of y.

E[X | Y] — the random variable equal to E[X | Y = y] on the outcomes where Y = y.

Partition — disjoint parts that together cover the sample space. Their probabilities add to one.

Identities, with their hypotheses

E[E[X | Y]] = E[X] — always, for finite means.

E[X] = Σ E[X | A_i] P(A_i) — over a partition.

Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y]) — both terms, always.

E[g(X) | A] = Σ g(x) P(x | A) — the conditional version of the mean of a function.

Conditional mean is linear — E[X + Z | Y] = E[X | Y] + E[Z | Y], with no independence needed, for the same reason linearity holds unconditionally.

Numbers to recognise

Two dice, sum at least 10: 10.6667, from 64/6.

Larger face given the smaller face, averaged: 4.4722 = 161/36, the same as unconditionally.

Wait for two consecutive heads: 6, and 4 from the one-head state.

Piece of the unit interval holding 0.4: 0.74.

Binomial of 3 with uniform bias: mean 1.5, variance 1.25 against 0.75 for a fixed fair coin.

Five traps, in the order they cost marks

Omitting the step already taken in a recursion.

Using the fixed-parameter variance after mixing.

Dropping the between-blocks term from a variance.

Weighting by conditional probabilities instead of the probabilities of the parts.

Dividing by P(Y = y) when Y is continuous.

Four questions to ask before writing anything

What is already known, and does the known part form a partition? Inside a part, is the mean now an ordinary calculation? Do the weights add to one? And is the quantity asked for a mean, in which case the tower property applies, or a variance, in which case the between term must be carried? If those four answers are settled, the arithmetic is short.
