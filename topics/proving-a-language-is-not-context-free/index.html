Topic and objective

TOPIC: Proving that a language is not context-free — the pumping lemma for context-free languages read as a game with three moves, the five-part split, the two conditions that make it bite, Ogden’s lemma as the stronger tool, and the closure route that answers most exam questions faster.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a language, decide whether it is context-free by counting its independent equalities; state the pumping lemma with its quantifiers in the right order; refute any particular pumping length by an exhaustive search over a stated family of strings; prove a language is not context-free by an argument that leaves p symbolic; and use Ogden’s lemma or a closure argument when the plain lemma is not enough.

I read the request as the refutation machinery, not another tour of context-free grammars: CFGs and push-down automata introduces the four-tuple and one scene on the lemma, and closure and decision properties owns the regular pumping lemma and the closure tables. This page owns what neither does: the argument as a construction, Ogden’s lemma, and the fact that the lemma is necessary and not sufficient.

1 — Intuition: a game with three moves

A push-down automaton has one stack, so it can hold one unbounded count while it compares. The pumping lemma turns that physical fact into a test. Picture a game: the language names a number p; you name a long string w from the language; the lemma names a split of w into five pieces; you name a repetition count i. You win if the pumped string is outside the language. To prove the language is not context-free you must win for every p, which is why a proof never gives p a value. EDUCATIONAL INTERPRETATION

The analogy is standard in textbooks; it is offered to make the quantifier order memorable, not as a proof of anything.

2 — Formal definition, every symbol in words

The pumping lemma for context-free languages. If L is a context-free language then there exists a number p > 0 such that for every string w in L with |w| ≥ p, there exist strings u, v, x, y, z with:

w = u v x y z    |v x y| ≤ p    v y ≠ ε    u vi x yi z ∈ L for every i ≥ 0

In words: p is a number attached to the language; |w| is the number of letters in the string w; the string splits into five consecutive pieces; the middle three pieces together are at most p letters long; the pieces v and y are not both empty, so at least one letter actually moves; and repeating v and y the same number of times, including zero times, keeps the string in the language. VERIFIED FACT MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

3 — Why the five pieces exist

A string of length at least p has a parse tree with a path carrying more nonterminal nodes than the grammar has nonterminals, so one nonterminal A repeats on that path. The upper copy of A derives vxy and the lower copy derives x. Because both copies are the same symbol, either subtree may replace the other, which repeats v and y together.

The length bound |vxy| ≤ p is the span of the upper copy, and in a grammar in Chomsky normal form that span is at most 2k for k nonterminals. That is where p = 2k comes from.

4 — Why the short window is the whole lemma

Drop |vxy| ≤ p and the lemma becomes false: any long string could be split so that v and y were whole blocks in the right proportion, and every pump would stay in the language. The short window forbids that, because a window of p letters cannot span three blocks that each hold p letters.

So the condition is not decoration: it is the mechanism. Every contradiction in this lesson is produced by it.

5 — Worked example, first pass

Claim: anbncn is not context-free. Take w = apbpcp, which has length 3p. The window vxy has at most p letters. A window that holds a letter of the a-block and a letter of the c-block must hold all p b’s between them, so it is at least p + 2 letters long. Contradiction. So the window meets one block or two.

If it meets one block, pumping changes that block’s count only. If it meets two, two counts change by unequal amounts. In both cases the three counts stop being equal, so the pumped string leaves the language, for every split. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

6 — What a number cannot do

A proof must defeat every p. A computation can only defeat finitely many. So the page does both and keeps them apart: the search refutes p = 1 to 6 with named witnesses, and the argument above refutes all of them at once by leaving p as a symbol.

This separation is the single most useful habit for the exam, because the commonest wrong answer is a refutation of one p presented as a proof about the language.

Worked example 1 — anbncn, every case written out

Witness. w = apbpcp. Its length is 3p, so the lemma applies for every p ≥ 1.

Case 1, the window inside one block. v and y lie inside the a-block. Pumping with i = 2 raises the number of a’s by |v| + |y| ≥ 1 and leaves p b’s and p c’s. The counts differ, so the string is outside L. Symmetric inside the b-block and the c-block.

Case 2, the window across two blocks. vxy covers the end of one block and the start of the next. Pumping with i = 2 changes both counts, but by different amounts whenever |v| ≠ |y|; when |v| = |y| the third block’s count is now wrong instead. Either way the string leaves L.

Why there is no third case. A window that touched all three blocks would contain all p b’s, so it would be at least p + 2 letters, which the condition |vxy| ≤ p forbids. Two cases, every split, every p: the language is not context-free. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Worked example 2 — w w is not context-free

Take w0 = apb apb, which is in L because it is the string “apb” written twice. Its length is 2p + 2.

Because |vxy| ≤ p, the window cannot cover both copies of the block apb. So at most one of v and y reaches the first copy while the other reaches the second, and pumping breaks the equality of the two halves.

Computed: on the 518 test strings the search refutes p = 1 to 6, with witnesses aa, abab, aabbaabb, aaababaaabab, aabaabaabaab and aaabaaabaaabaaab. Those witnesses are not the textbook one, which is the point: many strings refute, and the search reports the first it meets.

Worked example 3 — what the search reports

Worked example 4 — the split count

The number of splits a string of length n offers at pumping length p, after the two conditions, is the sum over s = 1 to p of (n − s + 1)((s + 1)(s + 2)/2 − 1), because s is the window length and the bracket counts the placements of v, x and y inside it.

Computed and checked against brute force: n = 9, p = 3 gives 121 splits; n = 6, p = 2 gives 37; n = 12, p = 4 gives 295; n = 18, p = 6 gives 1106. That is why a search wants a short witness: aabbcc at p = 6 needs only 182 splits, while an eighteen-letter string needs 1106.

Worked example 5 — Ogden shortens the proof

Mark every a in apbpcp. There are p marked positions, so the lemma applies. The window carries at most p marked letters, and v and y together carry at least one, so the pump touches an a. A window that contains an a and also a c must hold all p b’s, so it exceeds p. Therefore the window lies inside the a-block, and pumping changes the number of a’s alone.

Computed: with the a’s marked, the search must try only 2, 13, 44, 110, 230 and 427 splits at p = 1 to 6, against 1106 unfiltered splits of an eighteen-letter string at p = 6. Same conclusion, one case.

Worked example 6 — a language that passes

Let L = { aibjckdl : i = 0 or j = k = l }. On a string with no a, pumping inside the b’s, c’s or d’s keeps i = 0, so the string stays in L. On a string with one a, pumping inside the a’s keeps j = k = l.

Computed: 228 test strings, no refutation at any p from 1 to 6. And yet L is not context-free, because L ∩ a+b*c*d* = { aibjcjdj : i ≥ 1 }, which the search refutes at every p on its 18 test strings, and context-free languages are closed under intersection with a regular language.

Edge case 1 — a context-free language fails at p = 1

anbn is context-free, and the search refutes p = 1 with the string ab: the only window is one letter, so pumping a gives aab and pumping b gives abb, neither in the language.

Computed: refuted at p = 1, survives at p = 2 to 6 on 12 test strings. The lemma asks for some p, so this is not a contradiction, and a student who reads it as one has inverted the lemma.

Edge case 2 — the smallest valid p is not small

anbmcn+m with n, m ≥ 1 is context-free, and the search still refutes p = 1, 2, 3 and 4 on its 25 test strings, because the family’s strings are tight and pumping down deletes the only b or the only a.

Computed: no refutation at p = 5 or 6. A valid pumping length for this language is at least 5; the theorem promises that one exists, never that it is 1 or 2.

Edge case 3 — the empty string

If ε is in L, the lemma still holds: take i = 0 and the pieces vanish. Nothing in the statement forbids the empty string in the language, and nothing requires it either.

What the second condition forbids is v and y being empty together, because then pumping would do nothing and the lemma would be vacuous.

Edge case 4 — a one-letter alphabet

Over a unary alphabet every context-free language is regular. So { ap : p prime } is not context-free, and it need not be shown by the lemma at all.

Computed: the search refutes it at p = 1 to 6 on 17 test strings, with witnesses aa, aa, aaa, aaaaa, aaaaa and aaaaaaa. The witness is a prime-length string, and pumping a unary string changes its length by an amount that need not stay prime.

Edge case 5 — pumping down

The lemma quantifies over every i ≥ 0, not just i ≥ 1. Deleting v and y is often the quickest contradiction, and it is the reason a tight family such as anbmcn+m with n, m ≥ 1 is refuted at small p: deleting the only b gives ac, which is outside the language.

A proof that only considers i = 2 has used half the lemma.

Edge case 6 — what a survival means

A survival is always relative to a family and a bound: the family here is finite and stated, and pumping was tested for i = 0 to 6. On the four-block language, p = 6 survives within 228 strings; on w anwRbn, p = 5 needs a sixteen-letter witness and p = 6 needs a longer one still.

So a survival is evidence about the family, never a statement about the language. Only the symbolic argument is a statement about the language.

“If it satisfies the pumping lemma, it is context-free”

Why it tempts: the lemma is presented as a characterisation in most first courses, and every worked example runs one way.

Minimal counterexample: L = { aibjckdl : i = 0 or j = k = l } survives every p from 1 to 6 on 228 strings and is not context-free.

Correct rule: the lemma is necessary and not sufficient. To show a language is context-free, build a grammar or a PDA.

“The lemma failed at p = 1, so the language is not context-free”

Why it tempts: a refutation feels like a result, and p = 1 is the first value anyone tries.

Minimal counterexample: anbn is context-free and is refuted at p = 1 by the string ab.

Correct rule: p is existentially quantified. You must refute every p, and the only way to do that finitely is to leave p as a symbol.

“One pumped piece moves, as in the regular lemma”

Why it tempts: the regular pumping lemma has one pumped piece y, and the notation looks similar.

Minimal counterexample: pump only v in apbpcp and you can conclude nothing, because the lemma allows v and y to be repeated together.

Correct rule: the context-free lemma pumps two pieces, by the same i, and it bounds |vxy| rather than |xy|. Mixing the two lemmas is the commonest notation error in this chapter.

“The window condition can be ignored in the proof”

Why it tempts: most contradictions use only the counts, so the window looks like decoration.

Minimal counterexample: without |vxy| ≤ p the lemma is false, because you could split apbpcp as v = ap, x = bp, y = cp and every pump would stay in the language.

Correct rule: the window is what limits the split to a local disturbance. Every case analysis starts by asking what the window can reach.

“Two equal counts mean not context-free”

Why it tempts: anbn has two counts and is the first non-regular example anyone meets.

Minimal counterexample: anbn is context-free; anbmcn+m is context-free because one counter holds the sum.

Correct rule: count the independent equalities that must hold at once. One stack holds one. Two simultaneous equalities across three blocks is the usual failure.

“Closure is only for regular languages”

Why it tempts: the closure table for regular languages is longer and better known.

Minimal counterexample: the four-block language is convicted by intersecting it with a+b*c*d*, a regular set, and the result is not context-free.

Correct rule: context-free languages are closed under union, concatenation, star and intersection with a regular language, and not under intersection or complement. Those four positive cases are the whole closure toolkit for this exam.

Pattern 1 — which of these languages is not context-free

Four languages, one answer. Reasoning: count independent equalities. One stack tracks one count. A sum such as n + m is one count; two equalities such as i = j and j = k are two. Nested constraints, where the pairs do not cross, are context-free; crossing constraints are not.

Computed on this page: GATE CSE 2019 offered w wR, w anbnwR, w anwRbn and anbi with i in {n, 3n, 5n}. The third is refuted at every p from 1 to 6 on nine test strings; the other three are not refuted from p = 2, and the fourth survives at p = 6. The published key is the third.

Pattern 2 — is this pumping length valid

A grammar is given, often with k nonterminals or with a stated number of variables, and a value of p is proposed. Reasoning: for a grammar in Chomsky normal form p = 2k, so k = 3 gives 8 and k = 10 gives 1024.

The trap is treating p as a free choice: a smaller p is not “stronger”, it is simply invalid, as anbn at p = 1 shows.

Pattern 3 — what does this split force

A split is described, for example “v lies inside the a-block and y inside the b-block”, and the question asks what follows. Reasoning: check the two conditions first, then pump once and compare counts.

If |vxy| > p the split is not allowed at all, and no conclusion follows from it. That is the distractor in most of these questions.

Pattern 4 — which statement is false

Four statements about classes, closure or decidability. Reasoning: test each against one fact. anbnc2n is not context-free but is recursive, so a statement pairing those two is true.

GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 is the model: four statements, answer III and IV only, where I fails because a unary context-free language is regular.

The procedure, in four lines

1. Count the independent equalities: one means context-free, two at once means not. 2. If a proof is demanded, name p as a symbol and build w out of p. 3. Ask what the window can reach, and list those cases only. 4. Before answering, check the direction: a survival is not a certificate and a small refuted p is not a proof.

MCQ GATE CSE 2019

Which one of the following languages over Σ = {a, b} is NOT context-free?

  1. { w wR | w ∈ {a, b}* }
  2. { w anbnwR | w ∈ {a, b}*, n ≥ 0 }
  3. { w anwRbn | w ∈ {a, b}*, n ≥ 0 }
  4. { anbi | i ∈ {n, 3n, 5n}, n ≥ 0 }

Answer: (3). Computed here: option 3 is refuted at every p from 1 to 6 on nine test strings of the shape akb ak+1b akbk+1. Options 1 and 2 are not refuted from p = 2, and option 4 survives at p = 6.

The reason is the stack. In option 3 the copy of w has already used the stack to match wR, so when the bn arrives there is nothing left to count it against. Option 2 puts the anbn before the mirror, so the stack is free for it. Option 4 is a union of three context-free languages, hence context-free.

MCQ GATE CSE 2016 Set 2

Consider L1 = { anbmcn+m : m, n ≥ 1 } and L2 = { anbnc2n : n ≥ 1 }. Which one of the following is TRUE?

  1. Both L1 and L2 are context-free.
  2. L1 is context-free while L2 is not.
  3. L2 is context-free while L1 is not.
  4. Neither L1 nor L2 is context-free.

Answer: (2). The published key is B, and the computation agrees: L1 is not refuted at p = 5 or 6 on 25 test strings, while L2 is refuted at every p from 1 to 6 on six.

L1 needs one count, the sum n + m, which a single stack holds: push every a and every b, then pop one symbol per c. L2 needs two counts at once, n = n and 2n = |c|, which one stack cannot hold. A wrong answer here usually comes from thinking that a larger exponent makes a language harder.

MSQ GATE CSE 2017 Set 2

Consider L1 = { ap | p is prime }, L2 = { anbmc2m | n, m ≥ 0 }, L3 = { anbnc2n | n ≥ 0 }, L4 = { anbn | n ≥ 1 }. Which of the following are CORRECT?

I. L1 is context-free but not regular.
II. L2 is not context-free.
III. L3 is not context-free but recursive.
IV. L4 is deterministic context-free.

  1. I, II and IV only
  2. II and III only
  3. I and IV only
  4. III and IV only

Answer: (4), III and IV only. The published key is D.

I is false: over a one-letter alphabet every context-free language is regular, so a prime-length language cannot be context-free. II is false: L2 is context-free, because m is counted once and n is free. III is true: L3 is refuted at every p by this page’s search, and a Turing machine decides it, so it is recursive. IV is true: anbn has a deterministic push-down automaton.

NAT original practice

A grammar in Chomsky normal form has 4 nonterminals. Using the pumping length p = 2k, what is the smallest p the lemma guarantees for its language?

Answer: 16. p = 24 = 16. The table computed here runs 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 for k = 1 to 8.

A common wrong answer is 4, from reading k as the number of terminals, or 8, from an off-by-one in the height. The height is k + 1 because the root counts, and a full binary tree of height k + 1 has 2k leaves.

MCQ original practice

A string of length 9 is tested against the pumping lemma with p = 3. How many splits satisfy the two conditions, that is, have a window of at most 3 letters and at least one letter in v or y?

  1. 36
  2. 121
  3. 495
  4. 1106

Answer: (B) 121. Summed over window lengths s = 1, 2, 3: (9)(2) + (8)(5) + (7)(9) = 18 + 40 + 63 = 121. Verified here against brute force.

(A) counts only windows of one and two letters. (C) is the count without the window condition. (D) is the count for a string of length 18 at p = 6.

MSQ original practice

Which of the following are true about the pumping lemma for context-free languages?

I. A language that satisfies it is context-free.
II. A language that fails it at some p is not context-free.
III. It can be used to prove a language is not context-free.
IV. Its pumping length depends on the grammar, not on the strings.

  1. I and II only
  2. III and IV only
  3. II, III and IV only
  4. I, III and IV only

Answer: (2), III and IV only.

I is false: the four-block language survives every tested p and is not context-free. II is false: anbn fails at p = 1 and is context-free. III is true: that is its only use. IV is true: p = 2k comes from the grammar’s nonterminal count, and one p must serve every string in the language.

MCQ original practice

In Ogden’s lemma, which condition replaces the plain lemma’s freedom to split anywhere in the window?

  1. Every letter of vxy must be marked.
  2. v and y together contain at least one marked position, and vxy contains at most p marked positions.
  3. All marked positions must lie inside x.
  4. The number of marked positions must equal p exactly.

Answer: (B). Those are the two extra conditions. The statement requires at least p marked positions in the whole string, not exactly p.

(A) is stronger than the lemma and would make it false. (C) inverts the condition, which is the point of the tool: the pump must touch a mark. (D) confuses “at least p” with “exactly p”.

MCQ original practice

Which argument proves that { aibjckdl : i = 0 or j = k = l } is not context-free, given that it survives the plain pumping lemma?

  1. It fails the pumping lemma at p = 1.
  2. Intersecting it with a+b*c*d* gives a language that is not context-free.
  3. Its complement is regular.
  4. It is not closed under reversal.

Answer: (B). The intersection is { aibjcjdj : i ≥ 1 }, refuted at every p by the search here, and context-free languages are closed under intersection with a regular language.

(A) is false: the computed table shows survival at every p from 1 to 6. (C) is irrelevant and false. (D) is not a closure property that decides the class: context-free languages are closed under reversal.

NAT original practice

A language is refuted by the pumping lemma at every p from 1 to 12, and no argument is given for p = 13 or beyond. How many values of p have been proved invalid?

Answer: 12. Twelve values, and that is the whole content of the computation.

The tempting answer is “all of them”, which is exactly the misconception this question tests. Refuting p = 1 to 12 proves that no context-free grammar for the language has any of those twelve pumping lengths; it says nothing about 13, 16 or 1024.

MCQ original practice

Which pair of languages is separated by one stack, in the sense that the first is context-free and the second is not?

  1. { anbn } and { anbmcn+m }
  2. { anbmcn+m } and { anbnc2n }
  3. { anbnc2n } and { anbn }
  4. { anbn } and { w wR }

Answer: (B). The first is context-free because the c count is the sum of two counts, one counter. The second demands i = j and 2j = k at once, two counters.

(A), (C) and (D) each pair two context-free languages, so nothing is separated. (C) also reverses the order, which changes nothing about the classes.

MCQ original practice

Over the alphabet {a}, the language { ap : p is prime } is:

  1. regular
  2. context-free but not regular
  3. not context-free, but recursive
  4. not recursive

Answer: (C). Every context-free language over a one-letter alphabet is regular, and this one is not regular, so it is not context-free. A Turing machine that tests primality by trial division decides it, so it is recursive.

(B) is the standard wrong answer, and it is the trap in GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 statement I. The computed search refutes it at every p from 1 to 6 on 17 test strings, which is consistent with, but not a proof of, (C); the proof is the unary-alphabet fact.

The statement, with the quantifiers in order

∃p > 0   ∀w ∈ L, |w| ≥ p   ∃u, v, x, y, z : w = uvxyz, |vxy| ≤ p, vy ≠ ε, ∀i ≥ 0 : uvixyiz ∈ L

Ogden, in one line

Same five pieces; you mark at least p positions first; then vy must contain a marked position and vxy at most p marked ones. Stronger than the plain lemma, and usually two cases instead of six.

The numbers worth remembering

p = 2k for a normal-form grammar with k nonterminals: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256. Splits at n = 9, p = 3: 121. At n = 18, p = 6: 1106.

The counting test

One independent equality: context-free. A sum: context-free. Two equalities at once across three blocks: not context-free. Nested pairs: context-free. Crossing pairs: not.

Five traps

Surviving the lemma proves nothing. A small refuted p proves nothing. Two pumped pieces, not one. The window condition is the mechanism, not decoration. Two counts are not automatically two too many.

One line to carry into the exam

The lemma kills languages, never saves them: to convict, leave p as a symbol and let the window decide the cases; to acquit, build a grammar.
