Topic and objective

TOPIC: Bayes' theorem as a revision rule — how a prior probability, a likelihood and the total probability of the evidence combine into a posterior probability, and what that posterior is allowed to be used for afterwards.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Write the three inputs of every Bayes question (prior, likelihood, evidence) and compute the posterior from them. Compute the evidence as a sum over every hypothesis, and say what goes wrong when one hypothesis is missing. Recognise the base-rate case, where a test that is 99 per cent accurate still leaves a positive result no better than a coin toss. Use the odds form, where each observation multiplies the odds by a likelihood ratio. Update twice on the same coin and get the same answer as updating once on both tosses. Tell P(A | B) from P(B | A) when a question supplies both. State the two conditions the hypotheses must satisfy before the rule may be applied at all.

I am reading this microtopic as one habit: before answering "how likely is the cause, now that I have seen the effect", write down what you believed before, what the effect would look like under each cause, and how often the effect happens at all. The five sections of the lesson are that habit applied five ways.

Prerequisites

  • Probability, random variables and distributions — the axioms, conditional probability as a ratio, independence, and the law of total probability. That lesson already works one Bayes question; this one generalises it.
  • Conditional expectation — the same "average inside the part, weight by the parts" move that Bayes uses, and the reason a missing part of a partition is a silent error.
  • Counting and combinatorics — needed only to read a likelihood off a box of balls.
  • Fractions. Every number in this lesson is exact, and several answers are exact fractions such as 4/19 and 3/8.

No calculus is used anywhere in this lesson.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A posterior is a prior times a likelihood, divided by the evidence. Three inputs, one output.
  • The evidence is a sum over every hypothesis. Leave one out and every posterior is inflated.
  • Rare causes stay rare after a positive test. The base rate, not the accuracy, sets the scale.
  • Each observation multiplies the odds by a likelihood ratio. That is the form that scales.
  • P(cause | effect) is not P(effect | cause). They share a numerator and differ in the denominator.

From a conditional probability to a revision

Bayes' theorem is not a new axiom. It is the definition of conditional probability, written twice and divided. Everything that follows — the base-rate result, the odds form, sequential updating — is that one line rearranged.

Level 1 — the intuition

You believe something. Something happens. You believe it more, or less. Bayes is the size of that change, and it is not a matter of taste: it is fixed by how often the thing you saw happens under each of the possibilities.

The usual picture is a room of people. Of every 10,000, a hundred have a disease and 9,900 do not. A test catches 99 of the hundred and wrongly flags 99 of the 9,900. Among the people holding a positive result, 99 are ill and 99 are healthy. The answer is not "99 per cent accurate, so 99 per cent likely". It is one half.

Level 2 — the formal statement

Let $$H_1, \dots, H_k$$ be hypotheses that are disjoint (at most one is true) and exhaustive (at least one is true), and let $$E$$ be observed evidence with $$P(E) > 0$$. Then for each $$i$$:

$$P(H_i \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(E \mid H_j)\,P(H_j)}$$

In words: the probability of hypothesis $$i$$ given the evidence equals the probability of the evidence given hypothesis $$i$$, times the probability of hypothesis $$i$$ before the evidence, divided by the probability of the evidence computed over all hypotheses.

The numerator is the joint probability $$P(H_i \cap E)$$. The denominator is $$P(E)$$. So the theorem is just $$P(H_i \mid E) = P(H_i \cap E) / P(E)$$, with the denominator expanded.

Level 3 — the vocabulary, named once

  • Prior — $$P(H_i)$$, what you believed before the evidence. It must come from somewhere outside the theorem: a design, a count, or an assumption.
  • Likelihood — $$P(E \mid H_i)$$, how well hypothesis $$i$$ explains what you saw. Note the direction: evidence given hypothesis.
  • Evidence (also marginal likelihood or normalising constant) — $$P(E)$$, how often the evidence happens at all. It is the same number for every hypothesis, which is why it can be dropped when only a ranking is wanted.
  • Posterior — $$P(H_i \mid E)$$, the revised belief. The posteriors over all $$i$$ add to one.

Level 4 — where the rule comes from, in three lines

Write the definition of conditional probability in both directions. Both expressions contain the same joint probability, so they may be equated.

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(H \cap E)}{P(E)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(E \mid H) = \frac{P(H \cap E)}{P(H)}$$

The second line rearranges to $$P(H \cap E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H)$$. Substituting that numerator into the first line gives the theorem. The denominator $$P(E)$$ is then expanded by the law of total probability over the hypotheses, which is the sum in Level 2. Derivation

Nothing here assumes independence, and nothing here is Bayesian in any philosophical sense — it is a theorem about conditional probability that holds for every probability space.

Seven worked items, each with its arithmetic shown

1. Five coins, two heads

A box holds five coins: four regular with P(head) = 0.5, one fake with P(head) = 1. Draw one at random, toss twice, see two heads. What is P(fake)?

Prior: 1/5 and 4/5. Likelihood: 1 under the fake, 1/4 under a regular. Products: 0.20 and 0.20. Evidence: 0.40.

Posterior: 0.20 / 0.40 = 0.50. Two heads is strong evidence — it quadruples the odds — and it still only reaches an even chance, because the prior odds were 1 to 4. Derivation

2. The base rate, at four prevalences

Sensitivity 99 per cent, specificity 99 per cent, held fixed. Only the prevalence changes.

Prevalence 0.1 per cent gives a posterior of 0.0902; 1 per cent gives 0.50; 10 per cent gives 0.9167; 50 per cent gives 0.99. The test never changed. The prior did all the work.

3. The noisy channel — GATE CSE 2021 Set 1

Sent H with probability 0.1 and L with 0.9; received H with probability 0.3 when H was sent and 0.8 when L was sent.

Paths: 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.03 and 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72. Evidence: 0.75.

P(sent H | received H) = 0.03 / 0.75 = 0.04, and the other posterior is 0.72 / 0.75 = 0.96. The two add to one. The channel is most likely to have carried L even though H is the signal it most faithfully reproduces.

4. Three boxes, one white ball — GATE DA 2025

Priors 1/2, 1/6, 1/3; the boxes give white with probabilities 1/3, 2/3, 1/2.

Products: 1/6, 1/9, 1/6. Evidence: 4/9.

Posteriors: 3/8, 1/4, 3/8. Box 2 has the highest likelihood of producing white and the lowest posterior, because it was the least likely box to have been chosen. This is a Data Science and AI paper item, labelled as such.

5. Two boxes, one red ball — GATE CSE 2005

Box P: chosen with 1/3, red with 2/5. Box Q: chosen with 2/3, red with 3/4.

Products: 2/15 and 1/2. Evidence: 19/30.

P(P | red) = (2/15) / (19/30) = 4/19, and P(Q | red) = 15/19. The box that was twice as likely to be chosen is also the box that produces red more often, so the evidence barely moves the belief.

6. Both conditional directions — GATE CSE 2017 Set 2

P(P applies) = 1/4, P(P | Q) = 1/2, P(Q | P) = 1/3. The question asks for P(P does not apply | Q does not apply).

Joint: (1/3)(1/4) = 1/12. Then P(Q) = (1/12)/(1/2) = 1/6. Union: 1/4 + 1/6 − 1/12 = 1/3.

P(neither) = 2/3 and P(Q does not) = 5/6, so the answer is (2/3)/(5/6) = 4/5. Note the two given conditionals are 1/2 and 1/3: same joint, different denominators.

7. One head at a time, on the same coin

Take item 1 again, but update after each toss instead of both at once. After one head: 0.20 / (0.20 + 0.5 × 0.80) = 0.20 / 0.60 = 1/3. After the second head, with 1/3 as the new prior: (1/3) / (1/3 + 0.5 × 2/3) = (1/3) / (1/2) = 1/2.

The chain 0.200 → 0.333 → 0.500 lands exactly on the one-step answer, and the page checks the agreement for every count of heads from zero to five. Derivation Each head multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio 1 / 0.5 = 2, so the odds run 1:4, 1:2, 1:1.

The boundaries of the rule

Bayes' theorem has two hypotheses and one positivity condition written into it. Each of the four items below is one of them failing, with the arithmetic shown rather than described.

Evidence that cannot happen

If every likelihood is zero, the denominator is zero and the quotient is 0/0. The posterior does not exist.

This is the same fact as "never condition on a null event": $$P(H \mid E)$$ is defined only when $$P(E) > 0$$. In an exam, an option that quietly divides by zero is wrong even when its other numbers look right.

A hypothesis missing from the sum

Three hypotheses with priors 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and likelihoods 0.5, 0.2, 0.4 give contributions 0.25, 0.06, 0.08 and evidence 0.39. The first posterior is 0.25 / 0.39 = 0.641.

Omit the third hypothesis and the evidence becomes 0.31, so the same posterior becomes 0.25 / 0.31 = 0.806 — inflated by 39/31. The posteriors still add to one, which is exactly why the arithmetic check does not catch this.

Overlapping hypotheses

If two hypotheses can both be true, an outcome that belongs to both is counted in two paths, so $$P(E)$$ is over-counted and the posteriors are pulled towards the overlap.

The condition is disjointness, not independence. Independence is never required by Bayes; disjointness always is.

The same observation, entered twice

On the five-coin box, a single head gives 1/3. Enter that one head twice, as if it were two independent tosses, and the answer becomes 1/2.

Multiplying likelihoods is legal only for observations that are conditionally independent given the hypothesis. Two reports of one event are not two events.

A case where the answer is legitimately one half

The base-rate result is not a paradox and not an error in the test. With prevalence 1 per cent and a 99 per cent accurate test, the two groups holding a positive result are of equal size — 99 true positives against 99 false positives — so one half is the correct answer and the arithmetic says so twice, once by formula and once by counting people. A test can be very good and still leave you undecided, because the group it is applied to is overwhelmingly healthy.

Six beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each entry states the belief, then the counterexample that kills it. These are the errors the practice set is built to catch.

"A 99 per cent accurate test means a positive result is 99 per cent certain."

Wrong: accuracy describes the test given the disease, not the disease given the test. The sensitivity 0.99 is $$P(+ \mid \text{ill})$$; the question asks for $$P(\text{ill} \mid +)$$.

Counterexample: at 1 per cent prevalence the answer is 0.50; at 0.1 per cent prevalence it is 0.09. Same test, different answers, because the prior changed.

"The hypothesis with the largest likelihood is the most probable cause."

Wrong: rank by prior × likelihood, not by likelihood.

Counterexample: the three boxes of GATE DA 2025. Box 2 has likelihood 2/3, the largest of the three, and posterior 1/4, the smallest, because its prior was only 1/6.

"P(A | B) and P(B | A) are the same number."

Wrong: they share the numerator $$P(A \cap B)$$ and differ in the denominator, $$P(B)$$ against $$P(A)$$.

Counterexample: GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 supplies $$P(P \mid Q) = 1/2$$ and $$P(Q \mid P) = 1/3$$ in the same question, with $$P(P) = 1/4$$ and $$P(Q) = 1/6$$. Both are correct, and they are different numbers.

"The prior can be skipped when the evidence is strong."

Wrong: dropping the prior is the same as assuming all priors are equal. Sometimes that is a stated assumption and sometimes it is a silent error.

Counterexample: five coins with equal priors would give 0.20 / (0.20 + 0.20) only because the two priors happen to be equal. Change the box to four fake and one regular coin and the same two heads gives 0.9412 instead of 0.50.

"The posterior is the probability that the hypothesis is true, full stop."

Wrong in a precise sense: the posterior is the probability given the prior you supplied and the model you assumed. Change either input and the posterior changes.

This matters for the interpretation question: Bayes is arithmetic on conditional probability, and it does not decide where a prior comes from. In an exam the prior is always given; in a real decision it is the contested input.

"The posteriors must add to one, so the answer is safe."

Wrong: they add to one whenever you divide by whatever sum you computed, including a sum that left a hypothesis out.

Counterexample: the missing-hypothesis case in Edge cases — 0.641 becomes 0.806, and the two posteriors still add to one. The check that catches it is that the hypotheses must be exhaustive, not the addition.

The pair most often confused: likelihood against posterior

$$P(E \mid H)$$ answers "how often would I see this, if the hypothesis were true". $$P(H \mid E)$$ answers "how likely is the hypothesis, now that I have seen this". The first is an input you read off the problem — a die face, a test's sensitivity, a box's contents. The second is the output. On the five-coin box the likelihood of two heads under the fake coin is 1, and the posterior is 0.50; reporting 1 as the answer is the single most common lost mark in this topic.

What the question actually asks, and how to tell

Four shapes cover almost every Bayes item in the papers. The first move is always the same: find the word "given" and read which direction it points.

Pattern 1 — the cause given the effect

The text says "given that the ball drawn is white" or "given that the received signal is H". You are asked for the cause. This is the plain Bayes computation.

Move: write the prior and the likelihood for each cause, multiply, add, divide. Examples: CSE 2005 Q51 (4/19), CSE 2021 Set 1 Q54 (0.04), CSE 2025 Set 1 Q32 (0.50).

Pattern 2 — the effect alone

The text asks only for the probability of the observation, with no cause named: "the probability that a bulb chosen at random lasts more than 100 hours".

Move: this is the denominator of Bayes with no division afterwards — the law of total probability. CSE 2016 Set 2 asks exactly this and the answer is 0.55. Recognising it saves a minute of needless work.

Pattern 3 — two directions in one question

The text supplies one conditional in each direction, plus one marginal. The trap is to use the wrong one.

Move: convert both conditionals into the joint, which is the only quantity they agree on, then rebuild whichever marginal you need. CSE 2017 Set 2 Q26 does this and ends at 4/5.

Pattern 4 — the complement

The question asks for "does not apply, given that the other does not apply". Both events are negated.

Move: $$P(A' \mid B') = P(A' \cap B') / P(B')$$, and $$P(A' \cap B') = 1 - P(A \cup B)$$. De Morgan's law does the work; Bayes is not needed once the joint is known.

Answer formats

Bayes items appear as NAT with a two-decimal instruction (CSE 2025 Set 1, CSE 2021 Set 1) and as MCQ with fraction options (CSE 2005, CSE 2017 Set 2). For NAT, compute the fraction exactly first and round once at the end; 0.04 from 1/25 and 0.50 from 1/2 are exact, so rounding cannot bite. For MCQ, compare the option to your fraction rather than to a decimal.

Time budget

A two-hypothesis Bayes item should take about ninety seconds: thirty to write the four numbers, thirty to multiply and add, thirty to divide and check the sum is one. A three-hypothesis item takes about two minutes. If you are past three minutes, the likely cause is a denominator built from the wrong set of hypotheses.

NATGATE CSE 2025, Set 1, Q32

A box contains 5 coins: 4 regular coins and 1 fake coin. When a regular coin is tossed, the probability P(head) = 0.5 and for a fake coin, P(head) = 1. You pick a coin at random and toss it twice, and get two heads. The probability that the coin you have chosen is the fake coin is _______. (rounded off to two decimal places)

Answer: 0.50

Priors 1/5 and 4/5. Likelihoods: two heads is certain under the fake coin and 1/4 under a regular one. Products 0.20 and 0.20, so the evidence is 0.40 and the posterior is 0.20 / 0.40 = 0.50.

The likely error. Reporting the prior 0.20, or the likelihood 1, or weighting the regular coin's 1/4 by 1/2 instead of 4/5. The last of these gives 1/3, which is the answer after a single head — a good check on your own arithmetic.

The official key accepts 0.49 to 0.51. This is a CSE paper item.

MCQGATE CSE 2017, Set 2, Q26

P and Q are considering to apply for a job. The probability that P applies for the job is 1/4, the probability that P applies for the job given that Q applies for the job is 1/2, and the probability that Q applies for the job given that P applies for the job is 1/3. Then the probability that P does not apply for the job given that Q does not apply for the job is

  1. 4/5
  2. 5/6
  3. 7/8
  4. 11/12

Answer: A — 4/5

From P(Q | P) = 1/3 and P(P) = 1/4, the joint is 1/12. From P(P | Q) = 1/2, P(Q) = (1/12)/(1/2) = 1/6. Then P(P or Q) = 1/4 + 1/6 - 1/12 = 1/3, so P(neither) = 2/3, and P(Q does not) = 5/6. The answer is (2/3)/(5/6) = 4/5.

Why B appears. It is P(Q does not) itself, the denominator, reported as the answer. Why C and D appear. Both come from arithmetic slips in the union: using 1/4 + 1/6 without subtracting the joint gives 5/12 and a different quotient, and subtracting the joint twice shifts it the other way.

This is a CSE paper item.

MCQGATE CSE 2005, Q51

Box P has 2 red balls and 3 blue balls and box Q has 3 red balls and 1 blue ball. A ball is selected as follows: (i) select a box (ii) choose a ball from the selected box such that each ball in the box is equally likely to be chosen. The probabilities of selecting boxes P and Q are 1/3 and 2/3 respectively. Given that a ball selected in the above process is a red ball, the probability that it came from the box P is

  1. 4/19
  2. 5/19
  3. 2/9
  4. 19/30

Answer: A — 4/19

Products: (1/3)(2/5) = 2/15 and (2/3)(3/4) = 1/2. Evidence: 2/15 + 1/2 = 19/30. Posterior: (2/15)/(19/30) = 4/19.

Why D appears. 19/30 is the evidence itself — the denominator, not the answer. Why C appears. 2/9 is the ratio of the two likelihoods with the priors left out, which is the error the unequal box probabilities are there to catch. Why B appears. 5/19 is 1 minus 4/19, the posterior of the other box, which answers a different question.

This is a CSE paper item. The question number is quoted from question catalogues that agree on it.

NATGATE CSE 2016, Set 2

Suppose that a shop has an equal number of LED bulbs of two different types. The probability of an LED bulb lasting more than 100 hours given that it is of Type 1 is 0.7, and given that it is of Type 2 is 0.4. The probability that an LED bulb chosen uniformly at random lasts more than 100 hours is _______.

Answer: 0.55

Equal numbers of the two types means each is chosen with probability 1/2. The law of total probability gives 0.5 × 0.7 + 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.35 + 0.20 = 0.55.

The trap this question avoids setting. There is no "given that" clause pointing at a cause, so no division follows. A candidate who computes 0.7/0.55 or 0.4/0.55 has answered a question that was not asked. If the paper had added "given that it lasted more than 100 hours, the probability it is Type 1", the answer would be 0.35/0.55 = 0.6364.

This is a CSE paper item.

NATOriginal practice question

A disease affects 1 person in 1000. A test detects it in 99 per cent of affected people and gives a false positive in 1 per cent of unaffected people. A person chosen at random tests positive. The probability that this person has the disease, rounded to three decimal places, is _______.

Answer: 0.090

Prior 0.001. Products: 0.001 × 0.99 = 0.00099 for the ill, and 0.999 × 0.01 = 0.00999 for the healthy. Evidence 0.01098. Posterior 0.00099 / 0.01098 = 0.0902, which rounds to 0.090.

Why this is not 0.99. The false positives outnumber the true positives ten to one, because the healthy group is a thousand times larger. Counting 100,000 people: 100 ill, 99 detected; 99,900 healthy, 999 flagged. 99 out of 1098 is 0.090.

MSQOriginal practice question

Let $$H_1, \dots, H_k$$ be hypotheses and $$E$$ observed evidence with $$P(E) > 0$$. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. Bayes' theorem requires the hypotheses to be independent of one another.
  2. If one hypothesis is omitted from the sum in the denominator, every computed posterior is too large.
  3. Multiplying likelihoods across several observations is valid when the observations are conditionally independent given each hypothesis.
  4. If two hypotheses overlap, the computed posteriors can still add to one while both are wrong.

Answer: B, C and D

B is true. Leaving a term out shrinks the denominator, so every quotient grows. The page's own example: 0.641 becomes 0.806.

C is true. That is exactly the condition under which the joint likelihood factors, and it is what makes sequential updating exact.

D is true. Dividing by whatever sum you computed always produces numbers that add to one, whether or not the sum was right.

A is false. The hypotheses must be disjoint and exhaustive. They are usually the opposite of independent — if one is true the others are false by construction.

NATOriginal practice question

A bag holds two coins: one is double-headed and one is fair. A coin is drawn at random and tossed twice; both tosses show heads. The probability that the drawn coin is the double-headed one is _______.

Answer: 0.8 (that is, 4/5)

Priors 1/2 each. Likelihoods: 1 under the double-headed coin and 1/4 under the fair one. Products 1/2 and 1/8, evidence 5/8. Posterior (1/2)/(5/8) = 4/5 = 0.8.

The second route, as a check. The prior odds are 1:1 and the likelihood ratio is 1/(1/4) = 4, so the posterior odds are 4:1, which is a probability of 4/5. The comparison worth remembering. With five coins (four regular, one fake) the same two heads gives only 0.50, because the prior odds there are 1:4 rather than 1:1.

Revision sheet

The formula, the procedure, the two conditions, the four traps, and the four numbers worth remembering.

Formula and its three rearrangements

$$P(H_i \mid E) = \dfrac{P(E \mid H_i)P(H_i)}{\sum_j P(E \mid H_j)P(H_j)}$$ — the fraction form.
Posterior odds $$= \dfrac{P(E \mid H_i)}{P(E \mid H_j)} \times$$ prior odds — the odds form, where the evidence cancels.
$$P(H \mid E) = \dfrac{P(H \cap E)}{P(E)}$$ — the definition, which is where it comes from.

The two conditions

Disjoint: no outcome belongs to two hypotheses, so no path is counted twice. Exhaustive: every way the evidence can happen appears in the sum. Positivity: $$P(E) > 0$$, or the quotient is undefined. Independence is not required, and independence of the hypotheses is the opposite of what is wanted.

The five-step procedure

  1. Name the hypotheses; check disjoint and exhaustive.
  2. Write each prior, and the likelihood of the evidence under it.
  3. Multiply; add the products to get the evidence.
  4. Divide the wanted product by the evidence.
  5. Check the posteriors add to one, and re-read the direction asked.

The four traps

  • Reporting the likelihood as the posterior.
  • Leaving a hypothesis out of the denominator.
  • Swapping $$P(A \mid B)$$ and $$P(B \mid A)$$.
  • Multiplying likelihoods for observations that are not conditionally independent.

Four numbers to carry into the exam

0.50 — five coins, four regular and one fake, two heads (CSE 2025 Set 1). 0.04 — the noisy channel, H received (CSE 2021 Set 1). 4/19 — box P given red (CSE 2005). 4/5 — P does not apply, given Q does not (CSE 2017 Set 2). If a practice attempt misses one of these, the error is almost always in the denominator.

Quick self-check

A test with sensitivity 0.9 and specificity 0.9 is applied to a population with prevalence 0.01. Without computing, say whether the posterior after a positive result is nearer 0.5 or 0.9, then check: the true positives are 0.009 and the false positives 0.099, so the answer is 0.083. The point of the exercise is to trust the base rate before the arithmetic.

What could not be confirmed

Question numbers for the 2021, 2025 and 2005 items come from question catalogues rather than from the official papers, and catalogues disagree with each other. The paper, the year and the question text were confirmed; the number should be treated as a catalogue's filing. The 2016 Set 2 item is quoted without a question number for the same reason.

No claim is made about how many marks this topic will carry in GATE CSE 2027. The community observation on the Overview tab is a report about past papers, not a prediction.

What the page computes for itself

Nothing on the canvas is copied from a source. The five-coin posterior, the base-rate table across four prevalences, the noisy channel, the odds chain, the sequential update to five heads, the three boxes, the two conditional directions, the two-box posterior, the missing-hypothesis inflation and the double-counted observation are all computed in the lesson's own model, and 47 checks re-derive them a second way — exact fractions where the numbers are rational, and the law of total probability where the posterior must sum to one.
