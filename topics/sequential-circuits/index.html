Topic and objective

TOPIC: Design of sequential circuits — the four things GATE builds every question from: the latch and the flip-flop (how one bit is stored), the register and the counter (how bits are moved and counted), the finite-state machine (how a behaviour is turned into gates), and timing (why a correct circuit still has a maximum clock frequency).

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a circuit of flip-flops and gates, write its next-state equations, tabulate its states, and follow the cycle. Given a required behaviour, produce a state table and the flip-flop input expressions. Given delay numbers, compute the maximum clock frequency and say separately whether hold is satisfied. Distinguish a latch from a flip-flop, a Moore machine from a Mealy one, and the frequency of a counter from the frequency at its last stage.

I read this topic as sequential only, the second half of the syllabus line "Design of combinational and sequential circuits". The combinational half — adders, multiplexers, decoders, hazards — is a published lesson and this one assumes it. Nothing here re-teaches minimisation or the adder.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus.

Section 2 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus (IIT Madras, CS_GATE2027_Syllabus.pdf) reads: "Boolean algebra and minimization – algebraic technique, Karnaugh map, tabular method. Design of combinational and sequential circuits. Number representation and arithmetic (fixed and floating point)." The words "sequential circuits" are named directly.

The topic also feeds Section 3: "Design of arithmetic and logic unit (ALU). Design of control unit – hardwired and microprogrammed." A hardwired control unit is a finite-state machine, and the FSM material in this lesson is its prerequisite.

Class A means a question here is inside the declared boundary. It is not a promise that GATE 2027 contains one, and this lesson makes no claim about marks or subject weightage. The previous-year questions quoted are observations about old papers, each named with its year and paper.

Prerequisites

Three published lessons carry the machinery this one uses:

  • Combinational Circuits — gate networks, reading an expression off a network by labelling every wire, the multiplexer, and the static hazard. A latch is two gates with the output wired back, so reading a loop is the one new skill.
  • Boolean Algebra and Minimisation — canonical forms, the Karnaugh map and don't-cares. Every FSM design step ends in a minimisation, and the don't-care treatment of unused states comes straight from that lesson.
  • Number Representation and Arithmetic — binary encoding and the idea that a bit pattern means nothing until you name its representation. A state assignment is exactly that idea.

The one genuinely new ingredient is time: a stored value changes only when the clock says so, and the clock takes a finite time to be obeyed.

Visualization plan

Ten scenes, each one argument:

  1. State. The feedback path, the state table, and Moore against Mealy.
  2. The SR latch. A loop that reproduces its own value, the forbidden input, and the race — both found by iterating the gate equations.
  3. D latch and D flip-flop. One inverter removes the forbidden input; two latches with opposite enables sample once.
  4. JK and T. The four-mode table as one equation, and the race-around condition counted in clock widths.
  5. Timing. Clock-to-Q, setup, hold, and the maximum frequency over the slowest register-to-register path.
  6. Counters. The divide-by-two chain, 64 microseconds to 250 kilohertz, the settling time, and the synchronous toggle rule.
  7. Shift registers. The ring counter's n states, the Johnson counter's 2n, and the computed 0, 8, 12, 14, 15, 7, 3, 1.
  8. FSM design. Five steps, the free choice of assignment, the excitation tables, and the lock-out check.
  9. Analysis. Equations, table, cycle, then the unused states — with a real circuit walked clock by clock.
  10. The table. Five confused pairs, five traps, one order of operations.

What you should be able to do afterwards

Answer any sequential question in this order: what is the state?, what is the next-state function?, what does the clock have to do with it?

The most expensive mistake in this topic is answering a timing question about a circuit whose state you never established, or answering a state question as though the clock did not exist. The two halves are asked separately and marked separately.

Level 1 — Intuition: a door that stays open

You already own one. A push-button doorbell that latches a relay is a memory. Press once and the relay stays on after your finger leaves; press the other button and it stays off. Nobody is holding it. The circuit is holding itself.

That is what a sequential circuit adds to a combinational one: a wire from the output back to the input, so the circuit can be its own input. The value travelling round that loop is called the state, and "one bit of memory" means "one loop".

Educational interpretation The relay is exact for the holding part and only an analogy for the clocking part: a real relay settles when it settles, with no edge telling it when to look. A flip-flop looks once, at the edge. That difference is scene 3.

Level 2 — The formal definition

A sequential circuit is a network of gates and storage elements in which the outputs at any instant are determined by the present inputs and by a stored value called the state. Formally it is two functions and a set:

next : S × Σ → S     out : S × Σ → O (Mealy)  or  out : S → O (Moore)

Read that aloud: the next state is a function of the present state and the present input; the output is a function of the same two things (Mealy) or of the state alone (Moore). The set $$S$$ is the finite set of states, $$\Sigma$$ the input alphabet, $$O$$ the output alphabet.

A flip-flop is the storage element: one bit, changed only at a clock edge. n of them hold an encoding of at most $$2^n$$ states.

Level 3 — Visual understanding: the loop is the memory

Two NOR gates, each output wired into the other's input. Gate 1 computes its output from R and from gate 2's output. Gate 2 computes its output from S and from gate 1's output. Neither can be evaluated alone: they must be solved as a pair.

A pair of values that reproduces itself through both gates is a stable state. The circuit has two of them, $$(Q,Q')=(1,0)$$ and $$(0,1)$$. Sitting in one and holding the inputs quiet, it stays there indefinitely. That is the whole mechanism of memory, and it needs no clock.

Mathematical derivation The page does not assert the two stable states; it iterates the two gate equations from every starting pair and reports which pairs stop changing. The two above are the only fixed points with both inputs at zero.

Level 4 — Worked examples, in the animation

Four traces carry the whole build-up, and the page computes each one:

  • The latch. S equal to one from a cleared state settles at one; R equal to one settles at zero; both zero holds; both one collapses to a pair of zeros; releasing both oscillates forever.
  • The master-slave flip-flop. A waveform in which the data moves while the clock is high, showing the output ignoring it and taking only the value present at the rising edge.
  • The race-around. A JK flip-flop with both inputs high, toggling once per gate delay for as long as the clock stays high.
  • The counter. A 64-microsecond period at the last of four stages, divided back to a 4-microsecond clock.

Level 5 — Boundaries: when this model applies

The state-table model describes a circuit that has settled. Between clock edges the gates are still moving, and during that window the table's promise is suspended. Everything in the timing scene lives in that window.

Two modelling assumptions are worth naming because exam questions depend on them. First, a flip-flop samples once per edge: data present at the edge is what is captured, and data that moves afterwards is ignored until the next edge. Second, gate delays are finite and positive: if they were zero there would be no setup time, no hold time and no race.

Educational interpretation Real hardware adds metastability: a flip-flop whose data moves inside the setup window can hang between levels for an unpredictable time. No question in this syllabus asks you to quantify it, but it is the reason the setup window is a hard constraint rather than a suggestion.

Level 6 — Edge cases and counterexamples

Each of these is a case where a rule that works usually stops working:

  • The invalid encoding. A mod-6 counter in three flip-flops has two encodings it never visits. They are real states, reachable after power-on, and the design must say where they go.
  • The locked ring counter. All zeros is a fixed point of a ring counter's feedback. It is not a bug; it is the circuit working exactly as built, on an input it was not initialised out of.
  • The counterexample to "synchronous is faster". A 64-bit synchronous binary counter's toggle logic is an AND of sixty-three bits. Count that path before calling it fast; the ripple's disadvantage is linear, not absolute.
  • The counterexample to "more flip-flops, more states". Three flip-flops give eight encodings and the mod-6 counter uses six of them.

Level 7 — How GATE asks it

Six shapes cover almost every question. The GATE patterns tab works each one:

  • Count the flip-flops a given modulus needs (2011, Q15).

  • Convert a period at a stage into the counter's frequency (2025 Set 2, Q34).
  • Compute a maximum clock frequency from delay numbers.

Levels 8 and 9 — Practice, then consolidation

The practice tab holds eight questions: four are restatements of verified previous-year questions with the year and paper named, four are original. Answers are hidden until you reveal them, and mock-test mode hides them all until you submit.

The consolidation is three sentences. A latch is transparent; a flip-flop samples once. A ripple counter divides the clock and adds its delays; a synchronous one shares the clock and adds its logic. A machine is not finished until you have said where its unused states go.

Example 1 — The NOR latch, solved as a pair

Gate equations: $$Q = \overline{R + Q'}$$ and $$Q' = \overline{S + Q}$$. Iterate them from a starting pair until the pair stops changing.

S=1, R=0, from (0,1) → (0,0) → (1,0) → (1,0)

It takes two steps and then it is stable: the latch is set. From (1,0) with R equal to one it walks to (0,1) and stays: reset. With both inputs zero, (1,0) and (0,1) are each already fixed points — that is holding.

Mathematical derivation The iteration is a real computation, run in this page's model. "Stable" means a pair that reproduces itself, not a pair that looks plausible.

Example 2 — The forbidden input, and the race

With S = R = 1 every gate is forced low whatever the other says, so the pair collapses to $$(0,0)$$: not a state, because the two outputs are supposed to be complements.

Now release both to zero at the same instant. From (0,0) the next step is (1,1), and the step after that is (0,0). The pair oscillates with period two and never settles. Physically the gates are never exactly matched, so the faster one wins and the value is unpredictable.

The honest answer to "what is the next state?" is: undefined. Writing down a 1 or a 0 is inventing a result the circuit does not have. This is the one place in the topic where "it depends on the hardware" is the correct exam answer.

Example 3 — The master-slave flip-flop on a waveform

Clock low: master open, slave shut. Clock high: master shut, slave open. Take the waveform where the clock is low, high, low, high, and the data is 1, 0, 0, 1 at the last low sample before each rising edge, then moves again while the clock is high.

The output takes 0 at the first rising edge and 1 at the second, and ignores every change made during the high levels. That is edge sampling, produced by two level-sensitive parts with opposite enables.

Mathematical derivation The page simulates the two latches and checks, for every rising edge, that the output equals the data at the previous sample, and that the output does not move while the clock stays high.

Example 4 — 64 microseconds to 250 kilohertz

A four-bit ripple counter: four toggle stages, so the last stage's period is $$2^4 = 16$$ times the clock period. Given 64 microseconds at the last stage:

Tclk = 64 µs ÷ 16 = 4 µs  →  fclk = 1 / 4 µs = 250 kHz

The counter's frequency is its input clock, 250 kHz. The frequency at the last stage is 15.625 kHz, and that is a different question.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, Question 34, with accepted answer 250. The arithmetic above is reproduced in the page's model from the divider, not copied from the answer.

Example 5 — The Johnson counter from 0000

Four flip-flops in a line; the complement of the last bit is fed back to the front. Write Q0 as the most significant bit and shift right, so the new front bit is the complement of the old last bit.

0000 → 1000 → 1100 → 1110 → 1111 → 0111 → 0011 → 0001 → 0000

In decimal: 0, 8, 12, 14, 15, 7, 3, 1, then back to 0. Eight states from four flip-flops, which is $$2n$$.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2015 Set 1, Question 11 (1 mark), with the sequence above as the accepted option. The page generates the cycle from the shift rule and prints the same eight values.

Example 6 — The 2017 mixed flip-flop circuit

A T flip-flop whose input is the other output, and a D flip-flop whose input is the T flip-flop's output. Name the outputs $$Q_1$$ (the T flip-flop) and $$Q_0$$ (the D flip-flop).

Q1+ = Q1 ⊕ Q0     Q0+ = Q1

From 11: next is 01. From 01: next is 10. From 10: next is 11. So the walk is 11, 01, 10, 11 — a three-state cycle. After the third clock the pair is 11; after the fourth it is 01.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, Question 33 (2 marks). The initial state counts as clock zero, which is why "after the third" is 11 and not 10.

Example 7 — A mod-6 counter, and where the spare states go

Three D flip-flops, counting 000 to 101 and back. With the unused states 110 and 111 treated as don't-cares, one valid minimal design is:

D0 = Q0′    D1 = Q1Q0′ + Q2′Q1′Q0    D2 = Q1Q0 + Q2Q0′

Check the cycle: 000→001→010→011→100→101→000. Check the spares: 110 goes to 111, and 111 goes to 100, which is in the cycle. So this design is self-starting — at most two clocks from any encoding into the cycle.

Mathematical derivation This is an original worked example, not a previous-year question. Every transition above is evaluated in the page's model, including the two unused ones.

Example 8 — The same machine, two assignments

The six states above were assigned 000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101. Assign them instead 000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111 — the same machine, a different encoding.

The state sequence in terms of states is identical; the expressions for $$D_0, D_1, D_2$$ are not. One assignment may need fewer gates or give a shorter critical path, and finding the best one is a hard problem in general.

Consequence for the exam: if a question asks for "the" minimal expression, it has fixed the assignment somewhere. Read that clause before deciding that two different correct-looking answers cannot both be right.

Edge case 1 — The invalid SR input is invalid in both directions

S = R = 1 is forbidden because it destroys the state, and the release from it is forbidden because it races. Both halves matter, and questions ask about the second half far more often: "the inputs are returned to zero simultaneously, what is Q?"

The answer is not 1 and not 0. The pair oscillates and the outcome depends on which gate is faster.

Edge case 2 — A latch built into a synchronous design

A transparent latch passes a narrow pulse that a flip-flop would ignore, because the path from data to output is open for the whole high level. A design that assumes edge behaviour but was built from latches works on the bench and fails on the first noisy input.

This is a counterexample to "a latch is a cheap flip-flop". It is a different component with a different timing model.

Edge case 3 — All zeros is a fixed point of a ring counter

With the feedback taken unchanged from the last stage, an all-zero register shifts zeros forever. The counter is not broken; it is doing exactly what the wiring says. A self-starting ring counter needs one extra gate to inject a one.

The Johnson counter escapes this because the feedback is inverted, so the first shift from all zeros writes a one.

Edge case 4 — Unused encodings are states, not gaps

A mod-6 counter in three flip-flops can be in 110 after power-on, after a glitch, or after a supply dip. The don't-cares that shrank the expressions also decided where 110 goes.

A machine is self-starting only if every unused encoding eventually reaches the cycle. One that maps to itself locks out, and no number of clocks recovers it.

Edge case 5 — Setup is not hold

Setup compares the longest path against the clock period; hold compares the shortest path against the hold time. A circuit can pass setup comfortably and fail hold, and lowering the frequency will not help, because the clock period is not in the hold inequality.

The repair for hold is to lengthen the short path, shorten the hold time, or reduce skew — never to slow the clock.

Edge case 6 — The race-around needs a level, not an edge

A level-triggered JK with J = K = 1 toggles once per gate delay for as long as the clock is high. Widen the clock and the output toggles more times. The final state is a function of the clock width, which is not a defined flip-flop behaviour.

An edge-triggered JK, or a master-slave pair, toggles exactly once. That is the entire reason edge-triggered devices became standard.

Edge case 7 — The counter's frequency is not the last stage's

An n-bit ripple counter divides its input clock by $$2^n$$. "The frequency of the counter" means the input clock; "the frequency at the last stage" is that divided by $$2^n$$. Confusing the two changes the answer by a factor of sixteen in a four-bit counter.

Read which waveform the question's period belongs to before inverting anything.

Counterexample — "More flip-flops give more states"

Three flip-flops give eight encodings. A mod-6 counter uses six of them and two are unreachable in normal operation. A mod-5 counter in three flip-flops uses five and leaves three.

The count of flip-flops is a lower bound on the encoding, not an equality with the number of states. The minimum for N states is the ceiling of log base 2 of N, and the design usually has exactly that.

Misconception 1 — "A latch and a flip-flop are the same thing"

The belief: both store a bit, so the words are interchangeable.

The correction: a latch is level-sensitive and transparent while enabled; a flip-flop is edge-triggered and samples once per edge. The timing model, the setup window and the race-around question all follow from that difference.

Minimal counterexample: a D latch with the enable held high copies every glitch on its data line; a D flip-flop clocked from the same signal ignores everything between edges.

Misconception 2 — "The forbidden SR state sets Q to zero"

The belief: S = R = 1 behaves like a reset, or like a toggle.

The correction: both NOR outputs are forced low, so the pair is not complementary and no state is stored. What happens next depends on how the inputs are released, and if they are released together the pair oscillates.

Minimal counterexample: hold S = R = 1 and observe both outputs at 0. Release both and observe the pair alternate with period two.

Misconception 3 — "Moore and Mealy differ only in notation"

The belief: the two models are two ways of writing the same table.

The correction: they differ in when the output is valid and in how many states are needed. A Mealy output depends on the present input, so it can change inside the cycle and can glitch; a Moore output is registered with the state and cannot.

Minimal counterexample: the "detect 101" machine needs three states as Mealy and four as Moore, and the Mealy output can respond in the same cycle the last bit arrives.

Misconception 4 — "Maximum frequency means the longest path"

The belief: count the gates on the biggest-looking path and invert the total.

The correction: the constraint is per register-to-register path: clock-to-Q of the launching flip-flop, plus that path's logic, plus setup time of the capturing flip-flop. The binding path is the one with the largest total, which need not have the most gates.

Minimal counterexample: a path with no gates at all can be the slowest, if it starts at a flip-flop with a large clock-to-Q and ends at one with a large setup time.

Misconception 5 — "Synchronous counters are always faster"

The belief: sharing the clock makes the counter faster at every width.

The correction: a synchronous binary counter toggles bit k only when every lower bit is one, so the toggle logic is an AND whose depth grows with the width. The ripple's delay is linear in n; the synchronous one trades that for a combinational path that also grows.

Minimal counterexample: a wide synchronous binary counter built with two-input gates has a toggle path of logarithmic depth, which is better than linear but is not constant.

Misconception 6 — "A correct cycle is a finished design"

The belief: if the counter visits the required states in the required order, the design is done.

The correction: the unused encodings still exist, and the circuit still defines a next state for them. If any unused state maps into a closed set of unused states, the machine can lock out.

Minimal counterexample: a mod-6 design in which 110 maps to 110. The six required states are all correct and the counter can still freeze forever after one glitch.

Pattern 1 — Trace a given circuit for k clocks

Given a diagram, write one next-state equation per flip-flop, then walk the table k steps. The equations come from the gate network; the flip-flop type converts an input into a next state.

D flip-flop: Q+ = D    T: Q+ = Q ⊕ T    JK: Q+ = JQ′ + K′Q

Worked case: GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, Question 33 (Example 6). The initial state is clock zero, so "after the third clock" is the third row of the walk, not the fourth.

Pattern 2 — Count the flip-flops for a modulus

A mod-N counter has N distinct states, so it needs the smallest n with $$2^n \ge N$$.

n = ⌈log2 N⌉

Worked case: GATE CSE 2011, Question 15 — mod 258 needs nine, because $$2^8 = 256 < 258$$. The distractors are 8 (one short) and 258 and 512 (the modulus and the next power of two).

Pattern 3 — Name the counter from its sequence

Three signatures are enough. Binary counts in order. A ring counter moves a single one, so consecutive states differ by one shift. A Johnson counter moves a block of ones, so its states are 2n in number and are not in numeric order.

Worked case: GATE CSE 2015 Set 1, Question 11 — the sequence 0, 8, 12, 14, 15, 7, 3, 1 is a Johnson counter from 0000. Option (A) in that paper is the mirror image, which is the same counter with the opposite bit order.

Pattern 4 — Convert a stage period into a frequency

In an n-bit ripple counter the last stage's period is $$2^n$$ clock periods, so divide before inverting. Read whether the given period belongs to the clock or to the last stage.

Worked case: GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, Question 34 — 64 µs at the last of four stages gives a 4 µs clock and 250 kHz. Dividing by four instead of sixteen gives 62.5 kHz; inverting 64 µs without dividing gives 15.6 kHz.

Pattern 5 — Maximum clock frequency from delay numbers

List every register-to-register path, compute clock-to-Q plus that path's logic plus setup for each, take the maximum, invert. Then check hold separately on the shortest path.

Worked case: a synchronous circuit with clock-to-Q 2 ns, a longest logic path of 6 ns and a setup time of 2 ns gives a 10 ns period and 100 MHz. A shortest path of 1 ns plus a minimum clock-to-Q of 1 ns clears a 1 ns hold time.

Pattern 6 — Design expressions from a next-state table

Assign the states, write the next-state table, convert each required transition into a flip-flop input through the excitation table, then minimise each input over the state bits and the machine inputs.

Worked case: the mod-6 counter of Example 7. With D flip-flops the input is the next state, which is why D design questions are the shortest; with JK the don't-cares make several minimal answers possible.

A timing-model note worth reading: GATE CSE 1991, Question 5(c)

That question asks for the maximum clock frequency of a three-flip-flop counter in which each flip-flop and each AND gate has a 10 ns propagation delay, with setup time negligible. Verified fact The question and its parameters are reproduced by several question-bank sources.

Two readings exist, and the difference is the lesson. Reading one: the critical path is the chain through the flip-flops, three delays, giving a 30 ns period. Reading two: only the flip-flop delays bound the period, four of them including the last stage's, giving 40 ns. Community observation Question-bank reproductions of the paper record 25 as the accepted value — that is a 40 ns period, one over four 10 ns delays — while community discussions of the same figure argue for 30 ns and 33.3 MHz. I did not read the original 1991 answer key, so I am not asserting which reading the examiners intended.

What to carry away: a delay question is only answerable once the model is stated — which edges count, whether the AND delays are inside the loop, and how many stages the last one has to wait for. When a paper leaves that ambiguous, the marks are in stating your model, not in the digit.

MCQ Original

Q1. Which statement correctly distinguishes a D latch from a positive-edge-triggered D flip-flop?

(A) The latch has no enable input, so it stores on every change of its data line.
(B) While enabled, the latch's output follows its data input; the flip-flop's output changes only at the clock edge.
(C) The flip-flop stores two bits, one for each clock level.
(D) The latch is built from NOR gates and the flip-flop from NAND gates.

Answer: (B). A latch is level-sensitive: while the enable is high the data path to the output is open, so the output follows the data, including glitches. A flip-flop is edge-triggered: it samples the data at the active edge and holds for the rest of the cycle.

Why the others fail. (A) is the opposite of the truth — the latch has an enable and the flip-flop is the one that ignores its data between edges. (C) invents storage; a flip-flop stores one bit. (D) is a real fact about some implementations but says nothing about behaviour, and both devices exist in both gate families.

Trap: a latch and a flip-flop made from the same two gates can look identical in a diagram. The difference is whether the enable is a level or an edge, and that is a property of the wiring, not of the symbol.

MCQ GATE CSE 2011

Q2. The minimum number of D flip-flops needed to design a mod-258 counter is:

(A) 9
(B) 8
(C) 512
(D) 258

Answer: (A) 9. A mod-N counter must be able to hold N distinct states, and n flip-flops hold at most $$2^n$$. Eight give 256, which is two short of 258; nine give 512. So $$n = \lceil\log_2 258\rceil = 9$$.

Why the others fail. 8 is the near miss the question is built around: 256 is close to 258 but not enough. 258 and 512 are the modulus and the next power of two — quantities that appear in the arithmetic but are not counts of flip-flops.

Shortcut with its assumption: $$\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$$ is exact for any N, and it does not depend on the flip-flop type. The same count holds for JK and T devices, because the number of states is a property of the encoding.

NAT GATE CSE 2025 Set 2

Q3. In a 4-bit ripple counter, if the period of the waveform at the last flip-flop is 64 microseconds, then the frequency of the ripple counter in kHz is ______.

Answer: a number.

Answer: 250. Each stage halves the frequency, so four stages divide by 16 and multiply the period by 16. The clock period is 64 µs ÷ 16 = 4 µs, and the frequency is 1 / 4 µs = 250 kHz.

Why the near misses happen. Dividing by 4 instead of 16 gives 16 µs and 62.5 kHz. Inverting 64 µs without dividing gives 15.625 kHz. Both are answers to different questions.

Trap: "the frequency of the ripple counter" is the frequency it is driven at, not the frequency it produces. The counter's job is to divide, so its output is the slower waveform.

MCQ GATE CSE 2015 Set 1

Q4. Consider a 4-bit Johnson counter with an initial value of 0000. The counting sequence of this counter is:

(A) 0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 14, 12, 8, 0
(B) 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 0
(C) 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 0
(D) 0, 8, 12, 14, 15, 7, 3, 1, 0

Answer: (D). The counter shifts right and feeds the complement of the last bit into the front. From 0000 that gives 1000, 1100, 1110, 1111, 0111, 0011, 0001 and back to 0000 — 0, 8, 12, 14, 15, 7, 3, 1, 0 in decimal.

Why the others fail. (A) is the same cycle read with the bit order reversed, which is the Johnson counter of the opposite convention — a genuine trap, not a random distractor. (B) and (C) are not cycles of this circuit at all: (C) is the even numbers, which no shift register produces.

Shortcut with its assumption: a Johnson counter has exactly 2n states, so the list must contain nine entries including the return. Every option does, so the length does not discriminate; the bit order does. State which end is the most significant before choosing.

MCQ GATE CSE 2017 Set 1

Q5. A T flip-flop and a D flip-flop are cross-connected: the D flip-flop's output feeds the T flip-flop's input, and the T flip-flop's output feeds the D flip-flop's input. Both outputs are initially 1 before the first clock. Then:

(A) Q1Q0 after the 3rd cycle is 11 and after the 4th is 00.
(B) Q1Q0 after the 3rd cycle is 11 and after the 4th is 01.
(C) Q1Q0 after the 3rd cycle is 00 and after the 4th is 11.
(D) Q1Q0 after the 3rd cycle is 01 and after the 4th is 01.

Answer: (B). The equations are $$Q_1^+ = Q_1 \oplus Q_0$$ and $$Q_0^+ = Q_1$$. From 11 the walk is 01, then 10, then 11, then 01: a three-state cycle. After the third clock the pair is 11 and after the fourth it is 01.

Why the others fail. (A) and (C) come from mis-writing one of the two equations — using AND instead of exclusive-OR for the T flip-flop, or forgetting that the D flip-flop's input is the other output rather than its own. (D) comes from starting the count one clock late.

Trap: the initial state is clock zero, not clock one. Write the clock number beside every row of the walk before reading off the answer; that single column prevents both (D) and most counting errors in this pattern.

MSQ Original

Q6. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) A hold-time violation cannot be repaired by reducing the clock frequency.
(B) In a synchronous counter, every flip-flop receives the same clock.
(C) A ring counter with n flip-flops has 2n distinct states.
(D) A mod-6 counter built from three flip-flops must use all eight encodings.

Answer: (A) and (B). Hold compares the shortest launch-plus-logic delay against the capture hold time; the clock period does not appear, so no frequency helps. In a synchronous counter the clock is distributed to every flip-flop and the toggle decision is made by combinational logic.

Why the others fail. (C) describes a Johnson counter; a ring counter with n flip-flops has n states in its cycle. (D) is false by counting: six states need three flip-flops, which give eight encodings, so two are unused.

Trap: (C) and (D) are the two confusions this lesson exists to prevent — ring against Johnson, and the number of encodings against the number of states. Both are single-word differences with different answers.

NAT Original

Q7. In a synchronous circuit, a flip-flop launches data into combinational logic whose longest path is 6 ns, and the data is captured by a second flip-flop. Both flip-flops have a clock-to-Q delay of 2 ns and a setup time of 2 ns; the hold time is 1 ns and the shortest logic path is 1 ns. The maximum clock frequency, in MHz, is ______.

Answer: a number.

Answer: 100. The setup constraint is one period ≥ clock-to-Q + longest logic + setup = 2 + 6 + 2 = 10 ns, so the maximum frequency is 1 / 10 ns = 100 MHz.

The hold check, which does not change the answer but must be done: the shortest arrival is 2 + 1 = 3 ns and the hold time is 1 ns, so hold is satisfied with 2 ns to spare. If it had failed, the circuit would be broken at every frequency and the answer would be "no frequency works", not a number.

Trap: adding the hold time into the period gives 11 ns and 90.9 MHz. Hold never enters the maximum-frequency calculation; it is a separate inequality with no period in it.

MCQ Original

Q8. A NOR-based SR latch is held with S = R = 1 for a long time, so both outputs read 0. Both inputs are then returned to 0 at exactly the same instant. What is the next state?

(A) Q = 1, because set was asserted most recently.
(B) Q = 0, because reset dominates in a NOR latch.
(C) Undefined: the outputs oscillate, and the value that settles depends on the relative gate delays.
(D) Q toggles once and then holds.

Answer: (C). With both inputs released together, the pair alternates between (1,1) and (0,0) and never reaches a fixed point. In real hardware one gate is marginally faster and wins, so the stored bit is unpredictable and not reproducible.

Why the others fail. (A) and (B) assume a priority that the circuit does not have — neither input was asserted "most recently", since both were held and both were released together. (D) describes a toggle, which is the JK behaviour and not the SR latch's.

The general lesson: when a question makes the outcome depend on gate delays without giving any, the correct answer is that it is undefined. This is the same discipline as the C lesson's undefined behaviour: do not invent a value for a program the standard does not define.

Definitions in one line each

  • Sequential circuit: output depends on present input and stored state.
  • Latch: level-sensitive storage; transparent while enabled.
  • Flip-flop: edge-triggered storage; samples once per active edge.
  • Setup time: data stable this long before the edge.
  • Hold time: data stable this long after the edge.
  • Clock-to-Q: edge to a stable output.
  • Race-around: a level-triggered JK toggling repeatedly while the clock is high.
  • Self-starting: every unused encoding reaches the cycle.
  • Moore: output from the state alone. Mealy: output from state and input.

The formulas that are actually used

SR (NOR): Q+ = S + R′Q, with SR = 0 required

D: Q+ = D     T: Q+ = Q ⊕ T

JK: Q+ = JQ′ + K′Q

n flip-flops for mod N: n = ⌈log2 N⌉

Ripple: fmax = 1 / (n·tpd)    last-stage period = 2n·Tclk

Setup: T ≥ tcq + tcomb,max + tsu

Hold: tcq,min + tcomb,min ≥ th

Ring: n states. Johnson: 2n states. Synchronous toggle: Tk = Q0Q1…Qk−1.

Excitation tables

Given the required transition, which input produces it:

D: D = Q+    T: T = Q ⊕ Q+

JK: 0→0: J=0,K=X   0→1: J=1,K=X   1→0: J=X,K=1   1→1: J=X,K=0

The four X entries are where multiple minimal JK designs come from. D has none, which is why D design questions have one answer and JK questions often have several.

The order of operations, and five quick questions

Order: name the state → write the next-state function → count the clock.

  1. How many states does the machine need, and how many flip-flops is that?
  2. Is the storage level-sensitive or edge-triggered?
  3. What is the slowest register-to-register path, and does hold pass?
  4. Where do the unused encodings go?
  5. Is the asked-for frequency the clock or the last stage?

The five traps, in one place

1. The forbidden SR input — S = R = 1 stores nothing, and releasing both races. 2. Race-around — a level-triggered JK with J = K = 1 toggles for as long as the clock is high, so the result depends on the clock width. 3. Hold mistaken for setup — hold has no clock period in it, so slowing down cannot fix it. 4. An uninitialised ring counter — all zeros is a fixed point, so the counter never starts. 5. An unused state that locks out — the don't-care that shrank the circuit is the same decision that decides where the spare encodings go.

Four of the five are caught by writing one extra line: the next state of the invalid encoding, the width of the clock, the shortest path, and the feedback from the all-zeros state.
