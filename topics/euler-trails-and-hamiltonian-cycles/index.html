Topic and objective

TOPIC: Euler trails and circuits, and Hamiltonian paths and cycles — the two questions that differ by one word, the criterion that decides the first, and the reasons no criterion is known for the second.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Tell an edge-rationing question from a vertex-rationing one, and say which object each one counts. Derive the parity condition from what a trail does at a vertex, and state the Euler criterion with both of its clauses, undirected and directed. Construct a tour with Hierholzer's walk-and-splice and say what it costs. Count the Euler circuits of a small graph exactly, and know why counting is not the exam question. Use Dirac's and Ore's theorems as certificates and never as tests, and give the counterexample that shows a failed hypothesis proves nothing. Prove that a particular graph is not Hamiltonian by exhausting its cycles, and recognise the Petersen graph.

I read this topic as one contrast rather than two topics: everything worth knowing here comes from the fact that one of the two questions has a one-line test and the other has none, and from the two ways a learner mixes them up. The two questions are taught side by side on purpose.

Prerequisites

  • Graph connectivity — walks, trails, paths, cycles, components, degrees, and the handshake lemma. The parity argument is one line of that lesson applied carefully.
  • Bipartite graphs — the same lesson proves that a graph is bipartite exactly when it has no odd cycle. That is the necessary condition used against K(2,3).
  • P, NP and NP-complete — what it means for a decision problem to be NP-complete, and what a reduction proves. The Hamiltonian half of this lesson rests on it.
  • Counting — factorials and the (n − 1)! / 2 count of cycles in a complete graph, which is the brute-force cost in the sixth scene.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • Edges or vertices. "Every edge once" is Euler; "every vertex once" is Hamilton. The wording fixes the criterion.
  • Parity is forced. A closed trail pairs every vertex's edges; an open one leaves exactly two odd endpoints.
  • Euler has a criterion, and it has two clauses. Even degrees and the edges in one component. Degrees alone are not enough.
  • Hamilton has no test. Deciding it is NP-complete; Dirac and Ore can certify a cycle and can never deny one.
  • Neither implies the other. K(2,3) has the trail and no cycle; a chorded six-cycle has the cycle and no trail.

1. Intuition — a postman and a salesman

A postman has to walk down every street in his round, and wants to finish where he started. His question is about streets: have I covered all of them, and can I do it without repeating one? A travelling salesman has to call on every customer, and wants to end where he began. His question is about customers: have I visited all of them, and can I do it without revisiting one? Educational interpretation

The two rounds feel like the same problem and are not. The postman's question has a one-line answer that needs only the degree of each junction: if every junction has an even number of streets and the streets form one connected network, the round exists. The salesman's question has no such answer, and if one were found it would settle the most famous open problem in computer science.

2. Formal definitions

Let G = (V, E) be a graph. A walk is a sequence of vertices where consecutive vertices are joined by an edge. A trail repeats no edge. A path repeats no vertex, and is therefore also a trail. A circuit is a trail that ends where it began; a cycle is a circuit that repeats no vertex except its endpoints.

An Euler trail is a trail that uses every edge of G exactly once. It is an Euler circuit (or Euler tour) if it is closed. A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex of G exactly once; it is a Hamiltonian cycle if it is closed.

Verified fact These are the standard definitions (West, Introduction to Graph Theory, 2nd ed.; Bondy and Murty, Graph Theory). Note what each one rations: the Euler objects ration edges and permit revisiting vertices; the Hamiltonian objects ration vertices and permit ignoring edges.

3. Visual understanding — what the drawing has to show

An Euler trail is a walk, so it is drawn as a path that covers every edge, and the eye can check it: each edge is painted once, and a vertex may be crossed many times. A Hamiltonian cycle is a selection, so it is drawn as a closed chain through the vertices, with the edges it did not choose left visible and unused.

That difference is why the animation tab paints edges rather than moving a token around: the object being rationed is the thing that has to be counted on screen. Every figure in the animation is drawn from the edge list computed in code, never from the picture, because a crossing in a drawing is not a vertex and a long edge is still one step.

4. Worked example — the seven bridges

Königsberg in 1736: four land masses joined by seven bridges. Model them as four vertices with degrees 3, 3, 3 and 5, and the page computes exactly that from the edge list. A trail pairs the edges at every vertex except at most two, so at most two vertices may be odd. Four are, so no walk crosses each bridge once, and a brute-force search over every ordering of the seven edges confirms it.

Euler's argument is one paragraph, and it is a proof: nothing in it depends on the particular bridges. That is the difference between this question and the Hamiltonian one, where the same paragraph does not exist.

5. Boundaries — the hypotheses that make the criterion true

The Euler criterion needs both clauses. Even degrees with two components gives nothing, as two disjoint triangles show. And the connectivity clause is about the vertices that carry edges: isolated vertices are ignored, which is why a graph plus three isolated vertices is still Eulerian.

The Hamiltonian theorems need their own hypotheses. Dirac needs n ≥ 3 and minimum degree at least n/2. Ore needs every non-adjacent pair to have degree sum at least n. Both are one-directional: they certify existence and say nothing when their hypothesis fails.

6. Edge cases and the two counterexamples to memorise

The chorded six-cycle (a hexagon plus the chords 0–2 and 3–5) has degrees 3, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3 — four odd — so no Euler trail, and the hexagon itself is a Hamiltonian cycle. The complete bipartite graph K(2,3) has degrees 3, 3, 2, 2, 2 — two odd — so it has an Euler trail, and it has no Hamiltonian cycle because its two sides have different sizes.

Together they show the two properties are independent in both directions. Neither is a special case of the other, and neither criterion may be used for the other question.

7. GATE application — what the question looks like

Euler questions are decided: "which of these has an Eulerian circuit", "how many odd-degree vertices", "which graph has an Euler path but not a circuit". The work is a degree count plus one connectivity check, and the marks are lost on the connectivity clause.

Hamiltonian questions are usually about counting (how many Hamiltonian cycles in K(n)), about complexity (is deciding it NP-complete), or about sufficient conditions. The counting question has a factorial answer, and the complexity question is answered by naming the class, not by searching.

Example 1 — the seven bridges, decided twice

Graph: vertices N, S, I, E; edges N–I, N–E, N–E, S–I, S–E, S–E, I–E. Seven edges, and the degrees computed from that list are 3, 3, 3, 5.

Odd vertices: four. A trail allows at most two. So no Euler trail, and no Euler circuit either — a circuit is a special case. The page also searches every ordering of the seven edges and finds nothing, so the criterion and the brute force agree.

The repair that would work. Remove one bridge between N and E and the degrees become 2, 3, 3, 4: two odd vertices, and a trail exists between them. Add a bridge between N and S instead and every degree becomes even: a circuit exists.

Example 2 — K(2,3), the trail without the cycle

Two vertices on one side (a, b) and three on the other (p, q, r), every cross pair joined. Degrees 3, 3, 2, 2, 2 — two odd, so an Euler trail exists between a and b, and the animation draws it: a, p, b, q, a, r, b.

No Hamiltonian cycle: a cycle in a bipartite graph alternates sides, so it needs the two sides to be the same size, and 2 is not 3. A Hamiltonian path does exist — p, a, q, b, r — which is the distinction a question tests when it says "path" rather than "cycle".

Example 3 — Hierholzer on the bowtie, step by step

The bowtie is two triangles sharing vertex 1: edges 0–1, 1–2, 2–0, 1–3, 3–4, 4–1. Degrees 2, 4, 2, 2, 2: all even, one component, so a circuit exists.

Walk from 0: 0–1, then 1–2, then 2–0. At 0 there is no unused edge, so the first walk closes after three edges and leaves 1–3, 3–4, 4–1 behind. Vertex 1 is on the tour and still has unused edges, so walk its cycle 1–3–4–1 and splice it in at vertex 1.

Result: 0, 1, 3, 4, 1, 2, 0. Six edges, each once, closed. The page checks both properties rather than trusting the drawing.

Example 4 — counting the tours of K(5)

K(5) has ten edges and every degree 4. Every Euler circuit passes vertex 0 exactly 4/2 = 2 times, and each circuit can be walked in two directions, so the number of traversals that start at vertex 0 is 4 times the number of circuits.

Counting traversals from 0 by exhaustive search gives 528. Counting distinct circuits directly — canonicalising each cyclic edge sequence over rotation and reversal — gives 132. And 528 = 4 × 132, so the two independent counts agree.

Why 132 and not 528. A circuit has no first edge. Rotating it or reversing it is the same circuit, and a count that forgets this is four times too large on K(5) — the standard counting error.

Example 5 — Dirac and Ore on two six-vertex graphs

K(6) minus a perfect matching: each vertex loses exactly one edge, so every degree is 4. With n = 6, n/2 = 3, and 4 ≥ 3, so Dirac applies and a Hamiltonian cycle exists. The page finds one.

The six-cycle: every degree is 2, which is below 3, so Dirac fails; two non-adjacent vertices have degree sum 4, which is below 6, so Ore fails too. The graph is Hamiltonian anyway — it is a cycle. So neither hypothesis is necessary, and failing one is not evidence against a cycle.

Example 6 — the Petersen graph, three searches

Ten vertices, fifteen edges, every degree 3, girth 5. Searching every cycle from vertex 0 finds none, so it is not Hamiltonian. Repeating the search ten times, once with each vertex deleted, finds a cycle every time: the graph is hypohamiltonian, and the Petersen graph is the smallest example.

Every degree is 3, so all ten vertices are odd, and a trail allows two: no Euler trail either. Two "no" answers, from two unrelated arguments — a parity count that takes linear time, and a search that does not.

The graph with no edges

A graph with no edges has a trivial Euler circuit (the empty trail) and, by the usual convention, no Euler trail in the sense a question means. The page states the convention instead of hiding it: it excludes the empty graph from its exhaustive comparison, because the two readings differ there and nowhere else. On any graph with at least one edge, the criterion and the brute-force search agreed on all 1,342 graphs checked.

One vertex, and two vertices

One vertex with a loop is Eulerian; one vertex without edges is the empty case above. Two vertices joined by two parallel edges: both degrees are 2, so a circuit exists (there and back). Two vertices joined by one edge: both degrees are 1, so there is a trail and no circuit. Dirac's theorem excludes n = 2 for exactly this reason — its hypothesis is vacuous there and its conclusion is not a cycle.

Parallel edges and loops

The criterion is about degrees, so parallel edges are counted with multiplicity and change nothing in the statement: Königsberg needs them. A loop contributes 2 to the degree of its vertex, which is why a graph of loops alone is even and Eulerian. A drawing cannot show multiplicity clearly, which is the practical reason to work from the edge list.

Disconnected graphs, and isolated vertices

Two disjoint triangles have all degrees even and no tour, because no walk can leave a component. Isolated vertices are ignored: the criterion is about the vertices that carry edges. A question that says "connected" and a question that says "all vertices of nonzero degree in one component" mean the same thing here, and both are needed for the tour.

Directed graphs

Balance is counted per direction: in-degree equals out-degree at every vertex. For a trail, one vertex may have out-degree one greater (the start) and one may have in-degree one greater (the end). Connectivity is required on the underlying undirected graph, and balance plus that weak connectivity already forces strong connectivity — the condensation of a balanced digraph is a one-node DAG. All 63 directed graphs on three vertices agree with a brute-force search.

Zero odd vertices versus two

Zero odd vertices gives a circuit, and any vertex may be the start. Two odd vertices give a trail that is not closed, and the two odd vertices are the only legal endpoints. Four or more gives neither. The number of odd vertices is always even, because the degrees sum to twice the edge count, so "three odd vertices" is never a real case.

Hamiltonian path without a cycle

K(2,3) has a Hamiltonian path and no Hamiltonian cycle; so does any bipartite graph whose sides differ by exactly one. A path is the weaker demand, and a question that says path cannot be answered with a cycle criterion. The Petersen graph has neither — deleting one vertex leaves a cycle, which shows how far apart the two questions can be.

Bipartite balance, and the component condition

A Hamiltonian cycle in a bipartite graph uses the two sides alternately, so the sides must be equal: K(2,3) and the Herschel graph both fail for that reason. Deleting a set S leaves at most |S| components, because a cycle passes through S at most |S| times. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient, and the Petersen graph satisfies the second and is not Hamiltonian — so the condition cannot decide either.

1. "Euler means visiting every vertex"

Why it feels right: both questions are introduced with the same sentence about "covering the graph".

Counterexample: K(2,3) has an Euler trail that visits a, p, b, q, a, r, b — vertex a twice — and it has no Hamiltonian cycle at all. Repair: name the object the question rations before choosing a criterion.

Cost: this is the single most common wrong answer on the topic, and it is invisible in the arithmetic because the numbers are all correct.

2. "All degrees even means an Euler circuit"

Why it feels right: it is the clause that gets all the attention, and it is the one Euler is remembered for.

Counterexample: two disjoint triangles: six vertices of degree 2, no tour. Repair: the criterion has two clauses, and the second one is connectivity of the edge-carrying part.

Where it is tested: GATE CSE 2007 Question 23 option A is exactly this mistake, stated about "any" k-regular graph with k even.

3. "A failed Dirac check means no Hamiltonian cycle"

Why it feels right: the theorem is remembered as an equivalence because the Euler criterion is one.

Counterexample: the six-cycle fails Dirac and Ore and is Hamiltonian. Repair: a sufficient condition proves existence; only its contrapositive-free form is available, so failure proves nothing.

Sharper: if a simple necessary-and-sufficient test existed, deciding Hamiltonicity would not be NP-complete.

4. "A tour may not revisit a vertex"

Why it feels right: "path" and "trail" sound alike, and a path may not revisit a vertex.

Counterexample: the bowtie's tour is 0, 1, 3, 4, 1, 2, 0 — vertex 1 twice, and it is a valid Euler circuit. Repair: an Euler object rations edges. Revisiting a vertex is allowed and often unavoidable: a vertex of degree 2k is passed k times.

Useful count: on a tour, each vertex is passed exactly half its degree times.

5. "Fleury's algorithm is linear"

Why it feels right: it is presented next to Hierholzer's, and both produce a tour.

Counterexample: Fleury tests whether the next edge is a bridge at every step, and that test is a search. Repair: Hierholzer is O(V + E); Fleury is not, because of the repeated bridge tests.

GATE relevance: questions about the cost of constructing a tour distinguish the two.

6. "Counting circuits is what the criterion is for"

Why it feels right: both are about Euler circuits, so they look like one topic.

Counterexample: K(5) has 132 circuits and the criterion answers only "at least one". Repair: existence has a linear test; counting does not, and the count has its own formula (the BEST theorem for directed graphs) which is outside this lesson.

Counting trap: rotation and reversal are the same circuit, so a naive sequence count is 4 times too large on K(5).

7. "A drawing shows whether a cycle exists"

Why it feels right: the Petersen graph looks like it has a big cycle around the outside, and a crossing looks like a vertex.

Counterexample: the outer pentagon of the Petersen drawing is a 5-cycle, not a Hamiltonian cycle, and the crossings are not vertices. Repair: work from the edge list; the page computes every verdict from it.

Related trap: a long edge is one step in a graph, and a short one is also one step.

8. "If it is not Hamiltonian it cannot be Eulerian"

Why it feels right: both sound like "the graph is hard to cover".

Counterexample: the bowtie is Eulerian and not Hamiltonian; K(2,3) is not Hamiltonian and Eulerian; the Petersen graph is neither; K(4) is both. Repair: the two properties are independent, and all four combinations occur.

Structural link: the line graph L(G) is Hamiltonian exactly when G is Eulerian, which is the only tight relation between the two.

GATE CSE 2007, Question 23 — read, then recomputed

"Which of the following graphs has an Eulerian circuit? (A) Any k-regular graph where k is an even number. (B) A complete graph on 90 vertices. (C) The complement of a cycle on 25 vertices. (D) None of the above."

(A) fails. Even degree is one clause. Two disjoint triangles are 2-regular, every degree is even, and no single walk covers both. The word "any" is what makes the option false, and one counterexample is enough.

(B) fails. K(90) is connected and every vertex has degree 89, which is odd — so it has not even an Euler trail. Connectivity cannot repair parity.

(C) holds. The complement of a 25-cycle joins each vertex to all but itself and its two cycle neighbours, so each degree is 25 − 1 − 2 = 22, and 22 is even. It is also connected: two cycle neighbours have a common non-neighbour in the complement, which joins them by a two-edge path. The page checks the degree and the connectivity directly on the 25 vertices, and reports 275 edges.

Answer: C. The marks are in the second clause: without the connectivity argument, (C) is not yet established. Verified fact Statement, options and answer read from GATE Overflow's reproduction of the paper and from IIT Delhi's published solutions page, both of which give Question 23 and answer (C). The verdicts were recomputed here rather than copied.

GATE CSE 2019 — counting cycles in a complete graph

"Let G be an undirected complete graph on n vertices, where n > 2. Then the number of different Hamiltonian cycles in G is equal to (n)!, (n−1)!, 1, or (n−1)!/2."

The answer is (n − 1)!/2: fix a starting vertex, order the rest, and divide by two because a cycle and its reversal are the same cycle. The page verifies the formula against exhaustive enumeration for n from 3 to 7 (1, 3, 12, 60, 360). The community discussion around this question argues about whether the vertices are labelled; with labelled vertices the formula is the answer, and that is the reading the official key takes. Community observation The question number differs between archives, so only the year is stated.

The four question patterns

  • Decide existence. "Which of these has an Eulerian circuit?" Count odd degrees, then check one component. Two clauses, both required.
  • Count something. Hamiltonian cycles in K(n) give (n−1)!/2; Euler circuits need the rotation-and-reversal correction. Counting is never answered by the criterion.
  • Classify complexity. "Deciding Hamiltonian cycle is NP-complete" is answered by naming the class and, if asked, giving the certificate check that puts it in NP.
  • Apply a sufficient condition. Compute the minimum degree or the degree sums, state which theorem applies, and state its hypothesis before using it.

The procedure, and where each step is tested

  1. Read which object is rationed: edges or vertices.
  2. Edge question: count odd degrees — 0 circuit, 2 trail, more nothing.
  3. Edge question: check the edges lie in one component. This is the step that decides option A and option C in the 2007 question.
  4. Vertex question: try Dirac (δ ≥ n/2), then Ore (degree sums on non-adjacent pairs). A certificate ends the question.
  5. Vertex question: if no certificate, look for a structural obstruction — unequal bipartite sides, or a cut set S with more than |S| components — and only then report that no cycle was found.

MCQGATE CSE 2007 Q23

Which of the following graphs has an Eulerian circuit?

  1. Any k-regular graph where k is an even number.
  2. A complete graph on 90 vertices.
  3. The complement of a cycle on 25 vertices.
  4. None of the above.

Answer: C

The complement of a 25-cycle gives every vertex 25 − 1 − 2 = 22 edges, which is even, and it is connected, so the two-clause criterion holds. The page computes 275 edges, degree 22 at every vertex, and connectivity, on the 25 vertices.

Why A fails. Two disjoint triangles are 2-regular with every degree even and no Euler circuit: connectivity is missing. Why B fails. K(90) has degree 89 at every vertex, which is odd, so it has not even an Euler trail. Why D fails. One option holds.

Shortcut and its assumption. "Even degrees plus connected" is a complete test, so once both are checked no search is needed. The assumption is that the graph has at least one edge, which it does.

NATGATE CSE 2019

Let G be an undirected complete graph on n vertices, where n > 2. The number of different Hamiltonian cycles in G is

  1. n!
  2. (n − 1)!
  3. 1
  4. (n − 1)! / 2

Answer: D — (n − 1)! / 2

Fix a starting vertex. The remaining n − 1 vertices can be ordered in (n − 1)! ways, and each ordering gives a closed walk through all vertices. A cycle and its reversal are the same cycle, so divide by two.

Why A and B fail. They forget that a cycle has no first vertex: rotating the same cycle gives (n − 1)!/2 different sequences. Why C fails. It is right only for n = 3. The page verifies the formula by exhaustive enumeration: 1, 3, 12, 60 and 360 for n = 3 to 7.

Assumption to state. The formula counts cycles in a labelled complete graph. With unlabelled vertices there is exactly one cycle up to isomorphism, which is why the option set includes 1.

MCQOriginal practice question

A connected graph has exactly four vertices of odd degree. Which one of the following is true?

  1. It has an Euler circuit but no Euler trail.
  2. It has an Euler trail but no Euler circuit.
  3. It has neither an Euler trail nor an Euler circuit.
  4. It must be non-Hamiltonian.

Answer: C

A closed trail pairs every vertex's edges, so it needs zero odd vertices; an open trail leaves one unpaired edge at each of exactly two vertices, so it needs exactly two. Four odd vertices exceeds both allowances, and connectivity cannot help: it is the parity that fails.

Why D fails. Parity says nothing about Hamiltonian cycles. K(4) with one edge subdivided into a path of length two has four odd vertices and is Hamiltonian; the chorded six-cycle in this lesson has four odd vertices and a Hamiltonian cycle.

Edge case. The number of odd vertices is always even, so three odd vertices is not a case that can occur — a question that offers it is testing the handshake lemma.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements are true for every graph G with at least one edge?

  1. If G has an Euler circuit then every vertex of G has even degree.
  2. If every vertex of G has even degree then G has an Euler circuit.
  3. If G has a Hamiltonian cycle then G is connected.
  4. If G is connected and every degree is at least 2 then G has a Hamiltonian cycle.

Answer: A and C

A is the necessity half, proved by pairing the edges at each vertex. C holds because a Hamiltonian cycle is itself a spanning connected subgraph.

Why B fails. Two disjoint triangles: all degrees even, no Euler circuit, because the edges are in two components. Why D fails. The Petersen graph is connected and 3-regular, so every degree is at least 2, and it has no Hamiltonian cycle — the page establishes that by exhausting every cycle.

Distinction being tested. B and D are the two clauses of the Euler criterion collapsed into one, and a sufficient condition read as a test. Both are the standard errors.

NATOriginal practice question

The complete graph K(6) has 60 Hamiltonian cycles. How many distinct Euler circuits does K(5) have, counting a circuit once up to rotation and reversal?

Answer: 132

K(5) has ten edges and every degree 4, so it is Eulerian. Counting distinct circuits by canonicalising each cyclic edge sequence over rotation and reversal gives 132. The independent count of traversals that begin at vertex 0 gives 528, and 528 / (2 directions × 2 visits to vertex 0) = 132, so the two counts agree.

Why 528 appears. It is the traversal count from a fixed start, which is not the question. Why 264 appears. It corrects for direction only and not for the two visits a tour makes to vertex 0. Why 10! appears. It counts every ordering of the edges, ignoring that most orderings are not walks.

Not needed for GATE. The exam asks whether a circuit exists. The count is included because the counting error is the one that costs marks on the K(n) Hamiltonian question.

MCQOriginal practice question

Which one of the following statements about deciding Hamiltonian cycles is correct?

  1. Dirac's theorem decides the question for every graph.
  2. The problem is NP-complete, and a proposed cycle can be verified in polynomial time.
  3. The problem is undecidable.
  4. Ore's theorem is a necessary and sufficient condition.

Answer: B

Karp listed the directed and the undirected Hamiltonian circuit among the twenty-one problems he proved NP-complete in 1972. A proposed cycle is checked by walking it and testing each consecutive pair plus the closing edge, which is linear, so the problem is in NP.

Why A and D fail. Both theorems are sufficient only: the six-cycle fails both hypotheses and is Hamiltonian. Why C fails. The question is decidable by exhaustive search; it is the running time that is the obstacle, not the existence of an answer.

Trap. "Exponential in practice" is not "undecidable". GATE distinguishes the two, and the distinction is the whole point of the complexity classes lesson.

MCQOriginal practice question

A connected graph on 6 vertices has all degrees even and 9 edges. Which one of the following is true?

  1. It has an Euler circuit, and it has at least 10 edges after any Hamiltonian cycle is removed.
  2. It has an Euler circuit, and removing any Hamiltonian cycle leaves 3 edges.
  3. It has no Euler circuit, because 9 is odd.
  4. It cannot be Hamiltonian, because it has an odd number of edges.

Answer: B

Connected with every degree even gives an Euler circuit, whatever the edge count — it is the degrees that must be even, not the number of edges. A Hamiltonian cycle on 6 vertices uses exactly 6 edges, and 9 − 6 = 3.

Why A fails. It keeps the criterion but adds a false arithmetic claim. Why C fails. It confuses the parity of the edge count with the parity of the degrees; the handshake lemma makes the edge count's parity irrelevant to the criterion. Why D fails. A Hamiltonian cycle uses n edges, so a graph with an odd edge count can be Hamiltonian whenever n is odd or when extra edges are present.

Consistency check worth doing. Six vertices with all even degrees and 9 edges is realisable: a 6-cycle (6 edges) plus a triangle among three of its vertices (3 edges), giving degrees 4, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following are necessary conditions for a graph to have a Hamiltonian cycle?

  1. Every vertex has degree at least 2.
  2. For every set S of vertices, G − S has at most |S| components.
  3. If G is bipartite, its two sides have equal size.
  4. G has an Euler circuit.

Answer: A, B and C

A holds because a Hamiltonian cycle uses exactly two edges at each vertex, so the degree is at least 2. B holds because a cycle enters and leaves S at most |S| times, so deleting S breaks it into at most |S| pieces — the page checks it on all 228 Hamiltonian graphs with at most five vertices. C holds because a cycle alternates sides.

Why D fails. The bowtie is Eulerian and not Hamiltonian; K(4) is both; K(2,3) is neither. The two properties are independent.

None of A, B or C is sufficient. The Petersen graph satisfies B and is not Hamiltonian, which is exactly why B cannot be used to decide.

The two criteria, side by side

Euler circuit exists ⟺ every degree is even and the vertices with nonzero degree lie in one component. Euler trail exists ⟺ exactly zero or two vertices are odd and the same component condition. Directed: in-degree equals out-degree everywhere (for a trail, one vertex one greater out, one one greater in) plus one weak component on the arcs. Verified fact These are Euler's theorem (1736), with the first complete proof of sufficiency published by Hierholzer in 1873.

Hamiltonian cycle: no criterion is known, and deciding it is NP-complete. Certificates: Dirac (n ≥ 3, δ ≥ n/2) and Ore (deg u + deg v ≥ n for every non-adjacent pair). Necessary conditions: degree at least 2, equal bipartite sides, and c(G − S) ≤ |S| for every S. Algorithms: Hierholzer for Euler in O(V + E); backtracking for Hamilton, exponential in the worst case.

Numbers worth carrying in

  • Königsberg: 4 vertices, 7 edges, degrees 3, 3, 3, 5 — four odd, so no trail.
  • K(2,3): degrees 3, 3, 2, 2, 2 — Euler trail, Hamiltonian path, no Hamiltonian cycle.
  • Chorded six-cycle: degrees 3, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3 — Hamiltonian cycle, no Euler trail.
  • Bowtie: degrees 2, 4, 2, 2, 2 — Euler circuit 0-1-3-4-1-2-0, and not Hamiltonian.
  • K(5): 10 edges, degree 4, 132 Euler circuits, 12 Hamiltonian cycles.
  • K(n): (n − 1)!/2 Hamiltonian cycles; 181,440 at n = 10.
  • Petersen: 10 vertices, 15 edges, degree 3, non-Hamiltonian, hypohamiltonian, no Euler trail.
  • Complement of C(25): degree 22, 275 edges, connected, Eulerian.

Five traps, and the defence

  • Answering one question with the other's rule. Name the object the question rations, out loud, before writing anything.
  • Even degrees without connectivity. Two disjoint triangles is the counterexample; the second clause is not optional.
  • A failed Dirac or Ore check reported as "no cycle". The six-cycle refutes the inference, and the Petersen graph shows how badly it can fail.
  • Counting sequences instead of circuits. A circuit has no first edge: divide by the rotations and the two directions.
  • Reading the drawing. A crossing is not a vertex, and multiplicity is invisible. Work from the degree list.

Five questions with answers

  1. Does an Euler circuit allow revisiting a vertex? Yes — only edges are rationed; a vertex of degree 2k is passed k times.
  2. Is "all degrees even" enough? No. Connectedness of the edge-carrying part is the second clause.
  3. Can a graph have an Euler trail and no Hamiltonian cycle? Yes — K(2,3).
  4. Can Dirac's hypothesis fail on a Hamiltonian graph? Yes — every cycle of length at least 5.
  5. How is "this graph is not Hamiltonian" established? By exhausting the cycles, or by a structural obstruction — never by a failed certificate.

The one-sentence version

Euler asks whether the edges can be laid end to end and is answered by a parity count plus one connectivity check; Hamilton asks whether the vertices can be laid end to end and has no such answer, only certificates and obstructions — so name the object first, then the hypothesis of whatever theorem you are about to use.
