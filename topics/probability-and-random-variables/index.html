Topic and objective

TOPIC: Probability, random variables and distributions — the Probability and Statistics block of GATE 2027 Section 1, Engineering Mathematics.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read a probability as a count of equally likely outcomes, then as a measure obeying three axioms. Compute a conditional probability by shrinking the sample space, and apply Bayes' theorem with a partition. Treat a random variable as a function, and read one distribution three ways — as a mass table, as a cumulative staircase, and as a density with area. Compute expectations and variances from definitions rather than from memory. Recognise and use the five distributions the syllabus names, with their means and variances, and know which assumption each one needs. Finally, tell mean, median and mode apart on skewed data.

I read this clause as one question asked five ways: what can happen, how likely is it given something else, what number summarises it, how far from that number does it stray, and which named family describes it. Every GATE item in this clause is one of those five, evaluated on small numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Counting and Combinatorics — the binomial coefficient and the multiplication rule, which is all that the binomial PMF and most counting arguments need.
  • Sets and Set Operations — events are sets, and union, intersection and complement are the operations you will use on them.
  • Arithmetic you already have: fractions, powers, factorials, and taking a square root.

One piece of calculus appears and only in the continuous half: the area under a curve. It is used as an area, with a closed form given each time, so no integration technique is assumed.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • Count, then divide. With equally likely outcomes, a probability is a count over the size of the space.
  • Conditioning shrinks the space. P(A | B) divides the overlap by the conditioning event, never by the whole space.
  • Expectation is a balance point. Weight each value by its probability; linearity holds always, the product rule does not.
  • Variance is spread. E[X squared] minus the square of E[X]; a shift adds nothing and a scale squares.
  • Five families, five signatures. Binomial counts successes, Poisson counts arrivals, exponential waits, uniform is flat, normal is the bell.

Intuition first: a bag of thirty-six slips

Two dice are thrown. Write every possible pair on a slip of paper — one-one, one-two, and so on up to six-six — and put all thirty-six slips in a bag. Drawing one slip at random is the same experiment. Now a probability question becomes a question about the bag: how many slips carry the property you care about?

Conditioning is then the act of being told something and throwing slips away. If you are told the sum exceeded nine, every slip whose sum is nine or less leaves the bag, and six remain. The probability of anything else is now a count inside those six. Educational interpretation The bag carries the structure — a finite set, equally likely, reduced by information — but not the continuous half of the lesson, where the bag is replaced by an area.

Formal vocabulary, every symbol in words

Sample space S — the set of every outcome that can occur. For two dice, 36 ordered pairs.

Event — a subset of S. "The sum is seven" is the set of six pairs that give seven.

P(E) — the probability of the event E, a number between 0 and 1.

A and B, written $$A \cap B$$ — the overlap: outcomes in both.

A or B, written $$A \cup B$$ — outcomes in at least one.

Complement $$A'$$ — every outcome not in A. So $$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$.

Random variable X — a function that assigns a real number to each outcome. Capital X is the function; small x is a value it can take.

The three axioms, and what follows

Verified fact Kolmogorov's axioms: (1) $$P(S) = 1$$; (2) $$P(E) \ge 0$$ for every event; (3) for disjoint events, $$P(E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \cdots) = P(E_1) + P(E_2) + \cdots$$.

Everything else is a consequence. From (1) and (3): $$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$. From (3) applied twice: the addition rule for two overlapping events,

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)Subtract the overlap because it was counted in both terms. When the events are disjoint the overlap is zero and the subtraction disappears.

Mathematical derivation Both identities are derived on this page from the axioms, and the arithmetic is re-checked in code on the GATE 2024 Set 1 numbers.

Conditional probability and independence

Definition. For $$P(B) > 0$$,

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)Read as: the probability of A computed inside the world where B is known to hold. The numerator is the overlap; the denominator is the whole of the new world.

Multiplication rule. Rearranged, $$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B)$$, which chains: $$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B \mid A)P(C \mid A \cap B)$$.

Independence. A and B are independent exactly when $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$, equivalently $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$ when the conditional is defined. Independence is an equation you check, not a feeling about the story.

Bayes' theorem and total probability

If $$B_1, \dots, B_n$$ partition the sample space — disjoint, and together covering everything — then

P(B_i | A) = P(A | B_i) P(B_i) / Σ_j P(A | B_j) P(B_j)The numerator is the one path you want. The denominator sums that path over every possible cause, which is the law of total probability for P(A).

The prior is $$P(B_i)$$, the likelihood is $$P(A \mid B_i)$$, the evidence is the denominator, and the posterior is the result.

Two-part case, which is what almost every exam question uses: with a cause and its complement, the denominator is just the sum of two terms.

PMF, CDF and density

Discrete. The probability mass function gives each value its probability: $$p(x) = P(X = x)$$, with $$p(x) \ge 0$$ and $$\sum_x p(x) = 1$$.

Continuous. A density $$f$$ gives probability as area: $$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f$$, with $$f \ge 0$$ and total area 1. The density may exceed 1; a probability may not.

Both. The cumulative distribution function $$F(x) = P(X \le x)$$ never decreases, tends to 0 on the left and 1 on the right, and is a staircase for a discrete variable and smooth for a continuous one.

Consequence. For a continuous X, $$P(X = a) = 0$$ for every a, so "at most" and "less than" give the same answer. For a discrete X they differ by the mass at that point.

Expectation and variance

Expectation, discrete and continuous:

E[X] = Σ x p(x) = ∫ x f(x) dxA weighted average of the values, weighted by probability. For a function of X, E[g(X)] = Σ g(x) p(x): apply g to each value first, then weight.

Linearity. $$E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$$ and $$E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$$ for any X and Y. Products need independence: $$E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$$ only then.

Variance. $$Var(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2$$, with $$\mu = E[X]$$. Standard deviation is its square root, and $$Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X)$$.

Verified fact These are the standard definitions as given in CLRS Appendix C.3 and in any first probability text; the second variance identity is the expansion of the square, derived on this page.

The five families the syllabus names, with the assumption each needs

Uniform on [a, b]. Density $$1/(b-a)$$. Mean $$(a+b)/2$$, variance $$(b-a)^2/12$$. Assumption: every value in the interval is equally likely — flat density.

Binomial(n, p). $$P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$. Mean $$np$$, variance $$np(1-p)$$. Assumption: n independent trials with the same p every time.

Poisson(λ). $$P(X=k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$$. Mean = variance = $$\lambda$$. Assumption: independent events at a constant rate in a fixed interval.

Exponential(λ). Density $$\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$$ for $$x \ge 0$$. Mean $$1/\lambda$$, variance $$1/\lambda^2$$. Assumption: constant rate, and it is the only common continuous family that is memoryless.

Normal(μ, σ²). Density $$\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}$$. Mean $$\mu$$, variance $$\sigma^2$$. Symmetric, so mean = median = mode.

Geometric(p), worth knowing although not named: the waiting time for the first success in Bernoulli trials. Mean $$1/p$$, variance $$(1-p)/p^2$$, and it is the discrete memoryless family.

Example 1 — a conditional probability, one step

Two fair dice are thrown. Given that the sum exceeds nine, what is the probability that the sum is eleven?

  1. The space has 36 equally likely outcomes. Two dice, six faces each, ordered pairs.
  2. "Sum exceeds 9" holds in 6 of them: three ways to make 10, two for 11, one for 12. Count, do not assume the sums are equally likely.
  3. "Sum is 11" holds in 2 of those 6: five-six and six-five. The conditioning event is now the whole world.
  4. Answer: 2/6 = 1/3. P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) = (2/36)/(6/36).

Trap. The unconditional probability of an eleven is 2/36 = 1/18. Conditioning raised it to 1/3, because the denominator shrank by a factor of six and the numerator did not.

Example 2 — GATE 2021 Set 1 Q54, Bayes with two paths

A sender transmits H with probability 0.1 and L with probability 0.9. Given H was sent, H is received with probability 0.3; given L was sent, H is received with probability 0.8. If H is received, what is the probability that H was sent?

  1. Path one: H sent and H received = 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.03. Multiplication rule along one branch.
  2. Path two: L sent and H received = 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72. The second way the evidence can happen.
  3. Evidence: P(H received) = 0.03 + 0.72 = 0.75. Total probability over a partition of two.
  4. Posterior: 0.03 / 0.75 = 0.04. The path you want, over all paths.

Why 0.3 is wrong. That is the likelihood, which the question gave you. Why 0.04 surprises people. The prior was 0.1, so the evidence moved the probability down: a corrupted L produces an H far more often than an H survives as an H.

Example 3 — GATE 2026 Set 1 Q48, an expectation from a table

X takes the values 1 to 8, with P(1) = P(2) = P(5) = P(7) = 1/6 and P(3) = P(4) = P(6) = P(8) = 1/12. Find E[X].

  1. Check the table: 4 × 1/6 + 4 × 1/12 = 2/3 + 1/3 = 1. A legal PMF, so the question is well posed.
  2. Weighted sum of the heavy values: (1 + 2 + 5 + 7)/6 = 15/6 = 2.5. Each value times its own probability.
  3. Weighted sum of the light values: (3 + 4 + 6 + 8)/12 = 21/12 = 1.75.
  4. E[X] = 2.5 + 1.75 = 4.25 = 17/4.

Why 4.5 is wrong. That is the plain average of 1 to 8, which assumes the eight values are equally likely. They are not, and the heavier weights sit on the smaller values, which is why the mean falls below 4.5.

Example 4 — GATE 2026 Set 2 Q4, reading a normal density

A real random variable has density $$f(x) = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-x^2/18)$$ for all real x. What are its mean and variance?

  1. Write the standard form: $$\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2)$$. Match term by term, not by pattern recognition alone.
  2. No shift inside the exponent, so $$\mu = 0$$.
  3. The exponent's denominator is $$2\sigma^2 = 18$$, so $$\sigma^2 = 9$$ and $$\sigma = 3$$. The denominator is twice the variance.
  4. The constant outside is $$1/(3\sqrt{2\pi})$$, which confirms $$\sigma = 3$$.

Why 18 is not the variance. The exponent is $$-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$$. Reading the denominator as the variance gives 18 and every option built on it is wrong. The constant in front is the check that catches this.

Example 5 — GATE 2024 Set 1 Q53, sampling without replacement

A bag holds 10 red and 15 blue balls. Two are drawn at random without replacement. Given that the first is red, what is the probability that the second is blue?

  1. After a red ball is drawn, 24 balls remain. Without replacement, so the total drops by one.
  2. All 15 blue balls are still there. A red was removed, so the blue count is untouched.
  3. Answer: 15/24 = 0.625.

Why 15/25 = 0.6 is wrong. That is the unconditional probability that the first ball is blue. Conditioning on a red having been drawn changes the denominator, which is the whole content of the question.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Each card states the condition a rule needs, and what breaks when the condition fails. These are the cases that separate a correct answer from an almost-correct one.

P(X = a) = 0 for a continuous variable

For the density $$f(x) = x^2/21$$ on [1, 4], the probability that X is exactly 2 is zero, even though the density at 2 is 4/21. The probability of an interval is the area over it.

Consequence: $$P(2 \le X \le 4) = P(2 < X \le 4) = P(2 \le X < 4) = 8/9$$. Only for a discrete variable does the choice of strict or non-strict inequality matter, and there it matters by exactly the mass at the endpoint.

A density may exceed one

The uniform density on [0, 0.5] is 2 everywhere on its support. That is legal: a density is a height, and probability is the area beneath it, which is still 1 over the support.

The constraint is on the integral, not on the value. Rejecting an option because "a probability cannot be 2" is right for a PMF and wrong for a density — read which one the question gave you.

Disjoint is not independent

With P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.1 (GATE 2024 Set 1 Q17): the events are neither independent (0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15 ≠ 0.1) nor disjoint (the overlap is 0.1, not 0).

Two events with a non-empty overlap cannot be disjoint, and two disjoint events with positive probabilities cannot be independent, because knowing one happened tells you the other did not. The only escape is a zero probability on one side.

The Poisson approximation has a condition

Binomial(1000, 0.003) is within 0.0015 of Poisson(3) in total variation. Binomial(10, 0.3) has the same mean of 3 and is nowhere near Poisson(3).

The condition is n large and p small, with np fixed — not merely that np is moderate. If a question gives ten trials, use the binomial.

Memorylessness belongs to two families only

Exponential: $$P(X > 7 \mid X > 3) = P(X > 4) = e^{-1}$$. Geometric, discrete: the same statement with counts.

Uniform is not memoryless: a wait uniform on [0, 10] conditioned on having already waited 3 leaves 7, not 10. Applying memorylessness to a uniform gives a number that is too large by a factor of 10/7.

Integer lambda gives two equal central bars

For Poisson with λ = 3, P(X = 2) = P(X = 3) = 0.2240. Both are modes, and the mean of 3 is one of them.

The same happens for a binomial when np is an integer: two adjacent bars tie only when the mean sits exactly halfway, so a whole-number mean normally gives a single mode. Compute the neighbouring bar before asserting which is largest.

A median can sit on an atom

GATE 2017 Set 1 Q37: X is Gaussian with mean 0, and Y = max(X, 0). Half the mass of X is at or below zero, so P(Y = 0) = 0.5 exactly.

Any value m with P(Y ≤ m) ≥ 0.5 and P(Y ≥ m) ≥ 0.5 is a median, and 0 satisfies both. The median of a variable with an atom is not found by integrating; the mass already answers it.

Independence is needed for the product, not the sum

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] holds for any pair. E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] requires independence (or at least zero correlation, which is weaker and rarely what a question states).

Variance needs independence as well: $$Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)$$ only then. Without it there is a covariance term, and the standard trap is an option that quietly omits it.

Five beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each card gives the belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule to use instead. These are drawn from the shapes of wrong options in past papers, not from a survey of learners.

1. "The most likely outcome is likely"

Why it tempts. Seven is the most probable sum of two dice, so it feels as if it should happen often.

Refutation. P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. The best single outcome still fails five times in six, and every other sum is less likely than that.

Correct rule. Compare the count with the size of the space, never with the other outcomes.

2. "Independent means it cannot happen"

Why it tempts. The everyday phrase "independent events" sounds like events that have nothing to do with each other, and two events that cannot both happen seem maximally unrelated.

Refutation. With a fair die, A = "odd" and B = "less than 4": P(A ∩ B) = 1/3 while P(A)P(B) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, so they are dependent. Disjoint events with positive probability are always dependent.

Correct rule. Independence is the equation P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Check the number, not the story.

3. "The mean is a typical value"

Why it tempts. For symmetric data the mean sits in the middle, and most examples in a first course are symmetric.

Refutation. For an exponential with mean 4, the median is 2.77. Half of all waits are shorter than 2.77, so reporting 4 as typical overstates a typical wait by about forty percent.

Correct rule. The mean is the balance point, and the tail pulls it. If the question says typical, skewed or most, consider the median or the mode.

4. "P(A | B) is the same as P(B | A)"

Why it tempts. Both are conditional probabilities with the same two events, and the notation looks symmetric at a glance.

Refutation. A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity applied to a disease with 1% prevalence gives P(disease | positive) = 0.0099 / 0.0198 = 0.5, while P(positive | disease) = 0.99. Same test, opposite conclusions.

Correct rule. Bayes' theorem is exactly the conversion between the two, and the denominator is the evidence probability, not one.

5. "The density value is a probability"

Why it tempts. For a discrete variable the mass is the probability, and the density occupies the same place in the formulas.

Refutation. The density 1/(3√(2π)) ≈ 0.133 is the height at the mean; the probability of being within one standard deviation is 0.6827. The height is not the probability, and it need not even be below 1.

Correct rule. For a continuous variable, probability is area. Only an interval has one.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

Four pairs get confused and all four are settled by writing down a definition rather than by intuition: mass against density, conditional against joint, independence against disjointness, and mean against median. In each pair, the first is computed and the second is a property of the shape — and a question that asks for the second is answered by looking at the distribution, not by computing a number.

Six question shapes, and the reasoning each demands

The categories below are patterns observed across the catalogued items for this clause. They are descriptions of how the topic is tested, not predictions about the 2027 paper.

Shape 1 — counting in an equally likely space

Two dice, six dice, cards, balls in an urn, permutations of {1..n}. The work is counting, not probability: build the space, count the favourable outcomes, divide.

Wrong approach. Assuming the derived quantity is uniform. Sums of two dice, maxima of two dice and absolute differences are all non-uniform even though the underlying outcomes are.

Shape 2 — the conditional that changes the denominator

"Given that the first ball was red…", "Given that at least one is a boy…". The sentence supplies a new sample space, and the answer is the count inside it.

Wrong approach. Dividing by the original total. If the answer comes out below the unconditional probability when the conditioning event should raise it, the denominator is the slip.

Shape 3 — Bayes with a given table or tree

The question gives priors and conditional probabilities, usually as a table, a tree or a labelled graph, and asks for the reverse conditional.

Wrong approach. Using one path as the denominator. The denominator is every path that produces the evidence, and its omission is visible immediately as an answer above one.

Shape 4 — expectation and variance from a stated PMF or density

A table, or a density with an unknown constant. First fix the constant so the total is 1, then compute the weighted sum, then the second moment.

Wrong approach. Computing the plain average of the values, or computing E[X²] and reporting it as the variance. The variance is E[X²] − (E[X])².

Shape 5 — recognise the family from a density or a moment

Match a given formula against the standard forms. The exponent's denominator is twice the variance; the constant in front must equal 1/(σ√(2π)). For a Poisson, the mean and the variance are equal.

Wrong approach. Reading the exponent's denominator as the variance, or reading a Poisson's variance as its mean plus something. Both are offered as options.

Shape 6 — a statement set (MSQ)

Four or five claims about one small example, and you select every true one. Each claim is usually one of the traps on the Misconceptions tab.

Wrong approach. Testing the claims against the story rather than against the numbers. Compute the three or four quantities once and check each claim against them.

The procedure that covers all six

Name the type. Discrete or continuous; equally likely or given by a table; which family, if the question names one.

Translate the words. Write the question as an event with inequalities. "At least two" is $$X \ge 2$$; "more than two" is $$X \ge 3$$. Conditioning adds a vertical bar and a smaller denominator.

Compute, then check. Every probability lies in [0, 1]; a PMF sums to 1; a variance is non-negative; a standard deviation is the square root. Each of those checks takes seconds and kills a whole class of slips.

Answer the question asked. Not the likelihood when the posterior was wanted, and not the variance when the standard deviation was wanted.

NATGATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q54

A sender S transmits a signal which can be H or L, with probabilities 0.1 and 0.9 respectively, to a receiver R. The probability that R receives L given that H was transmitted is 0.7, and the probability that R receives H given that L was transmitted is 0.8. If the received signal is H, the probability that the transmitted signal was H (rounded to two decimal places) is ______.

Answer: 0.04

P(H received) = 0.1 × 0.3 + 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.03 + 0.72 = 0.75. Then P(H sent | H received) = 0.03 / 0.75 = 0.04.

Why 0.3 is wrong. That is P(H received | H sent), which the question gives you. Why 0.96 appears. It is 0.72/0.75, the posterior for L, so it is the right computation for the wrong question.

NATGATE CSE 2026 Set 1 Q48

Let X be a random variable taking values in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, with P(X = 1) = P(X = 2) = P(X = 5) = P(X = 7) = 1/6 and P(X = 3) = P(X = 4) = P(X = 6) = P(X = 8) = 1/12. The expected value E[X] is ______.

Answer: 4.25

(1 + 2 + 5 + 7)/6 = 2.5 and (3 + 4 + 6 + 8)/12 = 1.75, so E[X] = 4.25 = 17/4.

Why 4.5 is wrong. The unweighted average of 1 to 8. Why the variance is 265/48. E[X²] = 283/12 = 23.5833, and 23.5833 − 18.0625 = 5.5208; the standard deviation is 2.3496. If a question in this family asks for the standard deviation, the answer is not the variance.

MCQOriginal practice

Two fair dice are thrown independently. Which one of the following is the probability that the sum is 11, given that the sum exceeds 9?

  1. 1/18
  2. 1/6
  3. 1/3
  4. 1/2

Answer: C — 1/3

The conditioning event has 6 outcomes (three for 10, two for 11, one for 12) and 2 of them give 11, so 2/6 = 1/3.

Why 1/18. That is the unconditional probability, 2/36. Why 1/6. That is P(sum > 9) — the denominator, reported instead of the answer. Why 1/2. It counts the three possible sums as equally likely instead of counting outcomes.

MSQOriginal practice

Let A and B be events with P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.1. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. A and B are independent.
  2. A and B are mutually exclusive.
  3. P(A ∪ B) = 0.7.
  4. P(A | B) = 0.2.
  5. P(neither A nor B) = 0.3.

Answer: C, D and E only

Union: 0.3 + 0.5 − 0.1 = 0.7. Conditional: 0.1/0.5 = 0.2. Neither: 1 − 0.7 = 0.3. Independence fails because 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15 ≠ 0.1, and mutual exclusivity fails because the intersection is 0.1, not 0.

Why A and B tempt. A small intersection feels like no intersection, and "independent" is often used loosely. Both are equations, and both fail by a visible margin. This is the shape of GATE 2024 Set 1 Q17, restated here as an original question with the same numbers.

NATOriginal practice

A continuous random variable X has density f(x) = C x² for 1 ≤ x ≤ 4 and zero elsewhere. The value of E[X] is ______ (give the answer to two decimal places).

Answer: 3.04 (exactly 85/28)

Normalise first: ∫₁⁴ Cx² dx = C(64 − 1)/3 = 21C = 1, so C = 1/21. Then E[X] = ∫₁⁴ x·x²/21 dx = (1/21)(256 − 1)/4 = 255/84 = 85/28 ≈ 3.0357.

Why 2.5 is wrong. That is the midpoint of [1, 4], which is the mean only for a flat density. This density rises with x, so the mean is pulled to the right. Why 1/21 is not the answer. That is the constant, not the expectation — and forgetting to normalise gives 63.75.

NATGATE CSE 2026 Set 2 Q4

The probability density function of a real random variable X is $$f(x) = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-x^2/18)$$ for all real x. The variance of X is ______.

Answer: 9

Compare with the standard form: the exponent is −(x − μ)²/(2σ²), so 2σ² = 18 and σ² = 9. The constant 1/(3√(2π)) confirms σ = 3, and there is no shift, so μ = 0.

Why 18 is wrong. It is twice the variance. Why 3 is wrong. It is the standard deviation, which the constant in front also reports — the question asks for the variance. Why 81 appears. Squaring the standard deviation twice.

MCQOriginal practice

Events A and B arrive as a Poisson process with rate 3 per minute. Which one of the following statements about the number of arrivals N in one minute is TRUE?

  1. The mean of N is 3 and the variance of N is 3.
  2. The mean of N is 3 and the variance of N is 9.
  3. The mean of N is 3 and the standard deviation of N is 3.
  4. P(N = 0) = 1/3.

Answer: A — mean 3, variance 3

For a Poisson, mean = variance = λ. Here P(N = 0) = e⁻³ = 0.0498, so option D is also numerically wrong.

Why C tempts. If the variance is 3, the standard deviation is √3 = 1.732, not 3. A distribution with mean 3 and standard deviation 3 would be far more spread out. Why B tempts. It borrows the squared rate from the exponential, whose variance is 1/λ².

MCQOriginal practice

The waiting time X for the next bus is exponentially distributed with mean 4 minutes. You have already waited 3 minutes. Which one of the following is the probability that you wait at least 4 more minutes?

  1. e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368, the same as waiting 4 minutes from the start
  2. e⁻¹·⁷⁵ ≈ 0.472, because the remaining mean is shorter
  3. 1 − e⁻¹ ≈ 0.632, the complement
  4. 0.25, because 4 minutes is one mean

Answer: A — e⁻¹ ≈ 0.3679

With mean 4, λ = 0.25, and P(X > 7 | X > 3) = e^(−0.25 × 4) = e⁻¹. Memorylessness makes the elapsed three minutes irrelevant.

Why B tempts. It applies the intuition that having waited already should shorten the remaining wait. That is true for a uniform wait and false for an exponential, and it is the whole content of the word memoryless. Why C is wrong. It gives the probability of waiting less than 4 more minutes.

The one-screen revision sheet

Definitions on the left, the five families and their signatures on the right, traps along the bottom.

Definitions you must be able to write

  • $$P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$$ for $$P(B) > 0$$.
  • Independence: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$.
  • Total probability over a partition: $$P(A) = \sum_j P(A \mid B_j)P(B_j)$$.
  • Bayes: posterior = one path / all paths.
  • PMF sums to 1; density integrates to 1; CDF never decreases and ends at 1.
  • $$E[X] = \sum x p(x)$$; $$Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$.
  • $$E[aX+b] = aE[X]+b$$; $$Var(aX+b) = a^2 Var(X)$$.
  • $$E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y]$$ always; $$E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$$ under independence.

The five families and their signatures

  • Uniform[a,b]: mean (a+b)/2, variance (b−a)²/12, flat.
  • Binomial(n,p): mean np, variance np(1−p), counts successes, needs independence and a fixed p.
  • Poisson(λ): mean = variance = λ, counts arrivals, the large-n small-p limit of the binomial.
  • Exponential(λ): mean 1/λ, variance 1/λ², median 0.693/λ, memoryless.
  • Normal(μ,σ²): mean μ, variance σ², 68.27 / 95.45 / 99.73 percent within one, two, three σ, symmetric.
  • Geometric(p): mean 1/p, variance (1−p)/p², the discrete memoryless family.

The five slips, one last time

Reporting the likelihood instead of the posterior · dividing a conditional by the whole space instead of by the conditioning event · reading a density's height as a probability · treating disjoint events as independent · reporting the variance when the standard deviation was asked for. Write the denominator and the units at the top of your working and none of the five survives.
