Topic and objective

TOPIC: Counting the linear extensions of a partial order — how many full orderings keep every constraint pointing forward. The same count appears in GATE as the number of topological orderings of a DAG, the number of interleavings of two threads, and the number of conflict-serialisable schedules of two transactions.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a partial order, decide which counting method is legal for its shape, and carry it out exactly. Name the elements, draw the arrows that must hold, then: a chain gives one; parallel chains give a binomial or a multinomial; a rooted tree gives $$n!$$ over the product of its subtree sizes; anything else gives a case split on a maximal element, or the subset table. Recognise the same count inside a concurrency question and inside a schedule question, and keep the number of orderings apart from the number of distinct outcomes.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class B — a prerequisite technique for topics the 2027 syllabus names. Discrete Mathematics names partial orders and lattices and Combinatorics: counting; Section 5 names graph traversals, whose topological ordering this counts; Section 8 names concurrency and synchronization and Section 9 transactions and concurrency control, where the same binomial counts interleavings and schedules. Three previous-year items were verified this run and are worked on the GATE patterns tab.

PREREQUISITES: a partial order and a Hasse diagram (Partial orders, Hasse diagrams and lattices); permutations, factorials and binomial coefficients (Counting and combinatorics); a directed acyclic graph and its topological order (Traversals, MSTs and shortest paths).

VISUALIZATION PLAN: eleven scenes. First the definition, seen as one ordering that survives and one that does not. Then the smallest cases, to fix the range between one and $$n!$$. Then the definition executed: all twenty-four orderings of four elements tested against three arrows, five kept. Then the two composition rules, series and parallel, with the binomial drawn as a choice of slots. Then the multinomial, and the divide-by-two-per-arrow shortcut broken on a chain of three and a chain of four. Then the hook-length formula on a tree. I read this topic as the counting method, not the theory of partial orders. Hasse diagrams, lattices and the definitions of order relations belong to the prerequisite lesson; this one assumes them and counts.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive, and the Hasse diagram that draws only its cover relations.
  • $$n!$$, and why the number of orderings of $$n$$ distinct things is $$n!$$.
  • The binomial coefficient $$\binom{n}{k}$$ as the number of ways to choose $$k$$ positions out of $$n$$.
  • A directed acyclic graph, and a topological order as a listing in which every edge points forward.

Helpful, not required: having met a schedule of two transactions, so that the last scene has somewhere to land.

Approximate learning time: 45–60 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. A linear extension is a full ordering that breaks no constraint. Topological orders of a DAG are the same objects.
  2. The count lies between 1 and $$n!$$. A chain gives one, an antichain gives $$n!$$, and adding a constraint can only lower it.
  3. Series multiplies; parallel chooses slots. Those two joins produce every closed form in the lesson, and the binomial is the parallel rule.
  4. The count is #P-complete in general. So a question always gives a shape a formula reaches, or a set small enough for cases or the subset table.
  5. Orderings and outcomes are different counts. Six interleavings can produce three distinct results, and the question says which it wants.

Intuition: a project plan, not a recipe

A recipe is a chain: chop, then fry, then serve. A project plan is weaker. “Pour the foundation before the walls” and “order the windows before glazing” are both constraints, and they say nothing about each other. Many day-by-day schedules satisfy the plan, and the planner wants to know how many.

That number is the number of linear extensions. It is not the number of legal next steps, and it is not the number of plans you can write down quickly: it is the number of complete orderings of all the tasks in which no constraint is violated.

Educational interpretation The analogy is exact about the structure — constraints are arrows, schedules are total orders — and loose about cost, since a real plan also has durations and resources. Nothing here models durations.

Formal definitions

A partial order on a set $$P$$ is a relation $$\le$$ that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. Write $$x < y$$ when $$x \le y$$ and $$x \ne y$$, read “$$x$$ must come before $$y$$”.

A linear extension of $$P$$ is a total ordering $$v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$$ of the elements in which $$x$$ appears before $$y$$ whenever $$x < y$$ in $$P$$.

Write $$e(P)$$ for the number of linear extensions of $$P$$. For a directed acyclic graph, $$e$$ of its reachability order is the number of topological orderings.

$$1 \;\le\; e(P) \;\le\; n!$$

The lower end is a chain, where the order is forced. The upper end is an antichain, where no two elements are comparable and every ordering is legal. Both ends are attained, so neither bound can be improved.

Mathematical derivation Every linear extension is one of the $$n!$$ orderings, so the count cannot exceed $$n!$$. At least one ordering exists because a finite partial order always has a minimal element, which may be placed first and removed; repeating places all $$n$$.

Level 1–2: what the definition asks you to do

The definition is a test on a whole ordering, not on a step. For each of the $$n!$$ orderings, check every constraint once. If none is broken, count it.

For four elements that is twenty-four tests, and the page runs all of them on the animation tab: three arrows, nineteen orderings rejected, five kept. That is the honest baseline, and every shortcut afterwards is a way of not running the loop.

Mathematical derivation The loop costs $$n! \cdot |R|$$ comparisons for $$|R|$$ constraints. At $$n = 10$$ that is 3,628,800 orderings, which is why a hand count needs structure rather than patience.

Level 3–4: the shape decides the method

Two joins build every poset a formula can reach. Series: every element of the first block before every element of the second, so the counts multiply. Parallel: nothing crosses between the blocks, so their elements interleave, and the number of interleavings of sizes $$m$$ and $$n$$ is $$\binom{m+n}{m}$$.

$$e(P \;\text{then}\; Q) = e(P)\,e(Q) \qquad e(P \parallel Q) = \binom{|P|+|Q|}{|P|}\,e(P)\,e(Q)$$

“then” is the series join and “parallel” is the disjoint union. A poset built from single elements by these two operations is called series-parallel, and every closed form in this lesson is one of these two rules applied repeatedly.

Mathematical derivation In series, a linear extension is a linear extension of $$P$$ followed by one of $$Q$$, and nothing else. In parallel, choose which of the $$|P|+|Q|$$ positions belong to $$P$$; the rest belong to $$Q$$, and each block keeps its own internal order.

Level 5–6: when the shape is not enough

The four-element poset with $$A < C$$, $$B < C$$ and $$B < D$$ is not series-parallel. Element $$B$$ touches both halves, so no cut separates it, and no formula applies. Its count is five, obtained by two cases on the last element: if $$C$$ is last, three orderings; if $$D$$ is last, two.

When even case analysis is unattractive, the subset table always works. Let $$f(S)$$ be the number of ways to order the set $$S$$ as a prefix. Then $$f$$ of the full set is one, and every other entry is the sum of $$f(S \cup \{v\})$$ over elements $$v$$ whose predecessors all lie in $$S$$. The answer is $$f(\varnothing)$$, at a cost of $$n \cdot 2^n$$.

Verified fact Counting linear extensions is #P-complete (Brightwell and Winkler, STOC 1991), so no polynomial formula can exist for a general poset. The decision problem, by contrast, is trivial: every partial order has a linear extension.

Level 7–8: the conditions these methods assume

Each method carries a condition, and using it outside the condition is the commonest error in this topic.

  • Binomial. Two blocks, no constraint crossing between them, and each block internally a chain of fixed order.
  • Multinomial. Several blocks, pairwise incomparable, each a chain.
  • Hook length. A rooted forest, read from the root downwards, so each element has at most one predecessor.
  • Case split. Only on a maximal element, and the cases must cover every linear extension exactly once.
  • Subset table. No condition at all — which is why it is the fallback, and why it is exponential.

A formula applied outside its condition gives a number, and a wrong number is harder to notice than a refusal to compute.

Example 1 — the diamond, by enumeration

Elements $$A, B, C, D$$ with $$A < B$$, $$A < C$$, $$B < D$$, $$C < D$$.

  1. $$A$$ must be first: it is the only element with nothing before it.
  2. $$D$$ must be last: it is the only element with nothing after it.
  3. $$B$$ and $$C$$ are incomparable, so they fill the two middle places in either order.
  4. Count: $$1 \times 2 \times 1 = 2$$, namely $$ABCD$$ and $$ACBD$$.

The series rule is visible here: the diamond is a single element, then an antichain of two, then a single element. $$1 \cdot 2! \cdot 1 = 2$$.

Example 2 — two parallel chains

$$B < C$$ and $$D < E$$, with nothing between the two chains. This is the middle of the 2016 question.

  1. Four elements, so four positions.
  2. Choose which two positions hold $$B$$ and $$C$$: $$\binom{4}{2} = 6$$ choices.
  3. Inside the chain the order is already fixed, so each choice gives exactly one ordering.
  4. The six: $$BCDE$$, $$BDCE$$, $$BDEC$$, $$DBCE$$, $$DBEC$$, $$DEBC$$.

Mathematical derivation Equivalently $$\frac{4!}{2!\,2!} = \frac{24}{4} = 6$$, which is the same binomial written as factorials. The denominator has one factorial per chain.

Example 3 — three parallel chains

Three disjoint pairs: $$A < B$$, $$C < D$$, $$E < F$$. Six elements.

  1. Choose two of six positions for the first pair: 15.
  2. Choose two of the remaining four for the second: 6.
  3. The last two belong to the third pair: 1.
  4. $$15 \times 6 \times 1 = 90$$.

Mathematical derivation That product is the multinomial $$\frac{6!}{2!\,2!\,2!} = \frac{720}{8} = 90$$. The page computes both and checks that they agree.

Example 4 — a rooted tree

Root $$r$$ with children $$x$$ and $$y$$, and $$x$$ has a child $$z$$. Read downwards: $$r$$ before $$x$$, $$r$$ before $$y$$, $$x$$ before $$z$$.

  1. Write each node’s subtree size: $$r$$ has 4, $$x$$ has 2, $$y$$ has 1, $$z$$ has 1.
  2. Multiply them: $$4 \times 2 \times 1 \times 1 = 8$$.
  3. Divide $$4!$$ by 8: $$\frac{24}{8} = 3$$.
  4. The three: $$rxyz$$, $$rxzy$$, $$ryxz$$.

Mathematical derivation $$r$$ must be first, and after removing it the two subtrees are parallel, of sizes 2 and 1, giving $$\binom{3}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 3$$. The hook-length formula and the composition rules agree, and the page checks that they do.

Example 5 — the poset no formula reaches

$$A < C$$, $$B < C$$, $$B < D$$. Count by the last element, which must be maximal: here $$C$$ or $$D$$.

  1. $$C$$ last. The rest is $$A, B, D$$ with only $$B < D$$: three orderings, $$ABD$$, $$BAD$$, $$BDA$$.
  2. $$D$$ last. $$C$$ must follow both $$A$$ and $$B$$, so $$C$$ is last of those three: two orderings, $$ABC$$, $$BAC$$.
  3. $$3 + 2 = 5$$.

Mathematical derivation The cases are exhaustive because the last element of a linear extension must be maximal, and every linear extension has exactly one last element. So no ordering is counted twice and none is missed.

Example 6 — the subset table for the same poset

Predecessors: $$A$$ none, $$B$$ none, $$C$$ has $$A$$ and $$B$$, $$D$$ has $$B$$. The base case is $$f(ABCD) = 1$$, and every other entry is the sum over the elements that may be placed next.

size 3
$$f(ABC)=1$$, $$f(ABD)=1$$, $$f(ACD)=1$$, $$f(BCD)=1$$
size 2
$$f(AB)=2$$, $$f(AC)=1$$, $$f(AD)=1$$, $$f(BC)=2$$, $$f(BD)=1$$, $$f(CD)=2$$
size 1
$$f(A)=2$$, $$f(B)=3$$, $$f(C)=3$$, $$f(D)=2$$
size 0
$$f( arnothing)=5$$, the answer

Read the recurrence twice. From the empty set only $$A$$ and $$B$$ may be placed, because $$C$$ needs both of them and $$D$$ needs $$B$$: so $$f( arnothing) = f(A) + f(B) = 2 + 3 = 5$$. From $$\{A\}$$ only $$B$$ is legal, since $$C$$ still needs $$B$$ and $$D$$ still needs $$B$$: so $$f(A) = f(AB) = 2$$. From $$\{B\}$$ both $$A$$ and $$D$$ are legal: $$f(B) = f(AB) + f(BD) = 2 + 1 = 3$$.

Entries for prefixes that can never occur, such as $$\{C\}$$ alone, are computed by the same rule and never reach the answer, because no legal first move leads there. The animation tab prints all sixteen so the arithmetic can be followed.

Counterexample 1 — dividing by two per arrow

On a chain $$A < B < C$$, the rule “divide $$n!$$ by 2 for each arrow” gives $$\frac{6}{4} = 1.5$$. A count is never a fraction, and the true answer is 1.

On a chain of four it gives $$\frac{24}{8} = 3$$, and the true answer is 1. The rule is right only when the arrows sit on disjoint pairs, where each pair really does halve the count independently.

The condition it violates: the factors are multiplied as if the constraints were independent. Two arrows that share an element are not independent.

Counterexample 2 — multiplying level factorials

Take a chain of three $$A < B < C$$ together with a separate pair $$D < E$$, five elements. Reading the levels as $$\{A, D\}$$, $$\{B, E\}$$, $$\{C\}$$ and multiplying their factorials gives $$\frac{120}{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 30$$.

The true count is 10, computed by the page: the chain contributes nothing and the pair is parallel to it, so the answer is $$\binom{5}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 10$$.

The condition it violates: a level is not an antichain block in general, so its elements cannot be permuted freely. $$B$$ and $$E$$ are in the same level but not interchangeable with the elements around them.

Counterexample 3 — six orderings, three outcomes

Two threads of two statements each give six legal interleavings. Running them, four of the six produce the same pair of values, so the set of possible outcomes has three members.

A question that asks for the number of interleavings wants 6; one that asks which outcomes are possible wants 3; one that asks how many orderings give a named outcome wants the multiplicity, which is 4 for one of the three.

The condition it violates: treating the map from orderings to outcomes as one-to-one. It is not, and the collision is the whole point of the 2024 question.

The cycle case is a refusal, not a small number: a relation with $$A < B < A$$ is not a partial order, so $$e$$ is undefined rather than zero. A DAG with a cycle has no topological ordering, and a schedule whose precedence graph has a cycle is not serialisable.

The distinction that decides marks

Number of orderings counts sequences. Number of outcomes counts the distinct results of running those sequences. Number of serialisable schedules counts sequences whose precedence graph is acyclic — a subset of all sequences, so it is a subtraction, not a binomial.

In the 2017 question the binomial gives all 70 schedules, and the answer 54 needs the 16 cyclic ones removed. The binomial alone is the answer to a different question.

Whenever a question gives a total and asks for a legal subset, expect a subtraction, and count the illegal ones by their own smallest witness.

Where the arrows come from, and where they do not

Only the cover relations need drawing, but every implied relation counts. $$A < B < C$$ forbids $$C$$ before $$A$$ without any arrow from $$A$$ to $$C$$.

Conversely, an arrow already implied changes nothing, which is why a DAG and its transitive closure have the same number of topological orderings. The page checks that closing a relation leaves the count at 1 for a three-chain.

So: draw few arrows, count all the constraints. Under-drawing the picture is safe; under-counting the constraints is not.

“Divide $$n!$$ by 2 for every constraint”

Why it tempts: it is exact on disjoint pairs. Three pairs of two give $$\frac{720}{8} = 90$$, which is right, and a formula that works once looks like a formula.

Minimal counterexample: a chain of three. $$\frac{6}{4} = 1.5$$, and the answer is 1.

Correct rule: divide by the factorial of each block’s size, and only where the blocks are disjoint and internally fixed. One factorial per chain, not one 2 per arrow.

One-second check: if the arithmetic gives a non-integer, the formula is wrong.

“Multiply the factorials of the levels”

Why it tempts: levels look like independent blocks, and for a poset that is a product of chains they are.

Minimal counterexample: the chain of three plus a pair, where the rule gives 30 and the answer is 10.

Correct rule: parallel blocks must be pairwise incomparable, and a level is not a block. Two elements in one level can still be separated by an element in the next.

Ask of each block: is every element of this block incomparable with every element of the others? If not, the block is not a block.

“A connected DAG has few topological orders”

Why it tempts: connectivity feels like constraint, and a tree does force its root first.

Minimal counterexample: three elements with $$A$$ before $$B$$ only. The graph is connected and has 3 orderings. A four-element antichain has 24 while being disconnected, and a four-element star with $$A$$ first has 6 while being connected.

Correct rule: the count is a property of the partial order, not of the underlying undirected graph. Connectivity of the drawing tells you nothing about $$e(P)$$.

The right question is always: how many elements are free, and how many arrows tie them?

“There must be a formula”

Why it tempts: every other counting topic in the syllabus has one, and the decision problem here is trivially yes.

Minimal counterexample: the four-element poset with $$A < C$$, $$B < C$$, $$B < D$$. No series and no parallel join produces it, and its count is 5, not any of 1, 2, 6 or 24.

Correct rule: counting linear extensions is #P-complete. Polynomial algorithms exist for trees, series-parallel posets and bounded width, and a question gives one of those or a set small enough for cases.

If a poset resists every formula, that is information, not failure: switch to cases or to the subset table.

“Six interleavings means six possible results”

Why it tempts: each interleaving is a different execution, so they feel like different outcomes.

Minimal counterexample: the 2024 question, where six interleavings produce three value pairs, one of them four times.

Correct rule: the map from orderings to outcomes is many-to-one. Count whichever object the question names, and check by running the outcomes rather than assuming.

The page runs all six and reports the three, so this is a computed distinction and not a slogan.

“A valid topological sort is one choice at each step”

Why it tempts: the Kahn algorithm picks a source, deletes it, and repeats; the count looks like the number of choices at each step multiplied together.

Minimal counterexample: three incomparable elements give $$3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$$, which is right; add one arrow $$A < B$$ and the step-count gives $$3 \times 2 \times 1$$ again unless the shrinking is tracked, while the answer is 3.

Correct rule: the choices available at step $$k$$ depend on which choices were made earlier. That dependence is exactly what the subset table records, and why a product of step counts is not a formula.

The product of step counts is right only when the poset is an antichain, where nothing shrinks.

Pattern 1 — count the orderings of a drawn DAG

The question draws a small DAG and asks for the number of topological orderings. Nothing is named; the work is all decomposition.

  1. Find the unique source and unique sink, if they exist. Fix both ends and delete them.
  2. Ask whether the remainder splits into blocks with no arrow between them.
  3. If it does, apply the binomial or the multinomial, one factor per block.
  4. If it does not, case-split on a maximal element, or list the orderings if $$n$$ is small.

The commonest wrong approach is to multiply the number of sources available at each step, which is right only for an antichain.

Pattern 2 — interleavings of two or more sequences

Two processes, threads or instruction streams, each keeping its own order, and a question about how many execution orders exist.

  1. Each stream is a chain. Nothing crosses between streams.
  2. So the count is the multinomial $$\frac{(m+n)!}{m!\,n!} = \binom{m+n}{m}$$.
  3. If the question asks for distinct results instead, run the orderings: the count of results can be smaller.
  4. If some statements are independent within a stream, the stream is not a chain and the multinomial is not the answer.

A frequent variation adds a shared variable and asks which values are possible, which is the 2024 shape and needs the simulation, not just the binomial.

Pattern 3 — schedules, and the subtraction

Two transactions with a fixed operation order, and a question about conflict-serialisable schedules.

  1. Count all interleavings: $$\binom{m+n}{m}$$.
  2. Identify the conflicting pairs: same item, at least one write.
  3. A schedule is conflict serialisable exactly when its precedence graph is acyclic. With two transactions that means not both orders occur.
  4. Count the cyclic ones by their smallest witness, and subtract.

For the 2017 item: $$\binom{8}{4} = 70$$, sixteen cyclic, so 54. The subtraction is the step people skip.

Pattern 4 — the counting question dressed as a Hasse diagram

A Hasse diagram is given and the question asks how many linear extensions it has, or how many ways the elements can be listed consistently.

  1. Read cover relations off the diagram; the implied ones follow by transitivity.
  2. Test for a tree (each element at most one predecessor), for parallel chains, and for series decomposition.
  3. Apply the matching formula, and say its name so the answer can be checked.
  4. If none applies, count by cases on a maximal element and add.

A diagram with one element below two others that rejoin below is the diamond: count 2. The same shape with the bottom element removed is a V: count 2 as well, for a different reason.

The decision procedure

  1. Name the elements and the arrows. Close nothing; just list what must hold.
  2. Count $$n$$. If $$n \le 6$$, enumerating is legitimate and fast.
  3. Look for series. A cut where every element on one side precedes every element on the other multiplies.
  4. Look for parallel blocks. Pairwise incomparable blocks give a multinomial.
  5. Look for a forest. At most one predecessor per element licenses hook length.
  6. Otherwise case-split on a maximal element, or run the subset table.

Six questions, each answerable by looking at the picture, and each ruling a method in or out.

MCQ original

Q1 — the diamond

A poset has $$A < B$$, $$A < C$$, $$B < D$$ and $$C < D$$. How many linear extensions does it have?

(a) 1   (b) 2   (c) 4   (d) 24

Answer: (b) 2

$$A$$ is the only minimal element and $$D$$ the only maximal one, so both ends are fixed. $$B$$ and $$C$$ are incomparable and fill the two middle places, giving $$ABCD$$ and $$ACBD$$.

(d) is $$4!$$, the count with no constraints at all. (c) doubles the middle block for no reason. (a) is the count for a chain, which this is not.

The shape is a single element, then an antichain of two, then a single element: $$1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 2$$.

NAT original

Q2 — four parallel chains

Eight elements, arranged as four disjoint chains of two, with no constraint between chains. The number of linear extensions is ______.

Answer: 2520

Each chain fixes its own pair, so the count is the multinomial $$\frac{8!}{2!\,2!\,2!\,2!} = \frac{40320}{16} = 2520$$.

By slots: $$\binom{8}{2}\binom{6}{2}\binom{4}{2}\binom{2}{2} = 28 \times 15 \times 6 \times 1 = 2520$$, which is the same product.

Common wrong answer: 40320, which forgets the four fixed internal orders, and 10080, which divides by only 4 once.

MCQ original

Q3 — the chain that breaks a shortcut

A chain of five elements has how many linear extensions, and what does “divide $$5!$$ by 2 for each of the four arrows” give?

(a) 1 and 0.9375   (b) 1 and 7.5   (c) 5 and 120   (d) 120 and 1

Answer: (b)

A chain has exactly one linear extension, because every pair is comparable. The shortcut gives $$\frac{120}{16} = 7.5$$, which is not even an integer.

(a) divides by a different power. (c) and (d) swap the two numbers or use $$n$$ for the count.

Any formula that returns a fraction on a legitimate poset is refuted immediately, and this is the cheapest check in the topic.

MSQ original

Q4 — which statements hold

Select every true statement about counting linear extensions.

(a) Adding a constraint can never increase the count.
(b) Adding a constraint implied by transitivity never changes the count.
(c) Counting linear extensions is polynomial because every poset has one.
(d) A rooted forest read downwards is counted by $$n!$$ over the product of its subtree sizes.
(e) The number of linear extensions of an antichain of $$n$$ elements is $$n!$$.

Answer: (a), (b), (d) and (e)

(a) holds: a constraint only forbids orderings. (b) holds: an implied constraint forbids none, which is why a DAG and its closure agree. (d) holds: the hook-length formula, checked on this page for a tree of four and a forest of two chains. (e) holds: with no constraints every ordering is legal.

(c) fails: the decision problem is trivial, but the count is #P-complete (Brightwell and Winkler, 1991).

(c) is the one that sounds like a proof: “existence is easy, so counting must be easy”. Existence and enumeration are different questions.

Q5 — the tree of five

A root has three children, and one of those children has one child of its own. The number of linear extensions of the resulting tree poset is ______.

Answer: 12

Subtree sizes: the root has 5, the child with a descendant has 2, the other two children have 1 each, and the grandchild has 1. Product $$5 \times 2 \times 1 \times 1 \times 1 = 10$$, so $$\frac{120}{10} = 12$$.

By composition: the root first, then three blocks in parallel of sizes 2, 1, 1, so $$\frac{4!}{2!\,1!\,1!} = 12$$, and the block of size 2 contributes exactly one order.

Common wrong answer: 24, from treating the four descendants as free. The grandchild is not free: it follows its parent.

Q6 — interleavings against outcomes

Two threads each execute three independent statements, and the statements share no variables. How many interleavings are there, and how many distinct final states?

(a) 20 and 20   (b) 20 and 1   (c) 6 and 6   (d) 720 and 1

Answer: (b) 20 and 1

Interleavings: $$\binom{6}{3} = 20$$, since each thread is a chain of three and nothing crosses.

Final state: the statements are independent and share nothing, so every interleaving produces the same result. The map from orderings to outcomes is twenty-to-one.

(a) assumes the map is a bijection. (d) is $$6!$$, which forgets the order inside each thread. (c) is $$\binom{4}{2}$$, the wrong total.

This is the contrast the 2024 question tests in the other direction: there the outcomes were three, not one, because the statements did share variables.

Q7 — one free element

Elements $$A, B, C, D, E$$ with $$A < C$$, $$B < C$$, $$B < D$$ and nothing else. The number of linear extensions is ______.

Answer: 25

The N poset on $$A, B, C, D$$ has 5 linear extensions. $$E$$ is incomparable with all four, so it may be inserted into any of the five gaps of each ordering — before the first element, between any two, or after the last.

$$5 \times 5 = 25$$, and the page confirms it by enumerating all $$5! = 120$$ orderings and testing the three arrows.

Common wrong answers: 5, from forgetting that $$E$$ exists at all, and 20, from allowing only the four gaps between elements and forgetting the two ends. A free element multiplies the count by $$n+1$$, which is five here and not four.

Q8 — the closely confused pair

Two transactions of three operations each run concurrently, and every operation of one conflicts with every operation of the other. What are the numbers of interleavings and of conflict-serialisable schedules?

(a) 20 and 20   (b) 20 and 2   (c) 2 and 2   (d) 20 and 18

Answer: (b) 20 and 2

Interleavings: $$\binom{6}{3} = 20$$.

Serialisable: if every operation of one conflicts with every operation of the other, any interleaving puts some operation of $$T_1$$ before one of $$T_2$$ and some after, so the precedence graph has both edges and a cycle. Only the two serial orders survive.

(a) is the answer when the transactions do not conflict at all, which is the opposite extreme. (c) is the count of serial orders only, not of interleavings. (d) removes the two serial ones instead of keeping them.

The pair to keep apart: interleavings is a binomial, serialisable is that binomial minus the cyclic ones, and the two agree only when no conflicts exist.

The procedure in one place

  1. List the arrows. Cover relations only, and remember the implied ones.
  2. Find the shape. Chain, parallel blocks, forest, or none of these.
  3. Pick the matching rule and name it, so the arithmetic can be checked.
  4. If nothing matches, case-split on a maximal element and add the cases.
  5. Sanity-check: the answer is an integer, at least 1, and at most $$n!$$.

The last step catches most slips in ten seconds, and it is the one people skip under time pressure.

The three names, and what each counts

Topological orderings
linear extensions of the reachability order of a DAG
Interleavings
linear extensions of parallel chains, one per process
Conflict-serialisable schedules
interleavings whose precedence graph is acyclic — a subtraction

All three are the same arithmetic, and the first move is the same: draw the partial order, then ask about its shape.

The one thing that differs is the last: a schedule question subtracts the cyclic orderings, so the binomial is a total and not an answer.

Traps, in one line each

  • Dividing by 2 per arrow: wrong whenever arrows share an element.
  • Multiplying level factorials: a level is not a parallel block.
  • Assuming a formula exists: the count is #P-complete.
  • Confusing orderings with outcomes: the map is many-to-one.
  • Multiplying step counts: the available steps depend on earlier choices.
  • Forgetting implied constraints: the picture is sparse, the order is not.

Every one of these is refuted by a counterexample on the Edge cases tab, with the number computed.
