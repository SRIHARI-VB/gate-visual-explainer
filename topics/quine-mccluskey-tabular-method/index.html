Topic and objective

TOPIC: The tabular method of Quine and McCluskey — the combining passes that produce every prime implicant, the prime implicant chart that turns the primes into a covering problem, essential prime implicants, row and column dominance, cyclic charts, and Petrick's method for solving them.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Run the passes to a fixed point and read the prime implicants off the unticked entries; build the chart with minterm columns only; read an essential prime implicant off a column holding one cross; recognise a cyclic chart and solve it with Petrick's method; and answer a counting question — how many primes, how many essentials, how many literals — without drawing anything.

I read this as the third minimisation method the syllabus names. The prerequisite lesson owns the orientation: one thirty-second scene on dash notation and the cover chart, inside a lesson about identities, minterms and Karnaugh maps. This lesson owns the algorithm: the rounds, the tick as containment, the chart as a set-cover instance, dominance, cyclicity, Petrick, and the cost that makes exactness stop.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Section 2 of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, published by IIT Madras, reads: “Boolean algebra and minimization – algebraic technique, Karnaugh map, tabular method. Design of combinational and sequential circuits. Number representation and arithmetic (fixed and floating point).”

Educational interpretation Class A by name. “Tabular method” is named verbatim, beside the algebraic technique and the Karnaugh map, so this is a named clause rather than an extension.

Community observation The counting form of the question — number of prime implicants, number of essential prime implicants — appears repeatedly across the papers read for this lesson. That is an observation about old papers, not a forecast for 2027.

The five ideas

1. The dash records the variable a merge removed, so a term is a set of minterms.

2. Only groups whose one-counts differ by one can combine, so the sort is the method.

3. A tick is containment: a ticked term cannot be prime.

4. Don't-cares go in when you list and out of the chart.

5. A chart with no single-cross column has no essential, and needs an exact procedure.

Intuition first: the same jigsaw, without the table

Two people are given the same pile of jigsaw pieces and told to find every piece that cannot be extended. The first spreads them on a table and looks. The second writes each piece's shape as a string, sorts the strings, and compares neighbours. The first is faster on twenty pieces and impossible on two hundred. The second is slower on twenty pieces and identical in effort per piece at any size.

Educational interpretation The Karnaugh map is the first person. The tabular method is the second. Nothing about the puzzle changed; only whether the work is stored in your eyes or on the page. That is why the syllabus names both: they find the same blocks, and only one of them can be written down for a machine.

The three words, precisely

An implicant of f is a product term that covers no minterm outside f. A prime implicant is an implicant that no larger implicant contains. An implicant is essential when it is the only prime implicant covering some minterm of f.

Verified fact These are the standard definitions, and the third is the one that is read off a chart rather than off a function: essentiality is a property of a prime relative to the other primes, so it cannot be decided from the function alone.

The cube, and the dash

A term is written as a string over three symbols: 0 for a complemented variable, 1 for an uncomplemented one, and - for a variable the term does not mention. So 0--0 over w,x,y,z means w′ and z′, and covers the four minterms obtained by filling the two dashes.

Mathematical derivation A term with k dashes covers exactly $$2^k$$ minterms — checked here on every cube over two, three and four variables. So “larger implicant” is a statement about sets of minterms, and containment can be tested by membership rather than by looking at the strings.

The combining theorem

$$x\cdot y + x\cdot y' = x\cdot(y+y') = x\cdot 1 = x$$

Two product terms that agree everywhere except in one variable sum to the term with that variable removed. In string form: two terms that differ in exactly one position merge to the term with a dash in that position.

Mathematical derivation The identity is the whole of the method. There is no corresponding identity for a difference of two variables, which is why a merge of two terms differing in two positions is always an error and never a shortcut.

Why the sort is not decoration

Flipping one bit changes the number of ones by exactly one. So two combinable terms always sit in groups whose one-counts differ by 1, and two terms inside the same group never combine, because they agree on their count.

Mathematical derivation This is what makes the method practical: comparing a column of m terms pairwise costs m² comparisons, while comparing only adjacent groups costs a scan. It is also the reason a term placed in the wrong group silently loses every merge it should have had.

Dashes must align before you compare

In the second round the terms already carry dashes. Two dashed terms combine only when their dashes are in the same positions and exactly one of the remaining positions differs. -110 and 011- differ in three positions and never combine.

Trap Two terms that look almost equal but whose dashes sit in different columns are not combinable. Merging them produces a term that covers a minterm outside f, so the result is not an implicant at all — the error is invisible until the answer is checked.

The chart, and why it is a covering problem

Rule one row per prime implicant and one column per minterm of f — don't-cares excluded. A cross marks coverage. Then: a column with one cross forces its row (essential); a row covering a subset of another's columns is redundant; and what remains after those reductions is the cyclic core.

Verified fact The chart is the incidence matrix of a set-cover instance, and the same three reductions — essential, dominance, branch — appear in every treatment of unate covering. That is why the vocabulary of the two literatures is interchangeable.

Petrick's method, precisely

Give each prime implicant a Boolean variable $$P_i$$, true when it is selected. For each column write the sum of the rows covering it. Multiply all the sums, then absorb with $$X + XY = X$$ and collapse with $$XX = X$$. Every product in the result is a valid cover; the minimal ones are the products with the fewest terms, tie-broken by total literals.

Verified fact The variables are not the circuit's inputs — they are decisions about the design. Stanley Petrick described the method in 1956, and it is the exact way to enumerate all minimum covers rather than one.

Where the exactness stops

The number of prime implicants is not bounded by anything small. This page searches every four-variable function with eight minterms and finds one with eight primes; Petrick's expansion can produce up to $$2^m$$ products before absorption, where m is the number of uncovered columns.

Mathematical derivation So the procedure is exact and the exactness is the cost. Practical tools reduce the chart first, apply Petrick only to the cyclic core, and for large functions give up exactness for a heuristic cover — which is why the syllabus asks for the method and not for a proof that it is efficient.

Worked examples, page 1 of 3 — the passes and the primes

E1. The full table for GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q43

The item. The total number of prime implicants of $$f(w,x,y,z)=\Sigma(0,2,4,5,6,10)$$ is ______.

Round 1. In binary and sorted by one-count: group 0 holds 0000; group 1 holds 0010 and 0100; group 2 holds 0101, 0110 and 1010. Six merges, and every parent is ticked: 0+2→00-0, 0+4→0-00, 2+6→0-10, 2+10→-010, 4+5→010-, 4+6→01-0.

Round 2. Of those six terms, two pairs combine. 00-0 with 0-00 gives 0--0, and 0-10 with 01-0 gives 0--0 again — one child, written once. The survivors are -010 and 010-.

Round 3. One term, no merge. 0--0 survives. Total merges: 6 + 2 + 0 = 8. Unticked terms: three.

Computed. The three primes are 0--0, -010 and 010-. The page enumerates all 81 cubes over four variables and finds the same three maximal implicants, so the count is 3 — the published key. The minimal cover uses all three and costs 2 + 3 + 3 = 8 literals.

E2. The chart, the essentials, and the literals

The chart. Three rows for the three primes; six columns for minterms 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 10. Crosses: 0--0 covers 0, 2, 4, 6; -010 covers 2, 6, 10; 010- covers 4, 5.

Single-cross columns. m0 is covered only by 0--0. m5 only by 010-. m10 only by -010. Three columns hold one cross, so all three primes are essential.

Delete and check. Take the three essentials and strike their columns. All six go, so nothing remains to choose. There is exactly one minimal cover, and it is the three essentials.

Computed. The page also runs Petrick's method on this chart and gets one product of three primes, and separately enumerates every subset of rows and gets the same single minimum. Literal count 8, and the expression evaluates to 1 exactly on {0,2,4,5,6,10} over all sixteen inputs.

E3. GATE CSE 2018, Q49 — don't-cares and an empty row

The item. For $$F(P,Q,R,S)=\Sigma m(0,2,5,7,9,11)+d(3,8,10,12,14)$$, the number of essential prime implicants is ______.

Generation. List the six minterms and the five don't-cares, then combine. The passes give six primes: -0-0, -01-, 0-11, 01-1, 1--0, 10--.

The chart. Columns are the six minterms only. Row 1--0 — that is $$P\cdot S'$$ — covers 8, 10, 12 and 14, all of them don't-cares, so its row is empty. It is prime, it is maximal, and no cover can ever select it.

Essentials. m0 only in -0-0; m5 only in 01-1; m9 only in 10--. Three single-cross columns, so 3 — the published key.

Computed. The minimal cover is $$Q'S' + P'QS + PQ'$$, seven literals, and it evaluates to 1 exactly on the six minterms and the five don't-cares. Dropping the don't-cares from the generation step yields a different, smaller prime set, which is the check that they are load-bearing.

Worked examples, page 2 of 3 — the cyclic chart and Petrick

E4. A chart with no essential at all

The function. $$F(A,B,C)=\Sigma m(0,1,2,5,6,7)$$ over A, B, C.

The primes. Six of them, each a pair: $$A'B'$$ (0,1), $$A'C'$$ (0,2), $$B'C$$ (1,5), $$BC'$$ (2,6), $$AC$$ (5,7), $$AB$$ (6,7). No quad exists, because minterms 3 and 4 are zeroes and block every larger block.

Why none is essential. Every minterm lies in exactly two primes. No column holds a single cross, so no prime is the unique cover of anything, so the set of essentials is empty.

Computed. Dominance removes nothing: all six rows have two columns and all six columns have two crosses, so neither rule fires. The chart is irreducible — it is the cyclic core. Exhaustive search confirms no two-row cover exists, so the minimum is three rows.

E5. Petrick's expansion, executed

One sum per column. (P1+P2)(P1+P3)(P2+P4)(P3+P5)(P4+P6)(P5+P6), where the columns are 0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 in that order and P1…P6 are the six primes.

Multiply and absorb. Sixty-four products before absorption; five survive it, of sizes four, three, four, three and four. So exactly two products hold three primes, and the minimum is three.

The two minima. $$F=A'B'+BC'+AC$$ and $$F=A'C'+B'C+AB$$. Both use three primes and both cost six literals.

Computed. Both were evaluated on all eight input combinations: each is 1 exactly on {0,1,2,5,6,7} and 0 on minterms 3 and 4. The expansion was also checked in the other direction — over all 64 assignments of the six decision variables, the absorbed sum is true exactly when the selected rows cover every column, so the absorption rule itself is verified, not assumed.

E6. The three degenerate functions

Empty ON-set. No minterms to list, so no merges and no primes. The prime set is empty, and so is the chart — a cover of nothing needs nothing.

Full ON-set. All sixteen minterms of four variables. Every term merges until one is left: ----, the constant 1. One prime, zero literals, one row.

One minterm. The function is $$\Sigma m(5)$$ alone. Minterm 5 is 0101, it has no partner, and it survives round one unticked. One prime, four literals, no dashes.

E7. Where the count gets large

The search. Every four-variable function with exactly eight minterms was enumerated — 12,870 of them — and the prime count computed for each.

The worst. Eight prime implicants, on the function $$\Sigma m(0,1,2,4,7,9,10,11)$$. Eight rows in the chart, and the choices among them multiply from there.

Why this matters for an exam. A four-variable, eight-minterm function is a small formula on paper and already a substantial chart. That is why a question on this topic almost always asks for a count — how many primes, how many essentials, how many literals — rather than for the whole expansion.

Edge cases and boundary conditions

Each of these is a place where the procedure behaves differently from the picture of it. The rule that covers all of them is the same one: ask which set of terms the step is being applied to.

An empty ON-set

There are no terms to list, so there are no merges and no prime implicants. The chart has no columns, and a cover of nothing needs nothing.

Mathematical derivation Computed: the prime set is empty. This is the one case where “the answer is the empty set” is correct rather than a sign of a mistake.

A full ON-set

All sixteen minterms of four variables merge until a single term is left: ----, the constant 1. One prime, zero literals, and the minimal expression is the constant.

Mathematical derivation Computed: exactly one prime. Any function whose ON-set covers the whole cube has no other prime, because the whole cube contains every implicant.

A prime that covers only don't-cares

On the 2018 function, 1--0 covers minterms 8, 10, 12 and 14, and every one is a don't-care. Its chart row is empty, so no cover can select it and adding it would only add literals.

Mathematical derivation Such a term is genuinely prime — it is maximal among implicants — so it must be listed. It is then discarded by the chart, not by the table. Keeping the two decisions separate is what stops the confusion.

Overlapping covers

On the 2015 function the three primes cover six minterms between them, but their sizes are four, three and two — the sum is nine, because minterms 2 and 4 are covered twice.

Educational interpretation A cover is allowed to cover a minterm more than once and forbidden to leave one out. Adding cover sizes and expecting the minterm count is a frequent arithmetic slip, and it is not evidence that the table is wrong.

Two children from the same pair of parents

In round two of the 2015 table, 00-0 with 0-00 and 0-10 with 01-0 both produce 0--0. A column of terms is a set, so the child appears once.

Mathematical derivation Counting merges and counting children are therefore different counts: eight merges produced three primes here, because several merges converge on one child.

Minterm numbering depends on the variable order

$$f(w,x,y,z)$$ puts w in the most significant position. Write the same function as $$f(z,y,x,w)$$ and every binary string and every prime's name changes, while the function does not.

Trap Every table on this page prints the order it used. In an exam, copy the order from the question's own statement before writing a single string.

A cyclic chart with a tie in literal count

Both minima of the cyclic function cost six literals. Nothing in the algebra prefers one, so a question that asks for “the” minimal expression is asking for either, and a question that asks how many there are is asking for two.

Mathematical derivation The tie is computed, not assumed: both minima were enumerated and both have three primes and six literals.

Essentials that do not cover everything

When the essential columns are struck out and columns remain, the essentials alone are not a cover. What is left is the reduced chart, and it must still be solved — by dominance if possible, by Petrick or branch and bound if not.

Educational interpretation On both functions worked in full here, the essentials happen to cover everything. That is a property of these two functions, not of the method, and it is exactly the case a harder question will break.

Misconceptions, each with the smallest counterexample

Six beliefs that survive a first reading and fail on a function. Every counterexample below was computed by the page's model.

“A bigger block is always a better block.”

False. Being large is not the same as being needed. On the 2018 function the largest prime, $$P\cdot S'$$, covers four minterms — and all four are don't-cares, so it appears in no cover at all.

Mathematical derivation Computed: its chart row holds zero crosses. A prime is maximal; a cover is chosen from the chart, and the chart can reject a maximal term outright.

“Every function has at least one essential prime implicant.”

False. $$\Sigma m(0,1,2,5,6,7)$$ has six prime implicants and every column of its chart holds exactly two crosses, so no prime is essential.

Mathematical derivation Computed: the essential set is empty. Answering such a question with “the essentials” answers nothing, which is why the cyclic case has to be recognised rather than worked through.

“Don't-cares go on the chart like minterms.”

False, and it is the commonest error in the procedure. Don't-cares are included when the terms are listed — they let an implicant grow — and excluded from the chart, because no cover is obliged to include one.

Mathematical derivation Computed on the 2018 function: with the don't-cares the passes find six primes; without them, a different and smaller set. Both halves of the rule are load-bearing, in opposite directions.

“Two terms that differ in two places combine.”

False. There is no identity for that. Merging them produces a term covering minterms outside f, so the result is not an implicant — the error is invisible in the table and fatal in the answer.

Mathematical derivation Checked here on every merge of all three functions: each child's minterms contain both parents' minterms. A two-bit merge fails that test by construction.

“The number of prime implicants equals the number of minterms, or of groups.”

False. The 2015 function has six minterms, three first-column groups and three primes; the 2018 function has six minterms and six primes; the cyclic function has six minterms and six primes.

Educational interpretation The prime count is the number of unticked entries across all rounds, which is a third quantity and matches neither of the other two.

“If Petrick's method gives a product, it is the minimum.”

Careful. Every product in the expansion is a valid cover, but only the shortest ones are minimal — and several may tie. On the cyclic function five products survive absorption and only two of them hold three primes.

Mathematical derivation Computed: products of sizes four, three, four, three and four. Reporting the first product in the list reports a four-prime cover, which is valid and not minimal.

The four question patterns, and the procedure that settles each

This topic appears in a small number of shapes, and the shape decides the method. In every case the arithmetic is short once the shape is recognised.

Pattern 1 — how many prime implicants?

Run the passes to a fixed point and count the unticked entries. Do not count groups, minterms, or rows of the largest column.

Seen as: GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q43 — answer 3. The same function appears in the prerequisite lesson's Karnaugh scene; here it is worked through the table instead.

Pattern 2 — how many essential prime implicants?

Build the chart with minterm columns only, then count the columns holding exactly one cross. Don't-cares are not columns, so they can never force a row.

Seen as: GATE CSE 2018, Q49 — answer 3. Note the trap built into it: the function has six primes and one of them is useless.

Pattern 3 — the minimal expression, or its literal count

Take essentials, strike their columns, reduce by dominance, then solve the core. Compare candidates by term count first and literal count second — in that order, because a cover with fewer terms is always preferred.

Seen as: the counting form appears across several papers; this lesson's own two minima are the worked case.

Pattern 4 — a statement to classify as true or false

Usually about don't-cares, about prime against essential, or about whether a given term is prime. Settle it by definition, and use the smallest counterexample you can write — a cyclic chart for essentiality, a don't-care-only prime for usefulness.

Seen as: the MSQ form of the topic, where several statements are judged independently.

The procedure, in the order that saves marks

1. Fix the order. Write the variable order from the question, most significant first. Every string depends on it.

2. List and group. Minterms and don't-cares, in binary, grouped by one-count. A term in the wrong group loses every merge it should have had.

3. Combine to a fixed point. Adjacent groups only; dashes must align; tick both parents. Stop only when no pair anywhere combines.

4. Collect unticked. That is the prime set. Cross-check on a small function by asking whether any prime can still grow.

5. Chart with minterm columns only. Read essentials down the columns, never across the rows.

6. Reduce, then solve. Strike essential columns, apply row and column dominance, and only then reach for Petrick or branch and bound.

Shortcuts, and the assumption each one needs

“Count the unticked entries in the last column.” True here, and it is the fast route. It assumes the passes actually reached a fixed point — a term unticked in an early column is also prime, so the count must be over all columns.

“All primes essential means the answer is the whole prime list.” True on the 2015 function, where the three essentials cover all six minterms. It assumes no column survives the strike-out; when one does, the remaining choice is still open.

“Don't-cares never matter for the count of essentials.” True, and it is worth knowing why: they are excluded from the chart, so they cannot force a row. It assumes you still included them when generating primes, which changes how many primes exist to be essential.

NATGATE CSE 2015, Set 3, question 43

The total number of prime implicants of the function $$f(w,x,y,z)=\Sigma(0,2,4,5,6,10)$$ is ______.

Answer: 3

Three rounds. Round 1: six minterms, six merges, nothing survives. Round 2: six dashed terms, two merges, survivors -010 and 010-. Round 3: one term, no merge, survivor 0--0. Three unticked entries, so three prime implicants.

Why the tempting answers fail. Six is the minterm count. Three is also the number of first-column groups, which is a coincidence here and not a rule — the 2018 function has three groups and six primes. Two is the number of survivors after round two, which misses the term that survives round three.

Checked. The page enumerates all 81 cubes over four variables and finds exactly the same three maximal implicants, so the count is not a transcription.

NATGATE CSE 2018, question 49

Consider the minterm list form of a Boolean function F given below.

$$F(P,Q,R,S)=\Sigma m(0,2,5,7,9,11)+d(3,8,10,12,14)$$

Here, m denotes a minterm and d denotes a don't-care term. The number of essential prime implicants of the function F is ______.

Answer: 3

Six prime implicants are generated from the minterms together with the five don't-cares. The chart has six columns, one per minterm, and one of the six rows is empty because 1--0 (that is $$P\cdot S'$$) covers only don't-cares.

Three columns hold a single cross: m0 in -0-0, m5 in 01-1, m9 in 10--. So three essential prime implicants.

Why 6 is the wrong answer. Six is the number of prime implicants, and the question asks for essentials — a strictly smaller quantity whenever some prime is redundant.

Why 0 is the wrong answer. A cyclic chart has no essential, but this chart is not cyclic: three of its columns hold one cross.

Checked. The minimal cover is $$Q'S'+P'QS+PQ'$$, seven literals, and it evaluates to 1 exactly on the six minterms and the five don't-cares over all sixteen inputs.

MCQOriginal practice question

In the tabular method, a don't-care minterm is

  1. included when the terms are listed and excluded from the prime implicant chart
  2. excluded when the terms are listed and included in the prime implicant chart
  3. included in both, and may make a prime implicant essential
  4. excluded from both, since it is not part of the function

Answer: A

A don't-care is included when the terms are listed because it may let an implicant grow into a larger one; it is excluded from the chart because no cover is obliged to include it.

Why B fails. Excluding it from the listing loses exactly the merges it exists to permit — computed on the 2018 function, the prime set changes.

Why C fails. A don't-care is not a column, so it cannot be the single cross that forces a row. It can never make a prime implicant essential.

Why D fails. It is not part of the required ON-set, but it is part of the function's specification and it changes the prime set.

NATOriginal practice question

For $$F(A,B,C)=\Sigma m(0,1,2,5,6,7)$$, the minimum number of prime implicants in a minimal sum-of-products expression is ______.

Answer: 3

The chart is cyclic: six primes, each covering two minterms, every column holding exactly two crosses, so no essential exists and dominance removes nothing. Petrick's expansion yields two products of three primes and three of four, so the minimum is three.

Why not 2. Six minterms, each prime covers two, so two primes could cover at most four minterms with no overlap — and every prime here overlaps. Exhaustive search over all pairs confirms no two-row cover exists.

Why not 6. Six is the number of primes, and taking all of them is a cover but not a minimum.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements are true?

  1. A prime implicant that covers only don't-care minterms appears in no minimal cover.
  2. Every essential prime implicant is a prime implicant.
  3. Every prime implicant is an essential prime implicant.
  4. If a chart has no column holding a single cross, it has no essential prime implicant.

Answer: A, B, D

A is true. Its chart row is empty, so selecting it covers nothing required and only adds literals. The 2018 function's 1--0 is the worked instance.

B is true by definition. Essentiality is a property some primes have, not a separate category of term.

C is false. This is the containment direction that matters. On the 2015 function all three primes happen to be essential, which hides the distinction; on the 2018 function three of six are, and on the cyclic function none are.

D is true. An essential prime is exactly the unique cover of some minterm, which is exactly a column with one cross. No such column, no essential — computed on the cyclic function, where the essential set is empty.

MCQOriginal practice question

Two terms in the second round of a tabular minimisation are -110 and 011-. Which statement is correct?

  1. They combine to --1-.
  2. They combine to -11-.
  3. They cannot combine, because they differ in more than one position.
  4. They cannot combine, because their dashes are in different positions.

Answer: C

Compare position by position: position 1 is dash against 0, position 2 is 1 against 1, position 3 is 1 against 1, position 4 is 0 against dash. Two positions disagree — and a dash against a fixed bit is itself a disagreement — so the terms differ in more than one place and no identity applies.

Why D is the tempting wrong answer. “Dashes in different positions” is a real rule, but it is a sufficient condition for failure only when the rest agrees. The decisive test is always the count of differing positions, and here it is two.

Why A and B fail. Both would produce a term covering minterms outside the function. Every merge in this lesson is checked for containment of both parents, and this pair fails that check.

NATOriginal practice question

For the function of GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q43 — $$f(w,x,y,z)=\Sigma(0,2,4,5,6,10)$$ — the number of literals in a minimum sum-of-products expression is ______.

Answer: 8

The three primes are all essential, so the minimal cover is the three primes and nothing else. Literal counts: 0--0 has two, -010 has three, 010- has three. Total 8.

Why not 6. Six is the minterm count. Literals are counted on the terms of the answer, not on the input.

Why not 3. Three is the number of terms. A three-term expression with two literals each would be 6, and the actual terms are not all two literals.

Checked. The expression evaluates to 1 exactly on {0,2,4,5,6,10} over all sixteen inputs, so it is a correct cover and, being the unique minimum, the minimum.

Revision sheet

Definitions, the procedure, the counts and the traps. Everything here is stated with its reasoning above.

Definitions in one line each

Implicant: a product term covering no minterm outside f.

Prime implicant: an implicant no larger implicant contains — an unticked entry.

Essential: the only prime covering some minterm — a column with one cross.

Cube: a string over {0, 1, -}; k dashes cover $$2^k$$ minterms.

Cyclic chart: no single-cross column, no dominance available.

The seven steps

1. Fix the variable order from the question.

2. List minterms and don't-cares in binary, grouped by one-count.

3. Combine adjacent groups; dash the differing bit; tick both parents.

4. Repeat until no pair combines — the fixed point.

5. Collect every unticked entry: the prime set.

6. Chart with minterm columns only; take essentials; apply dominance.

7. Solve the core — Petrick, or branch and bound.

The don't-care rule, and why

In when you list — it may let an implicant grow into a larger one. Out of the chart — no cover is obliged to include it, so it can never force a row.

Mathematical derivation Both halves are load-bearing in opposite directions: including them in the chart makes a useless row look forced; excluding them from the list loses primes that should exist.

The five sentences to carry in

1. The dash records the variable the merge removed, so a term is a set of minterms and containment is membership.

2. Only groups whose one-counts differ by one can combine, so the sort is not decoration.

3. A tick is containment, so a ticked term cannot be prime; unticked entries are the prime set.

4. Don't-cares go in when you list and out of the chart.

5. A chart with no single-cross column has no essential, and needs Petrick rather than inspection.
