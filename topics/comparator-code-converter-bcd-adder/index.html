Topic and objective

TOPIC: Magnitude comparators, code converters and the BCD adder — three design problems inside Section 2, Digital Logic, of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, under the clause "Design of combinational and sequential circuits".

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Design a comparator that compares two words of any width by passing a three-bit state upward, and say why the higher bits decide and the lower bits are never consulted. Count how many input pairs give each of the three answers, and cascade two comparators without corrupting the answer. Convert a word between binary, Gray, BCD and excess-3, and say what each code buys its circuit. Recognise the Karnaugh map axis as a Gray code and connect one-bit difference to cells that merge. Build a BCD adder from a four-bit adder plus a correction term, justify the constant six, and decide whether a proposed correction condition is exact on every reachable nibble.

I read this clause as one method asked three times: find a small quantity that summarises the bits already seen, write one rule per position, then check the rule on every input the circuit can receive. The three circuits below are the standard instances of that method that are not already adders, multiplexers or decoders — and none of them has a lesson of its own in this repository.

Prerequisites

  • Combinational circuits — half and full adders, the multiplexer, the decoder, and the static-1 hazard. This lesson assumes the four-bit ripple adder exists and does not re-derive it.
  • Boolean algebra and minimisation — minterm numbering, the map's Gray-code labels, blocks of 2 to the k, and don't-care conditions. The correction logic here is read with that vocabulary.
  • Number representation and arithmetic — binary place values and the meaning of a four-bit word.
  • Counting and one subtraction. The pair counts are powers of four, and the constant six is sixteen minus ten.

Nothing here needs a simulator or any software. Every number on the canvas is computed on the page and re-checked against the rule it should satisfy.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A specification is too big to tabulate. Two eight-bit words would need 65536 rows before a single gate is drawn.
  • The state is small. Three bits for a comparator, one running bit for a Gray code, one flag for a BCD adder.
  • Comparison reads high to low. The first differing bit decides; every lower bit is irrelevant.
  • A code is a spelling. Gray makes neighbours differ in one bit, and that is exactly why the map's edges touch.
  • The BCD adder is one constant. Six is sixteen minus ten, and the condition is the carry out or the two product terms that cover ten to fifteen.

Intuition first: two scorecards, and a referee who reads from the top

Two players hold scorecards with four digits each, most significant digit on the left. A referee compares them without doing any arithmetic. He looks at the leftmost pair. If one is bigger, he stops — the rest of the card cannot change the answer. If they are equal, he moves right and repeats. The moment a pair differs, he has his answer.

Two things in that picture are the whole of the comparator. The referee carries one small fact as he moves: are we still tied? And he reads in one direction only. A lower digit never overrules a higher one. Educational interpretation The analogy carries the direction and the carried fact. It does not carry the wire count, which is why the formal definition below exists.

Formal vocabulary, every term in words

Combinational circuit — a circuit whose outputs are fixed by its present inputs alone. There is no memory, so its behaviour is completely described by a truth table.

Specification — the relation the circuit must realise, stated before any gate is chosen. For a comparator the relation is: exactly one of greater, equal, less holds.

Magnitude comparator — a combinational circuit with three outputs, where the first is 1 exactly when the first word is numerically greater, the second exactly when they are equal, and the third exactly when the first is smaller.

Code — a rule that assigns a bit pattern to each value. BCD, excess-3 and Gray are four-bit codes for the same ten or sixteen values.

Code converter — a combinational circuit whose input word is one code for a value and whose output word is another code for the same value.

The one-bit comparator, from the definition

Write the definition first, then find the gate.

G = a AND NOT b  ·  E = a XNOR b  ·  L = NOT a AND bG is 1 only on the row a=1, b=0. L is 1 only on a=0, b=1. E is 1 when the two bits agree, which is exclusive-or with the output inverted.

Two of the three are single rows of the table, so each is a single product term. The third is two rows, which is why equality is the widest of the three expressions.

Mathematical derivation The page checks all four rows against these expressions and checks that G + E + L = 1 in every row. Mutual exclusion and exhaustiveness are not assumed; they are verified.

The rule that makes it scale

Let the state entering bit position i be the triple about all bits above i. Then one position updates it by:

G_out = G_in OR (E_in AND a>b)
L_out = L_in OR (E_in AND a<b)
E_out = E_in AND (a=b)In words: a higher position's verdict survives. A new verdict is only recorded if everything above was tied. The tie survives only if it was tied and this pair is equal too.

Start the state at (0, 1, 0) — "the bits above the top are tied" — and feed positions from the most significant down.

Mathematical derivation This is a carry chain over a three-value alphabet, which is why it composes the same way a carry-look-ahead chain does.

Counting the three outcomes

With n bits in each word there are 4 to the n ordered pairs.

equal = 2^n  ·  greater = less = (4^n − 2^n) / 2Each of the 2 to the n first-words has exactly one equal partner. Swapping the two words turns every greater pair into a less pair and back, so those two counts are equal.

At n = 4: 16 equal, 120 greater, 120 less. At n = 8: 256 equal, 32640 greater, 32640 less.

Mathematical derivation Both counts are also computed by enumeration in the page's model, for n from 1 to 8, and agree with the formulas.

Binary to Gray, and back

Write the binary word as bn−1 … b0 and the Gray word as gn−1 … g0.

gi = bi XOR bi+1  (bn = 0)
bi = gi XOR bi+1Encoding pairs each binary bit with the bit above it. Decoding pairs each Gray bit with the binary bit already decoded above it, so the decode chain is cumulative and the encode chain is not.

Equivalently, Gray of n is n XOR (n >> 1), and the inverse is a prefix exclusive-or.

Mathematical derivation Checked at every width from one to ten bits: the map is a bijection, the inverse is exact, consecutive words differ in one bit, and the last and first words differ in one bit too.

Excess-3, and why the offset is three

The code for digit d is the four-bit binary of d + 3.

invert(code(d)) = code(9 − d)Inverting four bits computes 15 minus the value. With the value being d + 3, that is 12 − d, which is (9 − d) + 3. So inverting the code of d gives the code of the nines complement of d.

That property is called self-complementing. It means a subtractor can be built as an adder with inverted inputs, with no borrow logic.

Mathematical derivation Verified on all ten digits, and separately that no excess-3 word is all zeros or all ones.

The BCD adder, and where the six comes from

Two BCD digits and a carry in give a true sum from 0 to 19. A four-bit adder returns that sum split as a carry out and a low nibble equal to the sum modulo 16. Decimal wants the sum split modulo 10.

if sum > 9: add 6 to the low nibbleSix is 16 − 10. For a sum s from 10 to 15, s + 6 = (s − 10) + 16: the nibble becomes s − 10 and the addition produces the carry out. The mismatch between base sixteen and base ten is repaired by exactly one constant.

The condition "sum greater than 9" is realised as three terms:

C_out + S3S2 + S3S1The carry out covers 16 and above. S3S2 covers 1100 to 1111. S3S1 covers 1010, 1011, 1110 and 1111. Their union is exactly 1010 to 1111, so together the three terms mean "greater than nine".

Mathematical derivation The condition is compared with "sum greater than 9" on all sixteen nibbles, and the whole adder is compared with decimal addition on all 200 valid inputs. Both agree everywhere.

What all three share, stated once

Each of the three is solved by a small state that composes upward, never by a table. The comparator's state is three bits; the encoder's is one running bit; the adder's is one flag. In each case the design is: write the per-position rule, start the chain with the identity element (tied, zero, no correction), and then check the rule on every input the circuit can actually receive. That last step is not decoration — it is what separates a plausible circuit from a correct one, and it is the step that the traps in the GATE patterns tab are built to break.

Five worked examples, each with the reason for every step

Numbers are given in binary where the bits are the point, and in decimal where the value is. Every result below was recomputed by the page's model.

Example 1 — one bit, all four rows

a and b each take 0 or 1. The definition assigns the three outputs; the gate expressions must reproduce them.

  • a=0, b=0. Equal. G = 0 AND 1 = 0, E = 0 XNOR 0 = 1, L = 1 AND 0 = 0. Reason: only equality holds of a number and itself.
  • a=0, b=1. Less. G = 0, E = 0, L = 1 AND 1 = 1. Reason: L is the single row 01.
  • a=1, b=0. Greater. G = 1 AND 1 = 1, E = 0, L = 0. Reason: G is the single row 10.
  • a=1, b=1. Equal. E = 1, G = 1 AND 0 = 0, L = 0 AND 1 = 0.

    • Check. Each row sums to 1, and the four rows exhaust the inputs. So the circuit is a valid comparator at one bit, not merely a plausible one.

    Example 2 — four bits: 1011 against 1001

    Read from bit 3 down to bit 0. The state starts tied.

  • Bit 3: 1 against 1. Equal, so the tie passes down. State (0, 1, 0).
  • Bit 2: 0 against 0. Still equal, still tied. State (0, 1, 0).
  • Bit 1: 1 against 0. Not tied and a>b, so greater goes to 1 and equal falls to 0. State (1, 0, 0).
  • Bit 0: 1 against 1. Equal, but E_in is already 0, so nothing changes. State stays (1, 0, 0).

    • Answer: greater. Why the last row does not matter: 1011 is 11 and 1001 is 9, and no change to the lowest bit could make 11 smaller than 9. The OR in the update rule is what encodes that.

    Example 3 — how many pairs give each answer

    A four-bit comparator is fed all 256 ordered pairs of four-bit words. How many pairs produce each output?

  • Total pairs. 16 × 16 = 256. Reason: each word is chosen independently.
  • Equal. One partner per first word, so 16. Reason: the second word must be exactly the first.
  • Greater and less. The remaining 240 split evenly, so 120 each. Reason: swapping the words is a bijection between the greater pairs and the less pairs.

    • Answer: 16 equal, 120 greater, 120 less. General form: 2 to the n, and (4 to the n − 2 to the n) over 2. At eight bits that is 256, 32640 and 32640.

    Example 4 — binary to Gray, and a previous-year item

    Convert 1011 to Gray, then back.

  • g3 = b3 = 1. The top bit has nothing above it.
  • g2 = b2 XOR b3 = 0 XOR 1 = 1.
  • g1 = b1 XOR b2 = 1 XOR 0 = 1.
  • g0 = b0 XOR b1 = 1 XOR 1 = 0. So the Gray word is 1110.
  • Decoding 1110. b3 = 1, b2 = 1 XOR 1 = 0, b1 = 1 XOR 0 = 1, b0 = 0 XOR 1 = 1, giving 1011 back.

    • Previous-year item, GATE CSE 2006 Question 40. Let h3h2h1h0 be the four-bit Gray code of n, and g3g2g1g0 the Gray code of (n+1) mod 16. Which of these is correct? (A) g0 = Σ(1,2,3,6,10,13,14,15) (B) g1 = Σ(4,9,10,11,12,13,14,15) (C) g2 = Σ(2,4,5,6,7,12,13,15) (D) g3 = Σ(0,1,6,7,10,11,12,13). Answer: C. The page tabulates all sixteen rows and reads the minterms of each output; only g2 matches its option. The trap is that the minterm index is the decimal value of the Gray word, not of n.

    Example 5 — the BCD adder on 7 + 5, and on 9 + 9 + 1

    7 + 5, carry in 0.

  • Binary sum. 0111 + 0101 + 0 = 01100, which is 12. Carry out 0, low nibble 1100.
  • Is it above nine? 1100 is above nine, so yes. The term S3S2 is 1.
  • Add six. 1100 + 0110 = 1 0010: carry out 1, nibble 0010.
  • Read it. Carry 1, digit 2, which is 12 written as a decimal answer.

    • 9 + 9, carry in 1.

  • Binary sum. 1001 + 1001 + 1 = 10011, which is 19. Carry out 1, low nibble 0011.
  • Is it above nine? The carry out alone says yes, without looking at the nibble.
  • Add six. 0011 + 0110 = 1001, and the carry out stays 1.
  • Read it. Carry 1, digit 9. Nineteen is one ten and nine units.

    • Why the second case is the one to remember. The low nibble was 0011, which is a perfectly good digit. Only the carry out revealed that a correction was needed. A student who writes the condition as "S3S2 + S3S1" alone gets 19 out as carry 0, digit 3 — which is 3.

    The boundaries, each with the condition it breaks

    Every case below is a place where the rule still applies but the usual shortcut does not. Each is stated with the property that fails.

    1. A cascade input that is not a tie

    Build an eight-bit comparator from two four-bit ones. The low stage's three flags become the high stage's tie input.

    What breaks: if the low stage is bypassed and the high stage is fed (0, 0, 0), then E_in is 0 before the first bit is examined, so neither G nor L can ever be set by the high nibble. The comparator reports "less" on every pair where the high nibbles differ.

    The rule: the identity element for the chain is (0, 1, 0). Anything else injects a verdict about bits that do not exist.

    2. Equal words, where no bit ever decides

    Compare 1010 with 1010.

    What happens: every position is tied, so E stays 1 and G and L stay 0 all the way down. The answer comes from the tie surviving the whole chain, not from any single bit.

    Why it matters: a design that reports equality by OR-ing the per-bit equalities is wrong — 1000 and 0001 have a tied bit each and are not equal. Equality is an AND over the whole word, which is why it costs n XNOR gates plus n − 1 AND gates: 15 gates at eight bits.

    3. A BCD sum of exactly ten, with no carry out

    5 + 5 + 0, and 9 + 1 + 0, and 4 + 6 + 0.

    What happens: the binary sum is 10 = 1010, with carry out 0 and nibble 1010. The carry out is zero, so a condition that tests only the carry out does nothing — and 1010 is not a digit.

    The rule: the correction must fire at ten, so the condition needs the two product terms as well as the carry out. This is the single most common error in the family.

    4. Nineteen, where the nibble looks fine

    9 + 9 + 1 = 19 = 1 0011.

    What happens: the low nibble 0011 is a valid digit, and it is the wrong answer. The carry out is what says the correction is needed. After adding six the nibble becomes 1001, which is 9.

    The rule: the correction always raises the carry. A design that adds six to the nibble but drops the resulting carry out of the second adder returns 3 instead of 19.

    5. The Gray wrap: fifteen to zero

    The Gray words run 0000 for zero up to 1000 for fifteen.

    What happens: 1000 and 0000 differ in exactly one bit, so the list closes into a ring. That sixteenth adjacency is the one a question targets, and it is the same wrap that makes the map's left and right edges adjacent.

    Why it matters: checking only the fifteen consecutive pairs inside the list proves less than the property claims.

    6. Invalid words at a converter's input

    A BCD-to-excess-3 converter is fed 1101.

    What happens: 1101 is not a BCD digit, so the output has no required value. Here — and only here — the six unused words are genuine don't-cares and may be used to simplify the circuit.

    The contrast: at the output of a BCD adder the same six words really occur, so there they are not don't-cares. The same words are don't-cares on one side of a circuit and reachable on the other.

    7. A condition that is exact here and would not be exact elsewhere

    The correction condition C_out + S3S2 + S3S1 equals "sum greater than nine" on all sixteen nibbles, and the page checks exactly that. It is worth knowing why the check is stated over sixteen nibbles rather than over the twenty possible sums: the two product terms see only the low nibble, so they cannot distinguish a sum of 10 from a sum of 26. That does not arise here, because the largest sum is 19. If the circuit were widened to add three digits at once, the same expression would silently mis-fire, and the condition would have to be stated in terms of the full sum instead.

    Seven beliefs that cost marks, and what replaces them

    Each entry gives the belief, why it tempts, the smallest case that defeats it, and the rule to hold instead.

    Misconception 1 — "a comparator can have two outputs high"

    Why it tempts. Three separate gates, drawn independently, look like three independent questions.

    Defeated by. A design that OR-s the two per-bit comparisons without gating on the tie: it sets greater and less together on a word like 1010 against 0101.

    The rule. Of any two numbers exactly one of the three relations holds, so exactly one output is 1. On all four one-bit rows, G + E + L = 1, and that is checkable.

    Misconception 2 — "comparison starts at the least significant bit"

    Why it tempts. Addition starts there, and most of the arithmetic a student has met is addition.

    Defeated by. 1000 against 0111: the low bits of the first word are all zero and of the second all one, so a low-first reading says the first word is smaller. It is larger, 8 against 7.

    The rule. The most significant differing bit decides. That is what place value means.

    Misconception 3 — "the correction fires only when the carry out is 1"

    Why it tempts. A carry out is the visible sign that the nibble overflowed, so it looks like the whole condition.

    Defeated by. 5 + 5 = 1010 with carry out 0. The nibble is not a digit and nothing fires.

    The rule. The condition is "sum greater than nine", which includes ten to fifteen, none of which produce a carry out of a four-bit adder.

    Misconception 4 — "the six unused BCD words are always don't-cares"

    Why it tempts. In a BCD-to-seven-segment decoder they genuinely are, and that is the circuit most students meet first.

    Defeated by. The BCD adder, where all sixteen nibbles occur across the 200 valid inputs. The page counts them: sixteen of sixteen are reachable.

    The rule. Don't-care status is a property of a particular circuit's reachable inputs, not of the code.

    Misconception 5 — "Gray code is binary in a different order"

    Why it tempts. Both are four-bit words for the same sixteen values, and a table of both looks like a relabelling.

    Defeated by. Gray of 4 is 0110, not 0100. It is a different word, produced by a different rule, and the rule is one exclusive-or per bit.

    The rule. Gray is defined by the one-bit-change property, and the exclusive-or formula is a consequence of it, not a synonym.

    Misconception 6 — "encoding and decoding use the same chain"

    Why it tempts. Each is described as "exclusive-or with the bit above", and the phrase is used for both.

    Defeated by. Encoding 1011 gives 1110 by pairing each binary bit with the bit above. Decoding pairs each Gray bit with the binary bit already decoded above, which is a cumulative chain. Applying the encode rule to 1110 gives 1001, not 1011.

    The rule. Encoding is pairwise; decoding is cumulative. The decode chain is one gate deeper at each position.

    Misconception 7 — "adding six changes the value of the answer"

    Why it tempts. Adding a constant to an output normally changes it, so the correction looks like an error being papered over.

    Defeated by. 7 + 5: the nibble is 1100 = 12, and after adding six it is 0010 = 2 with a carry out. The value reported is 12, not 2 and not 18 — because the carry out is part of the answer, and one ten plus two units is twelve.

    The rule. The correction moves six units of value out of the nibble and into the carry, which is worth sixteen. The reported value is unchanged; only its split into digit and carry is repaired.

    How this clause is actually asked

    Five shapes cover the family. Each is given with the reasoning it demands and the approach that loses the mark. No claim is made about how often any of them appears; that would need evidence this page does not have.

    Pattern 1 — count the input pairs

    Shape. "A four-bit comparator is fed all possible pairs. How many pairs give A > B?"

    Reasoning. 4 to the n total, 2 to the n ties, the rest halve. At four bits: 120.

    The losing approach. Counting ties as half the total as well, which gives 128 and 128. The tie count is the one that does not halve.

    Pattern 2 — trace a comparison by hand

    Shape. "Which bit position decides the comparison of A and B?" or "What is the output triple?"

    Reasoning. Write both words aligned, scan from the left, stop at the first difference. Everything below is irrelevant.

    The losing approach. Converting both words to decimal and comparing those, which is correct but slow, and which fails on the versions that ask which bit decided rather than which is larger.

    Pattern 3 — identify a code from a circuit

    Shape. "The circuit below converts Y1Y2Y3 into which code?" — a chain of exclusive-ors with the input feeding one leg of each.

    Reasoning. If each bit is exclusive-ored with the bit above, it is a Gray encoder. If the chain feeds back on itself cumulatively, it is a decoder.

    The losing approach. Checking only the all-zeros and all-ones rows, which are identical in binary and in Gray and therefore distinguish nothing.

    Pattern 4 — a minterm set for a code function

    Shape. Given h = Gray(n) and g = Gray(n+1), which sum-of-minterms describes gi? This is the shape of GATE CSE 2006 Question 40.

    Reasoning. Build the sixteen-row table with h, g and the minterm index, then read the column. The index is the decimal value of h, not of n.

    The losing approach. Indexing the rows by n. The rows are numbered by the input word, and the input word is the Gray code.

    Pattern 5 — the BCD adder's correction

    Shape. "What is the minimum number of gates to detect that the sum exceeds nine?", "Which expression is the correction condition?", or a numeric item on a corrected sum.

    Reasoning. Work out which nibbles are above nine — exactly 1010 to 1111 — then find the smallest set of terms covering them, then remember the carry out for sums of sixteen and above. The two product terms cover 1010 to 1111 between them; the carry out covers the rest.

    The losing approach. Writing "S3" alone because every invalid nibble has S3 = 1. That is true and it is not sufficient: 1000 and 1001 also have S3 = 1 and are valid digits. A condition must be exact, not merely true on the cases you tried.

    The habit that covers all five

    Before computing, name the quantity the question wants: a count of pairs, a deciding bit position, a code name, a minterm set, or a gate expression. Each has its own sanity check — a count of pairs must be a whole number less than 4 to the n, a deciding position must be a bit index, a code name must come from a rule rather than a table lookup, a minterm set must have at most sixteen members, and a correction expression must be false on 0000 to 1001. Most wrong options in this family fail one of those checks without any arithmetic.

    MCQGATE CSE 2006 Q40

    Consider numbers represented in 4-bit Gray code. Let h3h2h1h0 be the Gray code representation of a number n, and let g3g2g1g0 be the Gray code of the value (n+1) modulo 16. Which one of the following functions is correct?

    1. g0(h3h2h1h0) = Σ(1, 2, 3, 6, 10, 13, 14, 15)
    2. g1(h3h2h1h0) = Σ(4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15)
    3. g2(h3h2h1h0) = Σ(2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 13, 15)
    4. g3(h3h2h1h0) = Σ(0, 1, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13)

    Answer: C

    Build the sixteen rows: for each n, write h = Gray(n) and g = Gray((n+1) mod 16). The minterm index is the decimal value of h, because h is the input word. Reading the g2 column where it is 1 gives the indices 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 13 and 15.

    Why the others fail. The g0 column is Σ(0,1,6,7,10,11,12,13) — option A's list is the g3 column and option D's list is the g1 column. The three wrong options are the other three columns, so a single mis-read of which column belongs to which bit lands on a listed option.

    The trap. Indexing the rows by n rather than by h. Both are 0 to 15, so the mistake produces a plausible list rather than an obviously broken one.

    NATOriginal practice question

    A four-bit magnitude comparator is fed every ordered pair of four-bit unsigned words (A, B). How many of the 256 pairs produce A greater than B?

    Answer: 120

    There are 16 × 16 = 256 ordered pairs. Exactly 16 are ties, one equal partner per first word. The remaining 240 split evenly between greater and less, because swapping the words is a bijection between them. So 120.

    Why 128 appears. It halves all 256 and forgets that the tie count does not halve. Why 136 appears. It halves only the non-ties of one word rather than of the pair set.

    MSQOriginal practice question

    An eight-bit magnitude comparator is built by cascading two identical four-bit comparators, with the low nibble stage feeding the high nibble stage. Which statements are correct?

    1. The high nibble comparator's cascade inputs must be driven by the low nibble comparator's outputs.
    2. The low nibble comparator's cascade inputs must be (G, E, L) = (0, 1, 0).
    3. If the low nibble stage's cascade inputs are (0, 0, 0), the eight-bit result can still be correct whenever the high nibbles differ.
    4. The equality output of the eight-bit comparator is the AND of the two stages' equality outputs.

    Answer: A, B and D

    A is the definition of the cascade. B is the identity element: nothing below the low nibble, so the bits below are tied. D holds because equality of the whole word is equality of both halves, and the high stage's equality output is already gated on its cascade input.

    Why C is wrong. With cascade input (0,0,0) the high stage has Ein = 0 before it examines anything, so neither G nor L can be set by the high nibble. It reports "less" on every pair with differing high nibbles, which is wrong on half of them.

    NATOriginal practice question

    The binary word 1011 is converted to four-bit Gray code. Give the resulting Gray word as a decimal number.

    Answer: 14

    g3 = 1, g2 = 1 XOR 0 = 1, g1 = 0 XOR 1 = 1, g0 = 1 XOR 1 = 0. The Gray word is 1110, which is 14 read as an ordinary binary number.

    Why 11 appears. It is the input, unchanged — the answer to a conversion question is never the input. Why 13 appears. It comes from applying the decode chain instead of the encode chain.

    MCQOriginal practice question

    In a one-digit BCD adder built from a four-bit binary adder, a second four-bit adder and a correction network, which one of the following is the correction condition — that is, the condition under which six must be added to the low nibble?

    1. Cout only
    2. S3S2 + S3S1 only
    3. Cout + S3S2 + S3S1
    4. S3 + Cout

    Answer: C

    The condition must mean "sum greater than nine". Sums of 16 and above are caught by the carry out. Sums from 10 to 15 are caught by the two product terms, which between them cover exactly 1010 to 1111.

    Why A fails. 5 + 5 = 1010 has carry out 0 and is not a digit. Why B fails. 9 + 9 + 1 = 19 has a valid-looking nibble 0011 and needs the carry out. Why D fails. S3 is 1 for 1000 and 1001, which are valid digits, so it fires when it should not.

    NATOriginal practice question

    A BCD adder receives digit A = 8, digit B = 7 and carry in = 1. What is the value of the corrected low-nibble output, read as a decimal digit?

    Answer: 6

    8 + 7 + 1 = 16 = 1 0000. Carry out 1, low nibble 0000. Above nine, so add six: 0000 + 0110 = 0110, which is 6, and the carry out stays 1. The answer is one ten and six units.

    Why 0 appears. It is the nibble before correction. Why 16 appears. It is the true sum, not the digit.

    MSQOriginal practice question

    Which statements about four-bit excess-3 code are correct?

    1. The code for digit 0 is 0011.
    2. Inverting all four bits of the code for digit d gives the code for digit 9 − d.
    3. No excess-3 codeword is 0000 or 1111.
    4. Inverting all four bits of the code for digit d gives the code for digit 15 − d.

    Answer: A, B and C

    A: 0 + 3 = 3 = 0011. B: inverting four bits computes 15 minus the value; the value is d + 3, so the result is 12 − d = (9 − d) + 3, the code for 9 − d. C follows from the range: the codewords run from 0011 to 1100.

    Why D is wrong. It confuses the raw four-bit inversion with the code offset. The inversion gives 15 − (d + 3), and that is the code of 9 − d, not of 15 − d.

    MCQOriginal practice question

    Which one of the following best explains why the Karnaugh map's rows and columns are labelled 00, 01, 11, 10 rather than 00, 01, 10, 11?

    1. So that the map contains every input combination exactly once.
    2. So that labels which differ in one bit are placed next to each other, allowing their cells to merge.
    3. So that the map's numeric order matches the minterm numbering.
    4. So that the map works for five and six variables as well as four.

    Answer: B

    The order is a Gray code. Two cells merge exactly when their labels differ in one bit, so the labels that differ in one bit must touch. In counting order, 01 and 10 would sit adjacent while differing in two bits, and every group formed across that edge would be invalid.

    Why A is wrong. Any ordering of the four labels contains each combination once. Why C is wrong. The Gray order is precisely not numeric order. Why D is wrong. Five-variable maps use the same principle but this ordering is not what makes them possible.

    The one-screen revision sheet

    Definitions, formulas, decision procedures and traps. If you remember only this tab, remember the identity element and the constant six.

    Comparator

    G = a AND NOT b  ·  E = a XNOR b  ·  L = NOT a AND bOne gate each. G and L are single rows; E is two rows.

    G' = G OR (E AND a>b)  ·  E' = E AND (a=b)  ·  L' = L OR (E AND a<b)The per-position rule. G, E and L on the right are the state from the higher bits.

    Identity element: (0, 1, 0) — "the bits above are tied". Direction: most significant first. Counts: 2 to the n equal, (4 to the n − 2 to the n)/2 greater, the same less. Equality cost: n XNOR plus n − 1 AND.

    Codes

    Gray(n) = n XOR (n >> 1)Equivalently gi = bi XOR bi+1 with bn = 0.

    bi = gi XOR bi+1Decoding is cumulative: each binary bit needs the binary bit above it, already decoded.

    Properties checked: bijection, exact inverse, consecutive words differ in one bit, and the wrap 15 to 0 differs in one bit. Excess-3: code(d) = binary(d + 3), and inverting it gives code(9 − d). BCD: ten of sixteen words used; 1010 to 1111 are not digits.

    BCD adder

    correction = Cout + S3S2 + S3S1Exactly "sum greater than nine": the carry out for 16 and above, the two products for 10 to 15.

    corrected = low nibble + 6 × correctionSix is 16 − 10. The correction always raises the carry out of the second adder.

    Range: true sum 0 to 19 over 200 inputs. All sixteen nibbles occur, so 1010 to 1111 are reachable here and are not don't-cares.

    Decision procedure, four lines

    1. Name the input word and the output word.
    2. Name the small state that summarises the bits already seen.
    3. Write the per-position rule and the identity element that starts the chain.
    4. Check the rule on every input the circuit can receive — not on the example you chose.

    The traps, in one list

    Comparing from the least significant bit. Feeding a cascaded stage anything but (0, 1, 0). Reporting equality by OR-ing per-bit equalities instead of AND-ing them. Using the decode chain to encode. Treating 1010 to 1111 as don't-cares at an adder output. Firing the correction only on the carry out, which misses ten to fifteen. Dropping the carry raised by the correction. And answering with the intermediate nibble instead of the digit-and-carry pair the question asked for.
