Topic and objective

TOPIC: Deterministic linear-time selection — the k-th smallest key, quickselect and its quadratic worst case, and the median of medians (Blum–Floyd–Pratt–Rivest–Tarjan) that removes it.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given an array and a rank k, explain why selection can be linear when sorting cannot, run quickselect and count its comparisons, prove the counting lemma that gives the median of medians at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side, write and solve the recurrence T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n), and say exactly why groups of five work and groups of three do not.

I read the request as the deterministic algorithm and its proof, not a repeat of the quickselect half of the divide-and-conquer lesson: every comparison quoted on this page is counted by the page’s own implementation, and the counting lemma is checked at every recursive call of five sampled runs.

Prerequisites

  • Asymptotic notation and what a constant factor hides — asymptotic complexity.
  • Recursion trees and the master theorem — divide and conquer, recurrence relations.
  • The quicksort partition: pivot, left side, right side, rank of the pivot — sorting algorithms.
  • Exact comparison counts and the adversary argument — selection lower bounds.
  • Median, rank and order statistic as three names for one idea; a geometric series with ratio below 1.

Five ideas this page establishes

  1. Selection asks for one rank, so the sorting lower bound Ω(n log n) does not apply: the minimum needs exactly n − 1 comparisons.
  2. Quickselect recurses into one side only, so it is linear when the pivot is good — and quadratic when it is not (2,016 comparisons at n = 64 on sorted input).
  3. Sorting each group of five and taking the median of the medians costs a fifth of the input and gives a pivot with at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side.
  4. That guarantee makes the recursion shrink by a factor of 0.9 per level, so T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n) is O(n) — at most 24n comparisons by substitution, about 7n measured.
  5. Groups of three give 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 exactly, so the levels stop shrinking; five is the smallest odd group size whose coefficients sum below one.

The one sentence worth remembering

A guaranteed good pivot, not a random one, is what turns selection from expected-linear into worst-case linear — and the guarantee is a counting lemma, not a picture.

Intuition first

A shuffled pile of 25 cards is face up, and someone asks for the 13th smallest. You could sort the pile — that answers every rank. Or you could pick one card, deal the rest into a smaller pile and a bigger pile, and notice which pile must contain the 13th smallest. The second route throws away about half the cards without ever ordering them.

The only question is which card to pick. Pick badly — always the smallest card — and each deal removes one card, so the work is quadratic. Pick by a rule that provably lands near the middle, and the pile halves every time. The median of medians is that rule.

Formal definition: the selection problem

Given n keys in an array and an integer k with 1 ≤ k ≤ n, return the key whose rank is k, that is, the key with exactly k − 1 keys smaller than it. k = 1 is the minimum, k = n is the maximum, and k = ⌊(n+1)/2⌋ is the median.

The cost model throughout is the comparison model: only comparisons between keys are counted, and keys are assumed distinct unless stated. Every number on this page is a count of comparisons in that model.

Verified fact The problem and the algorithm are CLRS chapter 9 (“Medians and Order Statistics”), section 9.3, whose SELECT is the median of medians.

Quickselect, precisely

PARTITION around a pivot p moves every key below p to its left and every key above p to its right, and returns the rank r of p. Then:

  • if r = k, return p;
  • if k < r, recurse on the left part with the same k;
  • if k > r, recurse on the right part with rank k − r.

Exactly one recursive call happens. That is the whole difference from quicksort, and it is why the total is a single path down the tree instead of a level-by-level sweep.

Median of medians, in five steps

  1. Break the n keys into ⌈n/5⌉ groups of five, by position. The last group may be short. — costs no comparisons.
  2. Sort each group of five and take its median. — at most 7 comparisons per group, a constant.
  3. Collect the ⌈n/5⌉ group medians and select their median x by the same algorithm. — costs T(n/5).
  4. Partition the whole array around x, giving the rank r of x. — costs n comparisons.
  5. If r = k return x; otherwise recurse on one side, as quickselect does. — costs at most T(7n/10 + 6).

Why a group median helps at all

A group median is the third smallest of its own five keys, so three of those five keys are at or above it. Order the group medians; at least half of them are at or above their own median x. Half the groups, three keys each, is a fixed fraction of the array that is provably at or above x.

That is the entire mechanism: the median of medians is a sample chosen so that each sampled key drags two or three other keys with it. A random pivot has the same effect on average; this one has it every time.

Symbols, in words

n is the number of keys in the current call. k is the rank being asked for. g is the group size, 5 by default. T(n) is the number of comparisons used by a call on n keys. 3n/10 − 6 is the guaranteed number of keys on each side of the median of medians. 7n/10 + 6 is the largest number that can remain for the recursive call. Θ and O compare growth rates, ignoring constants.

Worked example 1 — the full median-of-medians call on 25 keys

23 8 41 3 17 | 35 12 29 6 44 | 19 2 38 25 10 | 31 14 47 5 21 | 36 9 27 16 42    k = 13

  1. Groups of five, by position. The five groups are the five blocks above; nothing is sorted yet. — 0 comparisons.
  2. Each group’s median. Sorting the blocks gives medians 17, 29, 19, 21, 27. — at most 7 comparisons each, so at most 35 here.
  3. The median of the medians. Sorting 17, 29, 19, 21, 27 gives 17, 19, 21, 27, 29, so x = 21. — a recursive call on 5 keys.
  4. Partition around 21. Twelve keys are smaller (2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19) and twelve are larger (23, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 36, 38, 41, 42, 44, 47). — 25 comparisons.
  5. Compare ranks. The pivot has rank 13, and k = 13, so the answer is 21 and there is no second recursive call. — the whole call cost 65 comparisons, which is 2.6n.

Worked example 2 — quickselect on sorted input

Array 0, 1, 2, …, 15 with the first element as pivot and k = 16.

Every partition puts the pivot at rank 1 and sends all remaining keys right, so the array shrinks by exactly one key per level: 15 comparisons, then 14, down to 1.

15 + 14 + … + 1 = 120 = n(n−1)/2

At n = 64 the same input costs 2,016 comparisons for k = 64, and 1,520 for the middle. The middle is cheaper but still quadratic.

Worked example 3 — the recurrence by substitution

Assume T(m) ≤ c·m for all m < n. Then

T(n) ≤ c(n/5) + c(7n/10 + 6) + a·n = 0.9cn + 7c + an

This is at most cn provided 7c + an ≤ 0.1cn, that is c ≥ 10a(n)/(n − 70). For n > 140 the factor is below 2, so c = 20a works.

With this implementation’s per-element cost a = 2.4 (7/5 for the medians plus 1 for the partition), the bound is 24n comparisons. The measured count is 7.20n at n = 4,096, because real inputs do not hit the worst split at every level.

Worked example 4 — measured comparison counts

This page’s own implementation, on shuffled arrays, selecting the median:

  • n = 25: 103 comparisons (4.12n)
  • n = 100: 604 comparisons (6.04n)
  • n = 257: 1,718 comparisons (6.68n)
  • n = 1,000: 7,107 comparisons (7.11n)
  • n = 4,096: 29,505 comparisons (7.20n)

Worked example 5 — the lemma, counted

For the 25-key array above, the pivot 21 has 12 keys below and 12 above, against a bound of 3·25/10 − 6 = 1.5. The bound is weak at n = 25; it is asymptotic.

At n = 1,000 the bound is 294 keys per side, and the surviving subproblem is at most 706 keys. The implementation was checked at every call of five sampled runs (n = 60, 101, 255, 500, 999) and the smallest slack over the bound was 6.3 keys, so the inequality never failed.

Worked example 6 — the group-size arithmetic

Each side keeps (g+1)/(4g) of the array; the pivot step costs 1/g; the surviving work is 1/g + 1 − (g+1)/(4g) = 3(g+1)/(4g).

  • g = 3: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1.0000 — no decay
  • g = 5: 1/5 + 7/10 = 0.9000 — linear
  • g = 7: 1/7 + 5/7 = 0.8571 — linear
  • g = 9: 1/9 + 13/18 = 0.8333 — linear

Boundary: the lemma is vacuous for small n

3n/10 − 6 is negative for n < 20. The recurrence is therefore only useful above a threshold: 7n/10 + 6 < n needs n > 20, and the substitution needs n > 140.

Correct rule: stop the recursion at a constant-size base case (5, or 50 in some texts) and absorb every small n into the constant. A proof that ignores the +6 is not a proof.

The short last group

When n is not a multiple of five the last group has fewer than five keys. Two conventions are used: take the median of whatever keys are there, or drop the group from the median list. Dropping it is why the lemma carries the −6 correction rather than −2.

Either convention keeps the argument valid; mixing them inside one proof is where errors appear.

Duplicates

The lemma counts distinct keys. With repeated keys the partition must classify into less-than, equal and greater-than, and k is answered from the equal block. A two-way partition that sends equal keys to one side still terminates, but the rank arithmetic becomes delicate.

This page’s implementation uses a three-way partition and was checked on every rank of every array up to n = 40 with no mismatch.

Counterexample: the median of medians is not the median

Array: 66 49 94 1 85 | 8 20 75 5 38 | 3 34 60 76 98 | 54 50 73 56 17 | 46 12 4 80 63. The medians are 66, 20, 60, 54, 46 and the median of medians is 54, which has rank 14 among the 25 keys, not 13.

What survives: the pivot still has at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side. Here 13 are below and 11 above, against a bound of 1.5. The guarantee is a band, never an exact rank.

k = 1 and k = n are the exception

On sorted input with the first element as pivot, asking for the minimum returns after one partition, because the pivot is the answer. That is 15 comparisons at n = 16, not 120.

So “quickselect is quadratic on sorted input” is right for the general rank and wrong for the two extremes. State the k you mean.

Expected is not worst case

Randomised quickselect measured 3.25n comparisons on average over 20 seeded runs at n = 1024, with the worst of those runs at 4.61n. Those are observations about the algorithm’s coin flips.

An examiner supplies the array. If the question says worst case, only the deterministic guarantee answers it.

When you need many ranks

Selection is linear for one rank. For all n ranks, n calls would be quadratic, so sorting at Θ(n log n) wins. Selection is the right tool when the question asks for one rank, or for a constant number of them.

This is the boundary between this lesson and sorting.

The comparison model assumption

Every count here assumes keys can only be compared. If keys are small integers, a counting sort of the values can find any rank in Θ(n + range) without comparisons, which does not contradict the lower bound — it steps outside the model.

1. “The median of medians is the median.”

Why it seems reasonable: the name says median twice, and on the 25-key demo array the pivot 21 really is the 13th smallest.

Why it is wrong: a median of five is only the middle of its own group, so the median of five such numbers says nothing exact about the whole array. On the second array on this page the pivot is 54 at rank 14 of 25.

Minimal counterexample: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100 | 5, 6, 7, 8, 101 | 9, 10, 11, 12, 102 | 13, 14, 15, 16, 103 | 17, 18, 19, 20, 104 — medians 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, median of medians 11, rank 11 of 25.

Correct rule: the pivot is guaranteed to sit between the 3n/10 and the 7n/10 positions. That band is all the proof uses and all it needs.

GATE-style question: practice Q10. Visual: scene 6 shows the lucky case and names the unlucky one.

2. “Quickselect is O(n) in the worst case.”

Why it seems reasonable: randomised quickselect is fast in every experiment, and the average is linear.

Why it is wrong: a pivot chosen by position can be the extreme key on every level. Sorted input with the first element as pivot and k = n costs 63 + 62 + … + 1 = 2,016 comparisons at n = 64.

Correct rule: quickselect is Θ(n) expected and Θ(n²) worst case. Median of medians is Θ(n) in both.

GATE-style question: practice Q5.

3. “Groups of three work, just with a worse constant.”

Why it seems reasonable: smaller groups mean more groups, and more groups feel like more information.

Why it is wrong: with g = 3 each side keeps 1/3, the pivot step costs another 1/3, and 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 exactly, so the recursion tree has log n levels of full-size work: Θ(n log n).

Honest qualification: that recurrence is an upper bound. The claim that groups of three force superlinear time is not proven; Chen and Dumitrescu (2014) show the usual argument falls short and give linear-time variants with smaller groups.

Correct rule: the standard analysis needs the two coefficients to sum below 1, and 5 is the smallest odd g for which they do.

4. “Both sides are 3n/10, so the recursion is on 6n/10.”

Why it seems reasonable: the lemma mentions 3n/10 twice, and it is tempting to add the two.

Why it is wrong: the two sides partition the same n keys, so they sum to n. Only one side recurses, and the larger one holds at most 7n/10 + 6 keys.

Correct rule: one recursive call of size at most 7n/10 + 6, plus the pivot call of size n/5.

5. “O(n) selection contradicts the Ω(n log n) lower bound.”

Why it seems reasonable: sorting is Ω(n log n) and selection looks like sorting a little.

Why it is wrong: the lower bound counts the leaves a decision tree needs. Sorting must separate n! orderings; selection must separate only n outcomes, and n − 1 comparisons settle the minimum.

Correct rule: quote a lower bound together with the problem it bounds.

6. “Median of medians is what you should code.”

Why it seems reasonable: it has the better worst-case bound.

Why it is wrong: the constants are large — about 7n comparisons measured here against 3.25n for randomised quickselect — and the algorithm exists to make the worst case provable, not to be fast in practice.

Correct rule: use randomised or median-of-three quickselect in code; use median of medians when the question asks for a worst-case guarantee.

Which question categories apply

A — direct definition: the recurrence of SELECT, or the definition of the selection problem. B — property verification: is a given recurrence a correct upper bound for this pivot rule? C — calculation: a comparison count, or 3n/10 − 6 for a given n. E — counterexample reasoning: sorted input defeats a positional pivot. F — multiple conditions: an MSQ mixing worst case, expected case and the guarantee. H — multi-step reasoning: a pivot rule given in words, then a recurrence, then a class.

D — construction and G — misconception trap appear less often here, because GATE rarely asks you to build the algorithm; it asks what the guarantee implies.

The four shapes the question takes

  1. A pivot rule in words → write the recurrence → name the class.
  2. A cost given for one step → add it to the recurrence and absorb it.
  3. A nested structure (n arrays of n keys) → multiply the per-part cost by the count.
  4. A comparison count for a stated input → count levels and add.

The commonest wrong approach

Reaching for the master theorem first. T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n) has two recursive terms of different sizes, so the master theorem does not apply; the recursion-tree or substitution argument does. Write the level sums, see the ratio, and only then name the class.

Decision procedure for an unseen question

  1. Underline the guarantee: how many keys on each side, or what fraction?
  2. Write the two recursive sizes as fractions of n, plus the per-level cost.
  3. Add the two fractions. Below 1 → linear; equal to 1 → n log n; above 1 → polynomial.
  4. Check what is being counted: comparisons, time, or a number of calls.
  5. Check whether the question says worst case, expected, or amortised.

Closely confused pairs

Selection vs sorting: one rank vs all ranks; n − 1 vs Ω(n log n) for the extreme rank.

Expected vs worst case: over the algorithm’s coin flips vs over every input.

Median of medians vs median: a guaranteed band vs an exact rank.

Recursive call vs both calls: quickselect makes one, quicksort makes two.

MCQ Original practice

What does the selection problem ask for?

  1. The k-th smallest key of n keys, for a given k.
  2. All n keys in non-decreasing order.
  3. The median of every prefix of the array.
  4. The k most frequent keys.

Answer: (A). Selection returns one key of rank k; the median is the special case k = ⌊(n+1)/2⌋.

(B) is sorting. (C) is a different problem that needs more than one rank. (D) is frequency analysis, which needs a hash table, not an order statistic.

NAT Original practice

In the comparison model, what is the minimum number of comparisons needed to find the minimum of 100 distinct keys?

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 99. Every key except the minimum must lose a comparison, and one comparison produces at most one loser, so n − 1 = 99 comparisons are necessary; a single scan attains it.

100 is the count if you compare each key with a running minimum including the first. 50 is the pairing trick for the minimum and maximum together, which is a different question.

MCQ Original practice

Which recurrence correctly bounds the comparisons used by median of medians with groups of five?

  1. T(n) ≤ 2T(n/2) + O(n)
  2. T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n)
  3. T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(4n/5) + O(n)
  4. T(n) ≤ 2T(n/5) + O(n)

Answer: (B). One call on the n/5 medians, one call on the surviving side of at most 7n/10 + 6 keys, and linear work for groups, medians and the partition.

(A) is balanced quicksort. (C) is the answer to the GATE 2008 pivot question, where a sort recurses on both sides. (D) forgets that the second call is on the whole surviving side, not on the medians again.

NAT Original practice

For n = 250 keys, the counting lemma guarantees at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side of the median of medians. What is that number?

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 69. 3 × 250/10 = 75, and 75 − 6 = 69.

75 forgets the −6 correction for the pivot’s own group and a possibly short last group. 125 is half the array, which no argument here guarantees.

NAT Original practice

Quickselect uses the first element as pivot on the already sorted array 0, 1, …, 63 and is asked for k = 64. How many comparisons does it make?

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 2016. Each partition puts the pivot at rank 1 and leaves 63, then 62, …, 1 keys, so the total is 63 + 62 + … + 1 = 64 × 63/2.

64 × 63 = 4,032 double-counts the pairs. 1,520 is the cost of the middle rank on the same input, which the model measures separately.

NAT Original practice

Median of medians is called on n = 125 keys with groups of five. How many keys does the recursive call that finds the pivot operate on?

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 25. There are 125/5 = 25 groups, one median each, and the pivot is the median of those 25 medians.

125 is the outer call. 5 is the group size, not the number of medians. 6 is the number of keys the recursive call might keep on one side, which is not what was asked.

MCQ GATE CSE 2019

There are n unsorted arrays A1, A2, …, An, with n odd. Each contains n distinct elements, and no element is common to two arrays. The worst-case time complexity of computing the median of the medians of A1, …, An is:

  1. O(n)
  2. O(n log n)
  3. O(n²)
  4. Ω(n² log n)

Answer: (C) O(n²). One median of an unsorted array of n keys costs Θ(n) by selection, and there are n arrays, so collecting the n medians costs Θ(n²). The median of those n medians is one more selection, Θ(n), which is dominated.

(B) is the trap: it assumes each array is sorted, but the arrays are unsorted, so a median is a selection, not a lookup. (D) confuses the answer with a lower bound, and nothing here forces a log factor.

Provenance: verified GATE CSE 2019 question, item 29 of the paper as reproduced. Options as printed.

MCQ GATE CSE 2008 · Q43

Consider quicksort. Suppose there is a procedure for finding a pivot element which splits the list into two sub-lists, each of which contains at least one-fifth of the elements. Let T(n) be the number of comparisons required to sort n elements. Then:

  1. T(n) ≤ 2T(n/5) + n
  2. T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(4n/5) + n
  3. T(n) ≤ 2T(4n/5) + n
  4. T(n) ≤ 2T(n/2) + n

Answer: (B). Each sub-list has between n/5 and 4n/5 elements, so the worst case is the pair (n/5, 4n/5), and a sort recurses on both. The partition costs n.

(A) and (C) assume both parts have the same size, which the guarantee does not give. (D) assumes a perfect split, which is stronger than “at least one-fifth”.

Provenance: verified GATE CSE 2008 Q43. Note that 1/5 + 4/5 = 1, so this recurrence is Θ(n log n), not linear — the same arithmetic that rules out groups of three for selection.

MCQ GATE CSE 2006 · Q52

The median of n elements can be found in O(n) time. Which one of the following is correct about the complexity of quicksort in which the median is selected as pivot?

  1. Θ(n)
  2. Θ(n log n)
  3. Θ(n²)
  4. Θ(n³)

Answer: (B) Θ(n log n), in all three cases. The pivot always splits the array into two halves, so T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n), which is Θ(n log n) best, average and worst. The O(n) pivot search is absorbed: n log n + n is still n log n.

(A) is impossible: the output is n keys in order, so Ω(n log n) comparisons are needed. (C) is the unimproved worst case with a bad pivot rule. (D) over-counts the pivot search, which is paid once per level, not once per comparison.

Provenance: verified GATE CSE 2006 Q52.

MSQ Original practice

Which of the following statements are true? (Select all that apply.)

  1. The median of medians of n keys always has rank ⌊(n+1)/2⌋.
  2. It has at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side.
  3. Median of medians is Θ(n) in the worst case, and quickselect is Θ(n) only in expectation.
  4. With groups of three the standard analysis gives Θ(n log n).

Answer: (B), (C) and (D). (B) is the counting lemma; (C) is the point of the algorithm; (D) follows because 1/3 + 2/3 = 1.

(A) is the misconception: the pivot is only guaranteed to lie in the middle band. On the second array of this page it has rank 14 of 25.

MCQ Original practice

An array of 25 keys has group medians 66, 20, 60, 54 and 46, so the median of medians is 54. Which statement about the partition around 54 is necessarily true?

  1. Exactly 12 keys are smaller and 12 larger.
  2. At least 1.5 keys are on each side, so the guarantee says nothing useful here.
  3. At least 3n/10 − 6 = 1.5 keys lie on each side, and in this array 13 lie below and 11 above.
  4. The pivot must be the 13th smallest, because 54 is a median of medians.

Answer: (C). The lemma gives 1.5 keys per side at n = 25, which is weak but true; the actual counts for this array are 13 below and 11 above.

(A) is the demo array’s count, not this one. (B) draws the wrong conclusion: a weak bound is still a bound, and it becomes strong as n grows. (D) is the exact-rank misconception, and it is false here: the rank is 14.

NAT Original practice

With groups of seven, the largest fraction of the input the algorithm can recurse on is 1/7 + 5/7. Express that fraction as a decimal to two places.

Answer as a decimal, tolerance 0.01.

Answer: 0.86 (exactly 6/7 = 0.857…). Each side keeps (g+1)/(4g) = 8/28 = 2/7, so the surviving side is 5/7, and the pivot step costs another 1/7.

0.71 is 5/7 alone, which omits the pivot call. 0.90 is the groups-of-five figure, not groups-of-seven.

Definitions in one line each

  • Selection: return the key of rank k.
  • Rank: the number of keys smaller than this one, plus one.
  • Quickselect: partition, then recurse into one side.
  • Median of medians: sort groups of five, take their medians, select the median of those medians, partition around it.
  • Counting lemma: that pivot has at least 3n/10 − 6 keys on each side.
  • Survivor fraction: 3(g+1)/(4g) of the input remains after one level.

Formulas and their conditions

  • Minimum: n − 1 comparisons, necessary and sufficient (distinct keys, comparison model).
  • Median of five: at most 7 comparisons (4 to bracket, 3 to finish).
  • Quickselect worst case: n(n−1)/2 comparisons (positional pivot, adversarial input).
  • Median of medians: T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10 + 6) + O(n) ⇒ T(n) = O(n); at most 24n comparisons by substitution.
  • Group size g: each side (g+1)/(4g), survivor 3(g+1)/(4g); below 1 exactly when g ≥ 5.
  • GATE 2008 shape: T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(4n/5) + n ⇒ Θ(n log n).

Decision procedure

  1. Read the guarantee, not the story: how much is on each side?
  2. Write both recursive sizes as fractions of n.
  3. Add them: below 1 → linear; equal to 1 → n log n.
  4. Ask what is counted: comparisons, time, or calls.
  5. Ask which case: worst, expected, amortised.

Traps, each with its counterexample

  • “The pivot is the median” → the second array on this page: rank 14 of 25.
  • “Quickselect is linear” → sorted input, k = n, 2,016 comparisons at n = 64.
  • “Groups of three only cost more” → 1/3 + 2/3 = 1, no decay.
  • “Both sides are 3n/10” → they sum to n; one side is at most 7n/10 + 6.
  • “Selection cannot beat n log n” → the minimum needs n − 1.
  • “The +6 does not matter” → 3n/10 − 6 is negative below n = 20.

Six quick revision questions, answers inverted

1. Which problem has the lower bound Ω(n log n): sorting or selecting the median? — sorting.

2. How many comparisons does the median of medians guarantee on each side? — at least 3n/10 − 6 keys, not comparisons.

3. What are the two recursive sizes in SELECT? — n/5 and at most 7n/10 + 6.

4. Why does 9/10 matter? — it is the ratio of successive level costs, so the series is geometric.

5. What does g = 3 give? — coefficients summing to 1, hence Θ(n log n) by the recurrence.

6. Is median of medians used in practice? — rarely: the constants are large; it exists for the worst-case guarantee.
