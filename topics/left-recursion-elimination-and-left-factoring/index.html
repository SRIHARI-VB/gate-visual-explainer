GATE Visual Explainer Eliminating left recursion and left factoring
All topics Prerequisite: LL(1) and FIRST/FOLLOW Prerequisite: grammars Related: LR(1) and LALR(1) Transcript

Topic and objective

TOPIC: The two grammar transformations a top-down parser needs before any LL(1) table can be built — eliminating left recursion (immediate, indirect and hidden) and left factoring a shared prefix.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a grammar, decide whether it is left recursive and by which of the three routes; rewrite it so that no nonterminal reaches itself on the left; factor a shared prefix to a fixed point; count the productions before and after without writing them out; and state what each transformation preserves and what it does not.

I read the request as the preprocessing step the parsing lesson names and does not build. The prerequisite lesson builds FIRST, FOLLOW and the LL(1) table and says in passing that left-recursion elimination and left factoring are a preprocessing step; this page owns that step.

Prerequisites

  • Context-free grammars — a production, a derivation, a sentential form, and what it means for a grammar to generate a string.
  • LL(1) parsing and FIRST/FOLLOW — FIRST, FOLLOW, the two-branch rule for filling a table cell, and a cell with two entries meaning “not LL(1)”.
  • Reading ε as the empty string, and knowing that a derivation is finite.
  • No calculus, no probability, no counting beyond “one per alternative”.

Nothing on this page needs a compiler. Every grammar is rewritten, and every language compared, in the browser when the page loads.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • Two failures, two cures — a left-recursive rule loops; a shared prefix conflicts. Neither cure is part of the table construction.
  • Repeat on the right — A → Aα | β becomes A → βA′, A′ → αA′ | ε, and the ε is the base case.
  • Count, do not write — m recursive and n base alternatives become n + m + 1 productions.
  • Order matters — indirect recursion is found by a left-corner closure, then removed by substituting earlier nonterminals first.
  • Necessary, not sufficient — a grammar can have neither defect and still conflict. The table decides.

Intuition first: the recipe that starts with itself

A left-recursive rule is a recipe whose first instruction is “follow this recipe”. Educational interpretation

The sentence “a sum is a sum plus a term, or a term” is perfectly good English and a perfectly good description of what a sum is. Read as an instruction it is useless: to build a sum you must first build a sum. Read as a description it is fine, and that gap — a good description, a bad instruction — is the whole topic.

The cure is to describe the same set of sums from the other end: “a sum is a term, then any number of plus-terms”. “Any number” includes zero, and zero is where the empty string comes in.

Definition 1 — left recursion

Write A ⇒ Bγ to mean “A derives, in one or more steps, a sentential form that begins with the symbol B”. A nonterminal A is left recursive when A ⇒ Aγ for some γ.

Three routes reach it:

  • Immediate — a rule of A begins with A: A → Aα.
  • Indirect — the cycle passes through other nonterminals: S → A a, A → S d.
  • Hidden — the recursive symbol is not first, but everything before it can vanish: A → B A a with B nullable.

Mathematical derivation All three are the same condition A ⇒ Aγ; they differ only in how many steps the derivation takes and whether a nullable prefix is crossed.

Definition 2 — the left-corner relation

A reaches B on the left, written A →LC B, when some rule A → X1…Xk has Xj = B and every X1…Xj−1 is a nullable nonterminal. Take the transitive closure.

Then: A is left recursive exactly when A reaches itself in this relation.

Mathematical derivation If A reaches itself, the sequence of jumps spells out A ⇒ Aγ. If A ⇒ Aγ, every step of that derivation starts at a left-corner symbol, so A reaches itself. The nullable-prefix rule is what makes the hidden case visible.

Definition 3 — the direct rewrite

Let A → Aα1 | … | Aαm | β1 | … | βn, where no βi begins with A. Replace those rules by

A → β1A′ | … | βnA′
A′ → α1A′ | … | αmA′ | ε

Read it in words: A produces one base alternative, then the helper A′ produces zero or more copies of a recursive alternative. The count is n + m + 1 productions.

Mathematical derivation The old A generates βiαj1…αjk for any k ≥ 0. The new A generates the same set: one β from its own rules, then k copies of some α from A′, and A′ stops only by taking ε.

Definition 4 — the ordered algorithm

Number the nonterminals A1…An. For i = 1 to n:

  1. For j = 1 to i−1, replace every rule Ai → Ajγ by Ai → δ1γ | … | δkγ, where Aj → δ1 | … | δk are all the rules of Aj.
  2. Remove the immediate left recursion in Ai with Definition 3.

Verified fact The algorithm is Algorithm 4.19 of Aho, Lam, Sethi and Ullman, Compilers: Principles, Techniques and Tools, 2nd edition, stated for a grammar with no cycles and no ε-productions.

Definition 5 — left factoring

If two alternatives of one nonterminal share a non-empty prefix α, replace them by α followed by a helper:

A → αβ1 | αβ2   becomes   A → αA′ ,   A′ → β1 | β2

Take the longest common prefix, and repeat until no two alternatives of any nonterminal share one. The language is unchanged: the same strings, grouped differently.

Mathematical derivation Both sides of the replacement derive αβ1 and αβ2 and nothing else, so no string enters or leaves the language.

The conditions, stated once

The ordered algorithm is stated for a grammar with no cycles and no ε-productions in the input. Its output usually contains ε-productions, which is expected. The 1998 grammar on this page does contain A → ε; the substitution step there is safe because the nonterminal being substituted (S) is not nullable, and the model re-checks the result rather than assuming the textbook guarantee.

Worked example 1 — the direct rewrite, counted and checked

E → E + T | T      T → a

  1. Classify. E has one rule beginning with E, so E is immediately left recursive. T is not. (Justification: read the first symbol of each right side.)
  2. Split the alternatives. Recursive: α = + T. Base: β = T. (m = 1, n = 1.)
  3. Count. n + m + 1 = 3 rules for E, plus T’s one rule: 4 productions, from 3. (The helper is new; nothing else changes.)
  4. Write. E → T E′, E′ → + T E′ | ε, T → a.
  5. Check the language. The model enumerates every string over {a, +} up to length 7 and tests membership by dynamic programming: a, a+a, a+a+a, a+a+a+a — 4 strings, the same 4 both ways.
  6. Check the table. FIRST(E) = FIRST(T) = {a}; FOLLOW(E) = FOLLOW(E′) = {$, +}. No cell holds two rules, so the rewritten grammar is LL(1).

Mathematical derivation Every number above is recomputed on each page load; the same figures appear in the animation’s footer.

Worked example 2 — the count on a four-alternative grammar

S → S a | S b | c | d

m = 2 (a, b), n = 2 (c, d), so 2 + 2 + 1 = 5 productions:

S → c S′ | d S′
S′ → a S′ | b S′ | ε

The slip this catches. Four alternatives look like four rules. The answer is five, because the helper needs its own ε.

Worked example 3 — the empty β is still a base alternative

In A → A c | S d | ε, after substituting S’s rules, the alternatives are A c, A a d, b d and ε. So m = 2 and n = 2, and the base alternatives are b d and ε.

A → b d A′ | A′

The second rule is just the helper, because β was empty. Dropping it would delete the strings in which A derives nothing.

Worked example 4 — GATE CSE 1998 Q6b, in four steps

GATE CSE 1998 · Q6b Verified fact The paper prints S → A a | b, A → A c | S d | ε and asks for an equivalent grammar with no left recursion and the minimum number of production rules.

  1. Order. S, then A. (Any order works if it is followed; this is the one that keeps the count smallest here.)
  2. Substitute. A’s rule A → S d begins with S, whose rules are A a and b, so it becomes A → A a d and A → b d. Nothing else begins with S.
  3. Eliminate. A is now immediately left recursive: α = c, a d; β = b d, ε. That gives 2 + 2 + 1 = 5 rules for A.
  4. Report. S → A a | b; A → b d A′ | A′; A′ → c A′ | a d A′ | ε — 7 productions, from 5.

The model writes this grammar itself and compares it rule for rule with the published answer: identical. It then enumerates every string over {a, b, c, d} up to length 5 — 17 strings — and finds the same 17 for the original grammar, the rewritten grammar and the published answer.

Worked example 5 — left factoring, twice

A → a b c | a b d | a e

  1. Longest shared prefix. a b is shared by the first two alternatives only. So A → a b A′, A′ → c | d, and A → a e stays.
  2. Look again. A now has a e and a b A′, which share a. So factor again: A → a A″, A″ → e | b A′.
  3. Stop. No two alternatives of any nonterminal share a prefix. 3 productions became 5.

The model runs the passes to a fixed point and reports 2 of them; the language is {abc, abd, ae} before and after, and the factored grammar’s LL(1) table has no conflict cell.

Worked example 6 — the hidden case, where the direct rule does nothing

A → B A a | c      B → ε | b

The scan. First symbols of A’s right sides are B and c. Neither is A, so a one-step test reports no left recursion — and applying the direct rule changes nothing at all.

The closure. B is nullable, so A reaches the second symbol of B A a: A reaches A. The grammar is left recursive.

The cure. Substitute B’s right sides into A: A → A a | b A a | c. Now it is immediate, and the direct rule applies: A → b A a A′ | c A′, A′ → a A′ | ε. The model confirms no nonterminal is left recursive afterwards.

What this is a counterexample to. “A grammar with no rule beginning with its own left side is not left recursive.”

1. Deleting the ε empties the language

Take E → T E′, E′ → + T E′ | ε and delete the empty production, as a tidy-up.

What breaks. Every derivation of E must finish by rewriting E′ to nothing, and only the ε does that. Without it every sentential form still contains E′, so no string of terminals is ever produced.

The count. 4 strings up to length 7 with the ε; 0 without. The model enumerates both.

2. A nonterminal with no base alternative

Suppose A → A a | A b with nothing else. Then A generates nothing: every derivation of A leaves an A behind.

What the rewrite does. With n = 0 there is no base alternative, so A gets no production at all and the language stays empty. Renaming A′ back to A instead would hand A the language (a|b)*, which it never had.

The condition. The rewrite is language-preserving because the base alternatives carry the strings; with none, there is nothing to preserve.

3. The rewrite changes the tree, not the language

E → E − T | T parses 10 − 3 − 2 as (10−3)−2 = 5. After the rewrite the tree leans right and the same string evaluates to 10−(3−2) = 9.

The rule. Left recursion is what encodes left associativity. A syntax-directed question about a rewritten grammar is a question about the rewritten tree.

4. One factoring pass is not factoring

A → a b c | a b d | a e needs two passes: after factoring a b, the alternatives a b A′ and a e still share a.

The test. “No two alternatives of any nonterminal share a non-empty prefix” — checked on the result, not on the number of passes you ran.

5. Factoring cannot remove an ambiguity

The dangling else, factored, is S → i E t S S′ | a, S′ → e S | ε. No shared prefix, no left recursion — and the table still holds two rules in M[S′, e], because e is in FIRST(e S) and also in FOLLOW(S′).

The rule. The grammar is ambiguous before and after. Neither transformation touches ambiguity.

6. The ordered algorithm’s input condition

Algorithm 4.19 is stated for a grammar with no cycles and no ε-productions in the input. Its output contains ε-productions, which is normal.

What to do when the input has one. As in GATE CSE 1998 Q6b, the substitution is still safe when the nonterminal being substituted is not nullable — and the result must be checked rather than assumed. The model checks it: no nonterminal is left recursive afterwards.

7. Two nonterminals, one substitution each, wrong order

In S → A a | b, A → A c | S d | ε, rewriting A first and S never is the usual mistake, and it happens to work here. Rewriting S’s rules into S before A is substituted can reintroduce left recursion.

The rule. Substitute only earlier nonterminals into a later one, then remove what is immediate, and finish with the closure test on the result.

8. Neither defect, still not LL(1)

S → A | B, A → a, B → a. No rule begins with its own left side; no two alternatives of one nonterminal share a prefix. And M[S, a] holds both S → A and S → B.

What it is a counterexample to. “No left recursion plus no common prefix means LL(1).” It is the witness for GATE CSE 2003 Q16.

1. “Removing left recursion makes the grammar LL(1)”

Why it tempts. The rewrite is taught as the step before the table, so it feels like the fix.

Counterexample. A → a b c | a b d | a e has no left recursion and is not LL(1) until it is factored. The dangling else has neither defect after factoring and is still not LL(1).

Correct rule. Removing left recursion is a necessary condition. Build the table and count the two-entry cells.

2. “The ε in the helper is optional tidying”

Why it tempts. It looks like a redundant empty rule, and “simplify” is a habit.

Counterexample. Delete E′ → ε from E → T E′, E′ → + T E′ | ε and the language is empty: 4 strings become 0.

Correct rule. The ε is the base case of “zero or more”. Keep it.

3. “No rule begins with its own left side, so there is no left recursion”

Why it tempts. The direct rule is defined by exactly that shape, so the shape becomes the definition.

Counterexample. S → A a | b, A → A c | S d | ε is left recursive through S ⇒ A a ⇒ S d a. And A → B A a with B nullable hides the recursion behind a nullable symbol.

Correct rule. Left recursion is A ⇒ Aγ. Test it with the left-corner closure.

4. “The language is unchanged, so the computed value is unchanged”

Why it tempts. “Equivalent grammar” is repeated in every statement of the transformation, and equivalence is about the language.

Counterexample. 10 − 3 − 2 is 5 under the left-recursive grammar and 9 under the rewritten one.

Correct rule. Language preserved, trees changed. A syntax-directed translation reads the tree.

5. “The primes are part of the answer’s meaning”

Why it tempts. A′ and A″ look like they encode nesting.

Counterexample. In the factoring example the model creates A′ on the first pass and A″ on the second, so the outer helper ends up double-primed: A → a A″, A″ → e | b A′.

Correct rule. The primes record the order the passes ran. Renaming them is free: A → a X, X → e | b Y, Y → c | d is the same grammar.

6. “Factoring is what removes the conflict in the dangling else”

Why it tempts. Factoring visibly removes the duplicated i E t S, and the table looks tidier.

Counterexample. The factored grammar still puts S′ → e S and S′ → ε in the same cell, because FOLLOW(S′) contains e.

Correct rule. The second conflict comes from the ambiguity, not from the prefix. Only a different grammar (matched and unmatched statements) removes it.

The five question shapes, and what each one demands

Every shape below is answered by one of four procedures: the closure test, the direct rewrite, the ordered algorithm, or a FIRST/FOLLOW table. Naming the shape first is what stops a count question being answered with a grammar.

Shape 1 — “rewrite this grammar”

Give the equivalent grammar with no left recursion, sometimes with the minimum number of rules.

Procedure. Order the nonterminals, substitute earlier into later, remove what is immediate, count with n + m + 1, and check the language is unchanged.

Common wrong approach. Rewriting the nonterminal that looks recursive and stopping — which leaves the indirect case in place.

Shape 2 — “how many productions”

A count, usually with a phrase like “minimum number of rules”.

Procedure. n + m + 1 for each rewritten nonterminal, plus the untouched rules. Only rewritten nonterminals change size.

Common wrong approach. Counting the alternatives of the original and reporting that number; forgetting the helper’s ε; applying the formula to the whole grammar.

Shape 3 — “which grammar is LL(1)”

Often an MSQ listing statements about LL(1) grammars.

Procedure. Build FIRST and FOLLOW, fill the table with the two-branch rule, and count cells with two entries. No conflict means LL(1).

Common wrong approach. Deciding from the shape of the rules. S → A | B, A → a, B → a has no left recursion and no common prefix, and is not LL(1).

Shape 4 — “what does this transformation preserve”

A statement question: language, ambiguity, determinism, parse trees, associativity.

Procedure. Both transformations preserve the language. Neither preserves the parse trees (associativity changes), and neither removes ambiguity.

Common wrong approach. Assuming everything except the rules’ shape is preserved, including the tree.

Shape 5 — “is this grammar suitable for predictive parsing”

Sometimes phrased as “why is this grammar unsuitable”.

Procedure. Check left recursion by the closure, check for a shared prefix, then check the table. Report the first defect that applies, with its name.

Common wrong approach. Answering “because it is ambiguous” without showing a string with two trees, or “because it is left recursive” without showing the derivation.

The four-step decision procedure

1 closure test → which nonterminals reach themselves?
2 if any: order, substitute, eliminate, count with n + m + 1
3 factor to a fixed point, longest prefix first
4 build the table and count two-entry cells — that verdict is the answer

MCQGATE CSE 2003 · Q16

Which of the following suffices to convert an arbitrary context-free grammar to an LL(1) grammar?

  1. Removing left recursion alone
  2. Factoring the grammar alone
  3. Removing left recursion and factoring the grammar
  4. None of the above

Answer: D

Neither transformation removes ambiguity, and an ambiguous grammar is never LL(1). The dangling else on this page is the witness: factored and free of left recursion, it still puts two rules in M[S′, e].

Why C is wrong. S → A | B, A → a, B → a has neither defect and M[S, a] still holds two rules. The model computes one conflict cell for it.

Trap. The word “arbitrary” is doing the work. For a given grammar the two transformations may well be enough; the question is about every grammar.

NATGATE CSE 1998 · Q6b

For S → A a | b, A → A c | S d | ε, what is the minimum number of production rules in an equivalent grammar with no left recursion?

Answer: 7

Two rules of S are untouched. A becomes A → b d A′ | A′ and A′ → c A′ | a d A′ | ε, which is 2 + 2 + 1 = 5. Total 7.

Trap. Answering 5 by counting only A’s rules. Trap. Answering 6 by folding the empty β away. The model derives the grammar and counts it.

NATOriginal practice

After eliminating the left recursion in S → S a | S b | c | d, how many productions does the grammar have in total?

Answer: 5

m = 2, n = 2, so n + m + 1 = 5: S → c S′ | d S′ and S′ → a S′ | b S′ | ε.

Why 4 is wrong. Four alternatives become five, because the helper needs its empty production. Why 6 is wrong. That would be n + m + 2, which happens if you keep the original four rules as well.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following statements are true for the transformation that eliminates left recursion from E → E + T | T?

  1. It preserves the set of strings generated.
  2. It preserves the parse trees.
  3. It introduces a new nonterminal with an ε-production.
  4. It makes the grammar LL(1).

Answer: A and C

The language is the same 4 strings up to length 7, and E′ is a new nonterminal whose last alternative is ε. Both are computed by the model.

Why B is wrong. The trees change: the operator now associates to the right. Why D is wrong. This particular grammar does become LL(1), but that is a fact about this grammar, not about the transformation — the dangling else and S → A | B, A → a, B → a show the general claim fails.

MCQOriginal practice

Which grammar is left recursive only because of a nullable nonterminal?

  1. A → A a | b
  2. A → B A a | c, B → ε | b
  3. A → a A | b
  4. A → a b | a c

Answer: B

B derives ε, so A ⇒ B A a ⇒ A a. The left-corner closure jumps over the nullable B and finds A reaching itself; the one-step scan finds nothing.

Why A is wrong. Immediate, visible in one rule. Why C is wrong. Right recursive, not left recursive at all. Why D is wrong. Neither: no recursion and no shared prefix.

NATOriginal practice

How many productions does A → a b c | a b d | a e have after left factoring to a fixed point?

Answer: 5

Pass 1 gives A → a b A′, A → a e, A′ → c | d — four rules. Pass 2 gives A → a A″, A″ → e | b A′, and A′ keeps two — five rules.

Why 4 is wrong. One pass leaves a e and a b A′ sharing a. Trap. Factoring, unlike the direct rewrite, adds one rule per extra helper and removes one per factor, so the count must be run pass by pass.

MCQOriginal practice

Deleting E′ → ε from E → T E′, E′ → + T E′ | ε, T → a changes the language to

  1. the strings with at least one +
  2. the empty language
  3. the same language
  4. only the string a

Answer: B

Without the ε, E′ can never be removed from a sentential form, so no derivation reaches a string of terminals. The model enumerates 4 strings with the ε and 0 without.

Why A is tempting. “One or more” is what removing “zero” usually leaves — but here the helper can never finish at all, because every one of its rules keeps an E′. Why D is wrong. a needs E′ to vanish, which is exactly what the ε was for.

MSQOriginal practice

For S → A a | b, A → A c | S d | ε, which statements are true?

  1. S is left recursive.
  2. A is left recursive.
  3. Rewriting A alone removes every left recursion.
  4. The original grammar is LL(1).

Answer: A and B

The left-corner closure reports both: S reaches A and A reaches S, so each reaches itself. S ⇒ A a ⇒ S d a shows it for S in two steps.

Why C is wrong. Rewriting A while S still has S → A a leaves S reaching itself through A. Why D is wrong. A left-recursive grammar is never LL(1), and the model finds conflict cells in its table.

NATOriginal practice

With a = 10, b = 3, c = 2, what value does a − b − c have when parsed by the grammar obtained from E → E − T | T by eliminating left recursion?

Answer: 9

The rewritten grammar associates to the right, so the tree is 10 − (3 − 2) = 10 − 1 = 9. The left-recursive grammar gives (10 − 3) − 2 = 5.

Trap. Answering 5 from arithmetic habit. The question is about the tree the rewritten grammar produces, not about what the expression means in mathematics.

MCQOriginal practice

The factored dangling-else grammar S → i E t S S′ | a, S′ → e S | ε is

  1. LL(1), because the shared prefix has been removed
  2. not LL(1), because M[S′, e] holds two productions
  3. LL(1) but ambiguous
  4. not LL(1), because S is still left recursive

Answer: B

FIRST(e S) = {e} and e is also in FOLLOW(S′), so the empty alternative applies on e as well. The model reports exactly one conflict cell, at S′ and e.

Why A is wrong. The prefix conflict is gone; a different conflict remains. Why C is wrong. No LL(1) grammar is ambiguous. Why D is wrong. This grammar has no left recursion at all.

Definitions, one line each

  • Left recursive: A ⇒ Aγ for some γ.
  • Immediate: a rule A → Aα exists.
  • Indirect: the cycle passes through another nonterminal.
  • Hidden: A → B A a with B nullable.
  • Left corner: A reaches B when a rule of A has B at the first non-nullable position.
  • Left factored: no two alternatives of any nonterminal share a non-empty prefix.

Formulas

A → Aα1…|Aαm | β1…|βn
⇓
A → β1A′|…|βnA′
A′ → α1A′|…|αmA′ | ε

A → αβ1 | αβ2  ⇓  A → αA′ , A′ → β1 | β2

Count: n + m + 1 productions for each rewritten nonterminal.

Assumptions to state

  • The ordered algorithm assumes no cycles and no ε-productions in the input.
  • Language preservation needs at least one base alternative β.
  • Factoring uses the longest common prefix, and repeats.
  • LL(1) needs no left recursion, no shared prefix, and a conflict-free table — the first two are necessary, the third is the test.

The four counterexamples worth carrying in

  • S → A | B, A → a, B → a — neither defect, not LL(1).
  • Factored dangling else — neither defect, not LL(1).
  • A → B A a, B → ε | b — left recursive with no rule beginning with A.
  • 10 − 3 − 2 — 5 before the rewrite, 9 after.

The one screen

closure test → order, substitute, eliminate → count n+m+1 → factor to a fixed point → build the table → count two-entry cells
preserved: the language   changed: the parse trees   untouched: ambiguity
