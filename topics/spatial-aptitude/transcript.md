## Scene 1. What a spatial question actually asks

*0:00 – 0:30 · objective: Frame the four shape operations the syllabus names, and see that every question is settled by a written test rather than by staring at the picture.*

**0:00–0:07** — A spatial question almost always shows you one figure and then its image, and asks what happened in between. The picture is the data. The operation is the answer.

> Caption: A spatial question shows one figure and its image, and asks which operation produced the second.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The options are often four pictures, not four words. Eliminate with a property, never by feel.*

**0:07–0:15** — The syllabus names the operations exactly: translation, rotation, scaling and mirroring, plus assembling and grouping. Grouping is not a fifth operation; it is the same four applied to pieces instead of a whole.

> Caption: The syllabus names four operations on shapes: translation, rotation, scaling and mirroring.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Assembling and grouping are listed as operations, so a question can ask which pieces combine, not only which single move was made.*

**0:15–0:22** — Then two families follow from those four. Paper folding and cutting are just reflections, applied several times in order. And patterns in two and three dimensions ask the same question of a solid: which face, which view, how many pieces.

> Caption: Two more families follow from them: paper folding and cutting, and patterns in two and three dimensions.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A folding question is a mirroring question with a stack attached. Solve it with reflections, not with imagination.*

**0:22–0:30** — And the important part: every question in this area can be answered by a written test. The order of three corners. A dimension and a layer count. A product. Two counts of colour. This lesson is those tests.

> Caption: Every question here is answerable by a written test, not by guessing at a picture.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Answering by intuition is what the options are designed to punish. Write the test down.*

## Scene 2. Translation, rotation, scaling: what each one keeps

*0:30 – 1:00 · objective: Separate the three operations that preserve the figure from the one that reverses it, and name the invariant each one preserves.*

**0:30–0:37** — A translation slides the figure. Every point moves by the same amount, so no distance and no angle changes. Size stays, orientation stays, only the position moves.

> Caption: A translation slides the figure. Nothing about its shape, size or orientation changes.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A translation is the only operation that changes nothing but position, so it is the first thing to rule out.*

**0:37–0:45** — A rotation turns the figure about a point. Distances are preserved, so the shape and the size survive exactly. What changes is which way the figure faces.

> Caption: A rotation turns it about a point. Size and shape survive; the figure now faces a different way.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A rotation of a square by ninety degrees looks like the square, which is why squares are the worst example to reason from.*

**0:45–0:52** — A scaling multiplies every distance by the same number. The angles all survive, the lengths do not. And the area changes by that factor squared, which is where the area questions come from.

> Caption: A scaling multiplies every distance by the same factor. Angles survive, lengths do not.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Area scales as the square of the length factor, so doubling the side multiplies the area by four, not by two.*

**0:52–1:00** — Only the mirror reverses the figure. Take three corners and read them clockwise: translation, rotation and scaling all leave that order alone. A mirror swaps it. And no turning in the plane ever puts it back.

> Caption: Only a mirror reverses the figure, and that reversal cannot be undone by turning it in the plane.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A shape and its mirror image can have identical side lengths and identical angles. Orientation is the only thing that separates them.*

## Scene 3. Mirror against rotation: the one distinction

*1:00 – 1:30 · objective: Decide between a rotation and a mirror by reading the clockwise order of three labelled corners, and see that the two do not commute.*

**1:00–1:07** — Here is the test that settles almost every mirror question. Label three corners A, B, C going clockwise. A rotation keeps them clockwise. A mirror makes them anticlockwise. Read the order and you are done.

> Caption: Read three labelled corners clockwise. A rotation keeps that order; a mirror reverses it.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Do not compare the pictures as wholes. Find three identifiable corners and compare their cyclic order.*

**1:07–1:15** — The L tetromino cannot be turned into its mirror image in the plane. Not by one turn, not by any number. Its mirror image is a genuinely different figure. A square is the opposite case: it is its own mirror image, four times over.

> Caption: The L tetromino cannot be turned into its mirror image in the plane at all.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Chirality is not about looking asymmetric. The L tetromino has no symmetry at all, and that is precisely why its mirror is different.*

**1:15–1:22** — For a square there are exactly eight operations that leave it looking unchanged. Four rotations, counting the do-nothing turn, and four mirrors: vertical, horizontal, and the two diagonals. Compose any two and you land on one of the eight again.

> Caption: Eight operations keep a square in place: four rotations and four mirrors.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The do-nothing rotation counts. Leaving it out gives seven and loses the closure that makes the counting work.*

**1:22–1:30** — And order matters. Take a point one step to the right of centre. Rotate, then mirror, and it ends at the top left. Mirror first, then rotate, and it comes back to where it started. Same two moves, different order, different answer.

> Caption: Order matters: rotating then mirroring is not the same as mirroring then rotating.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A question that says "first rotated, then mirrored" is not the same question as "first mirrored, then rotated". Read the order in the sentence.*

## Scene 4. Counting lines of symmetry and rotational order

*1:30 – 2:00 · objective: Count lines of symmetry and rotational order for small grid shapes, and check both against the size of the symmetry group.*

**1:30–1:37** — Two counts do all the work here. A line of symmetry folds the figure exactly onto itself. The rotational order counts the turns that leave it looking the same, and the do-nothing turn is one of them.

> Caption: A line of symmetry folds the figure onto itself. A rotational order counts turns that do the same.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A diagonal line only counts when the figure really is symmetric about that diagonal. A square has both diagonals; a rectangle has neither.*

**1:37–1:45** — The square gives four lines and order four. A rectangle that is not a square gives two lines and order two, because its diagonals are not mirror lines: reflecting across a diagonal lays the rectangle on its side.

> Caption: The square has four lines and rotational order four. The rectangle has two lines and order two.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The diagonals of a non-square rectangle are the classic wrong answer. They are not mirror lines.*

**1:45–1:52** — The T tetromino has one line of symmetry, down the middle. The L tetromino has none, and no turn either. Its only symmetry is doing nothing, and that is exactly why its mirror image is a different shape.

> Caption: The T tetromino has one line; the L tetromino has none, because it is chiral.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A figure with no symmetry is not "hard", it is simply chiral. That is a fact about it, and questions use it.*

**1:52–2:00** — And there is a check you can run. Count the mirror lines, count the rotational order, add them, and you have the number of the eight operations that leave the figure alone. The model counts both ways and compares.

> Caption: The check: lines plus rotational order equals the size of the symmetry group, and that size divides eight.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Adding the two counts double-counts nothing, because a reflection is never a rotation. Adding them is correct; subtracting one is not.*

## Scene 5. Folding: layers double and the long edge changes

*2:00 – 2:30 · objective: Track dimensions and layer count through a sequence of folds, and find the long edge again after each fold.*

**2:00–2:07** — A fold brings half the sheet onto the other half. The visible rectangle halves in one direction, and the number of layers doubles. After three folds there are eight layers.

> Caption: Each fold halves one dimension and doubles the number of layers.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The area of paper never changes. Only the apparent, visible area changes, and it divides by two for every fold.*

**2:07–2:15** — Twenty by eight. The long edge is twenty, so the first fold gives ten by eight. The long edge is now ten, so the second gives five by eight. Now eight is the long edge, so the third fold halves it: five by four.

> Caption: A 20 by 8 sheet folded three times perpendicular to its long edge gives 10 by 8, then 5 by 8, then 5 by 4.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The phrase "perpendicular to its long edge" means the long edge is found again after every fold. It is not the same edge three times.*

**2:15–2:22** — The final sheet is five by four, so the perimeter is eighteen. Fold the twenty side three times instead and you get two and a half by eight, perimeter twenty-one, which is exactly the wrong option sitting there in the paper.

> Caption: The perimeter of the folded sheet is 18 centimetres, and the wrong answer is 21.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Both numbers are in the options. The difference is one phrase: perpendicular to its long edge.*

**2:22–2:30** — One check catches every folding slip. Original area equals final area times the layers. Twenty times eight is a hundred and sixty. Five times four, times eight layers, is a hundred and sixty. It agrees, so the tracking was right.

> Caption: The check on any fold sequence: original area equals final area times the layer count.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *This check catches a lost fold but not a wrong edge. Do both: the area check and the long-edge rule.*

## Scene 6. Cutting through a folded stack

*2:30 – 3:00 · objective: Count holes as punches times layers, and locate them by unfolding with reflections across each fold line.*

**2:30–2:37** — A punch goes through the whole stack. If there are four layers, one punch makes four holes. Holes equals punches times layers, and that single multiplication answers most of these questions.

> Caption: One punch goes through every layer, so holes equal punches times layers.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A punch exactly on a fold line is shared by two layers, so it makes fewer holes than the multiplication suggests.*

**2:37–2:45** — A four by four sheet, folded in half one way and then the other, is a two by two stack four layers thick. One punch, and the unfolded sheet carries four holes.

> Caption: A 4 by 4 sheet folded in half twice has four layers, so one punch makes four holes.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The visible square has four cells and the punch goes in one of them, which is why it is easy to think the answer is one.*

**2:45–2:52** — To find where the holes are, unfold in the reverse order. Each time you open a fold, reflect the holes across the line you just opened. The last fold made is the first one undone.

> Caption: Unfold in the reverse order, reflecting the holes across each fold line as you go.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Unfolding in the same order as folding gives a pattern that is plausible and wrong. Reverse the order.*

**2:52–3:00** — The four holes come out symmetric about both fold lines. That is not a coincidence: the holes must be closed under every reflection the folds used. Two perpendicular folds generate four positions, so four holes.

> Caption: The four holes are symmetric about both fold lines, which is why the pattern looks regular.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *If the number of holes is not a multiple of the layers, a punch landed on a fold line or the fold sequence was mis-tracked.*

## Scene 7. Cube nets and the opposite-face rule

*3:00 – 3:30 · objective: Fold a net by rolling a cube across it, and identify opposite faces without any folding.*

**3:00–3:07** — A net is six squares joined edge to edge that fold up into a cube. There are exactly eleven arrangements that work, and that number is small enough to check by machine.

> Caption: A net is a flat arrangement of six squares that folds into a cube. There are exactly eleven.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A net with six squares is not automatically a cube. Most hexominoes overlap themselves when folded.*

**3:07–3:15** — There are thirty-five free hexominoes. The model builds them all, then rolls a cube across each one and asks whether the six squares land on six different faces. Eleven of the thirty-five do.

> Caption: Thirty-five free hexominoes exist, and the model rolls a cube across each one: eleven close up.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Thirty-five and eleven are different questions. Thirty-five counts shapes; eleven counts foldable shapes.*

**3:15–3:22** — Here is the rule that avoids folding anything. In a straight line of squares, two steps apart means opposite faces. One step apart means adjacent. So four squares in a row give two opposite pairs straight away.

> Caption: Two faces are opposite exactly when they are two steps apart in a straight line.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The rule needs a straight line. Squares that are two steps apart around a corner are not opposite.*

**3:22–3:30** — And the consequence that earns the mark: opposite faces are never adjacent, so a single view of a cube can never show both. Look at the option, find two faces that are opposite in the net, and if both are visible, delete the option.

> Caption: Opposite faces can never be adjacent, so they can never both be visible in one view.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *This eliminates wrong options but does not confirm the right one. Use it first, then check the remaining candidate.*

## Scene 8. Minimum straight cuts in three dimensions

*3:30 – 4:00 · objective: Use the product formula for pieces and the sum formula for cuts, and see why eight pieces need three cuts.*

**3:30–3:37** — One straight cut through a block splits it. Cut once across each of the three axes and you have two along each axis, which is eight pieces from three cuts.

> Caption: One straight cut through a block adds one piece. Three cuts, one along each axis, give eight.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The number of pieces is not the number of cuts. Three cuts give eight pieces because they multiply.*

**3:37–3:45** — In general, if you make c-x cuts across one axis you get c-x plus one slabs. Do the same on the other two axes and multiply. The pieces are the product of the three counts each plus one.

> Caption: With c-x cuts across one axis, the pieces number the product of the three counts plus one.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The formula needs each cut to cross the whole block. A cut that stops halfway adds fewer pieces.*

**3:45–3:52** — So eight equal pieces need three cuts, one along each axis. Two cuts reach at most four pieces, and no clever placement beats that, because the product is what counts.

> Caption: Eight equal pieces therefore need exactly three cuts, not four.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The options usually include four. Four cuts can give twelve pieces, so four is not the minimum.*

**3:52–4:00** — The trap is that two different things get asked with almost the same words. How many pieces from these cuts is a product. How many cuts for these pieces is a search. Read which one you have been given.

> Caption: The trap is counting cuts as pieces, or pieces as cuts.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Pieces that are not a product of three integers each at least two cannot be made with whole-axis cuts at all.*

## Scene 9. Patterns and tiling: area and colour

*4:00 – 4:30 · objective: Settle a tiling question with two arithmetic tests — area divisibility, then the chessboard colour count.*

**4:00–4:07** — A tiling question looks like geometry and is arithmetic. The first test is area: the region must divide by the tile. One division removes half the options.

> Caption: A tiling question is an arithmetic question. The first test is area.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Area divisibility is necessary, not sufficient. Passing it proves nothing; failing it proves impossibility.*

**4:07–4:15** — The second test is colour. Shade the board like a chessboard and count the two colours. Each tile covers a fixed number of each, so the counts have to agree.

> Caption: The second test is colour: shade the board like a chessboard and count each colour.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *The colouring must be chosen to suit the tile. The chessboard suits dominoes; other tiles need other colourings.*

**4:15–4:22** — A domino always covers one black square and one white one, wherever you put it. So a region tiled by dominoes must have the two colours in equal numbers. That is a fact about the region, not about your skill.

> Caption: A domino covers one black and one white square, so unequal counts mean no tiling exists.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *This is an invariant, so it gives impossibility proofs only. Equal counts do not guarantee that a tiling exists.*

**4:22–4:30** — Take an eight by eight board, thirty-two of each colour, and remove two opposite corners. Both corners are the same colour, so you are left with thirty and thirty-two. The area still divides by two. The colours do not match. Impossible.

> Caption: Remove two opposite corners of an 8 by 8 board and the counts are 30 and 32: impossible.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Removing two adjacent corners gives 31 and 31, which passes this test. The obstruction is specific to opposite corners.*

## Scene 10. The five-step procedure

*4:30 – 5:00 · objective: Compress the lesson into a procedure that starts from the wording of the question and ends with a number.*

**4:30–4:36** — Step one is naming the family from the words of the question. Folded means reflections. Cuts and pieces mean products. Constructed means tiling. Symmetry means a count.

> Caption: Step one: name the operation from the wording of the question.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A question can mix families. "A folded sheet is cut and unfolded" is folding first, then reflections.*

**4:36–4:42** — Step two is the orientation test. Three labelled corners, read clockwise. Same order means rotation or slide. Reversed means mirror. Do this before comparing anything else.

> Caption: Step two: test orientation with three labelled corners before comparing anything else.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Compare corners, not areas. Two figures of equal area can differ by orientation alone.*

**4:42–4:48** — Step three is folding. Keep three numbers: two dimensions and the layers. Halve the one the fold crosses, double the layers, and find the long edge again. Then check the area against the layers.

> Caption: Step three: for a folding question, halve one dimension, double the layers, and find the long edge again.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *Without re-finding the long edge, every fold after the first is applied to the wrong side.*

**4:48–4:54** — Step four is cuts. In the plane, layers times punches. In space, the product of the three counts each plus one. And if the number of pieces is given, search the factorisations instead of guessing.

> Caption: Step four: for cuts, multiply layers by punches, or use the product formula in three dimensions.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A punch on a fold line is shared by two layers and breaks the multiplication.*

**4:54–5:00** — Step five is tiling: area first, then colour. Two numbers, and most of these questions are over. Remember that both tests can only prove impossibility. To prove it can be done you still have to draw it.

> Caption: Step five: for tiling, check area and then colour. Two numbers settle it.

Exam-trap caption mode adds: *A passing test is not a proof. Only a construction proves that a tiling exists.*
