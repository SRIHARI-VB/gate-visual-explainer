Topic and objective

TOPIC: Spatial Aptitude — transformation of shapes by translation, rotation, scaling, mirroring, assembling and grouping; paper folding, cutting and patterns in two and three dimensions. Section 4 of the General Aptitude syllabus, common to every GATE paper including CS.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide which operation turned one figure into another by reading the clockwise order of three labelled corners. Count the lines of symmetry and the rotational order of a small grid shape and check both against the eight operations that fix a square. Track a fold sequence through three numbers — two dimensions and a layer count — and re-find the long edge after every fold. Count holes as punches times layers and locate them by unfolding with reflections. Fold a cube net by rolling a cube over it, and identify opposite faces with the two-steps-apart rule. Settle a partition question with the product formula, and a tiling question with area and colour.

I read this clause as five computable tests rather than five kinds of picture: an orientation test, a symmetry count, a dimension-and-layer track, a product, and two counts of colour. Every question in the area is one of those five, and each is answerable on paper.

Prerequisites

  • Arithmetic: halving, multiplying three small integers, and a remainder. That is the whole numerical requirement.
  • Counting and combinatorics — the product rule is the reason a fold doubles the layers and three cuts give eight pieces.
  • Graphs: matching and colouring — the chessboard argument in the tiling scene is the same parity argument used for bipartite graphs.
  • No programming, no software. Every number on this page is computed by the page's own model.

The five tests, in one line each

  • Orientation. Three corners read clockwise: preserved means rotation, reversed means mirror.
  • Symmetry. Count the mirror lines and the turn order; their sum is the number of the eight operations that fix the shape.
  • Folding. Halve one dimension, double the layers, and find the long edge again.
  • Cutting. Holes equal punches times layers; in space, pieces equal the product of each axis count plus one.
  • Tiling. Area must divide, then the two colours must tie.

Intuition first: a flat sheet of paper and a pile of layers

Almost everything in this area is a question about a sheet of paper. Slide it: that is a translation. Turn it: a rotation. Photocopy it larger: a scaling. Turn it over: a mirror, and the drawing now reads backwards, which is the only one of the four that changes anything you cannot put right by turning. Fold it and the pile gets thicker while the visible rectangle gets smaller. Punch the pile and every layer takes the hole. Unfold it and the holes are mirrored across each crease. Cut a block instead and the pieces multiply along three axes. Educational interpretation The sheet carries the mechanism honestly. What it does not carry is the arithmetic, which is why each test below is written out separately.

The reason this is worth a lesson rather than a set of tips is that all five tests are short. None of them needs you to hold a picture in your head. They are the same kind of thing as a rank calculation: a procedure that turns a picture into two or three numbers.

The four operations, stated precisely

Translation — add the same vector to every point. Distances, angles and orientation are all preserved; only position changes.

Rotation — turn about a centre through a fixed angle. Distances and angles preserved, orientation preserved, direction changed.

Scaling — multiply every distance from a fixed point by the same factor k. Angles preserved, lengths multiplied by k, areas by k².

Mirroring — reflect across a line. Distances and angles preserved, orientation reversed. This is the only one of the four that reverses orientation, and it is the operation the whole section turns on.

The orientation test, in full

Pick three identifiable points on the figure and label them A, B, C in clockwise order. In any image of the figure, find the three corresponding points and read them clockwise.

A B C clockwise → rotation or translation
A C B clockwise → mirroringThe cyclic order of three labelled points is the cheapest orientation invariant there is, and it never fails. It needs no lengths and no arithmetic.

Mathematical derivation A rotation is a linear map with positive determinant and a translation adds a constant, so both preserve signed area of the triangle ABC, which is the same thing as preserving the cyclic order. A reflection has determinant −1 and reverses it. The model checks the order on the L tetromino in both images.

The eight operations that fix a square

Four rotations — by 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° — and four reflections — in the vertical axis, the horizontal axis and the two diagonals. Together they are closed under composition: composing any two gives one of the eight.

Why the do-nothing rotation counts. Closure needs an identity. Leave it out and the set stops being closed, and the counting that follows stops working.

Mathematical derivation The page composes all sixty-four pairs, identifies each composite by its action on five probe points, and confirms that every one is again in the set; it then checks associativity on all 512 triples. Nothing about the group is asserted without being computed.

Lines of symmetry and rotational order

Line of symmetry — a line such that reflecting the figure in it gives back the same figure. Rotational order — the number of rotations, counting the do-nothing one, that map the figure onto itself.

lines + turn order = number of the eight operations that fix the shapeA reflection is never a rotation, so the two counts share only the identity, which the turn order already includes. Adding them therefore double-counts nothing.

Mathematical derivation Verified on six shapes by computing both sides independently: the counts on the left come from testing each of the eight operations, and the total on the right comes from counting the same eight that leave the shape unchanged. The page also checks orbit times stabiliser equals eight.

Folding, stated precisely

A fold is a reflection of one part of the sheet onto the other, along a line of symmetry of the sheet. Three quantities change and one does not.

One dimension halves — the one the fold line crosses. The layer count doubles. The area of paper is unchanged.

original area = final area × layer countThis is the check that catches every tracking slip. For a 20 by 8 sheet folded three times: 160 = 20 × 8 layers, and the final rectangle is 5 by 4.

The long-edge rule. "Folded along the line of symmetry perpendicular to its long edge" means the long edge is found again after every fold, because halving it can make the other side the longer one. That single phrase is the whole of GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Question 9.

Cutting, stated precisely

In the plane. A punch or a cut passes through the whole stack. With L layers, one punch makes L holes in the unfolded sheet.

holes = punches × layersProvided no punch lands exactly on a fold line, where two layers share an edge and the hole is counted once instead of twice.

To locate the holes. Unfold in the reverse order of folding, reflecting the current hole set across each fold line as it opens.

In space. With cx cuts across one axis, cy across a second and cz across a third, the pieces number (cx+1)(cy+1)(cz+1).

Cube nets and the opposite-face rule

A net is a set of six squares joined edge to edge that folds into a cube without overlap. Such a set is a hexomino of size six.

The rule. In a straight run of squares, two steps apart means opposite faces; one step apart means adjacent faces. Four squares in a row therefore give two opposite pairs immediately.

The consequence that earns marks. Opposite faces are never adjacent, so a single view of a cube can never show both. That eliminates most wrong options before you have decided which cube the net makes.

Tiling, stated precisely

Two necessary tests, both arithmetic.

Area. The area of the region must be a multiple of the area of the tile.

Colour. Colour the board like a chessboard. A domino covers one square of each colour, so a region tiled by dominoes must have the two colours in equal numbers.

What the tests can and cannot do. Both prove impossibility only. Passing them is not a proof that a tiling exists; that needs a construction. Mathematical derivation The colour count is an invariant: it does not depend on the placement, so it is a property of the region.

The one sentence to carry into the exam

Every question in this area is decided by a property that the wrong options cannot fake: the cyclic order of three corners, the two symmetry counts, the pair (dimensions, layers), the product of three integers, or the pair of colour counts. Find which of those five the question is about, compute it, and the picture stops mattering. The picture is there to make the question readable, not to be the answer.

Example 1 — GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q9, the perimeter after three folds

A rectangular paper of 20 cm × 8 cm is folded 3 times. Each fold is made along the line of symmetry, which is perpendicular to its long edge. The perimeter of the final folded sheet (in cm) is 18, 24, 20 or 21.

  1. Start at 20 × 8. The long edge is 20, so the first fold halves it: 10 × 8. A fold perpendicular to the long edge halves that edge, not the other one.
  2. The long edge is now 10, so the second fold halves it: 5 × 8. The long edge is re-found after every fold. It is not the same edge three times.
  3. The long edge is now 8, so the third fold halves the 8: 5 × 4. This is the step the question is built to catch.
  4. Perimeter = 2 × (5 + 4) = 18. The visible rectangle is 5 by 4; the layers do not change its outline.
  5. Check: 20 × 8 = 160, and 5 × 4 × 8 layers = 160. Area times layers must return the original area.

Trap. Folding the 20 side three times gives 2.5 × 8 and a perimeter of 21, which is offered as an option. The difference is one clause: perpendicular to its long edge.

Example 2 — GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Q9, the cuts for eight pieces

A cube is to be cut into 8 pieces of equal size and shape. Each cut must be straight and must not stop until it reaches the other end of the cube. The minimum number of such cuts required is ______.

  1. A cut across one axis divides the block into two along that axis. It passes through the whole block, so it splits every piece it crosses.
  2. Two cuts reach at most 2 × 2 = 4 pieces, splitting 2 and 1 across the axes. The page enumerates every distribution of two cuts and reports the maximum.
  3. Three cuts, one per axis, give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Each axis contributes a factor of two.
  4. Answer: 3. The pieces are the product of the three axis counts plus one; eight needs three factors of two.

Trap. Four cuts is offered and looks safer. Four cuts can reach twelve pieces, so four is not the minimum. The pieces asked for are equal and cube-shaped, which fixes one cut per axis.

Example 3 — one punch through a twice-folded square (original)

A 4 × 4 square sheet is folded in half left-onto-right, then in half bottom-onto-top. One punch is made in the top-left cell of the folded square. How many holes appear when the sheet is unfolded, and where?

  1. Fold one halves the 4 to 2 and doubles the layers to 2. The visible rectangle is 2 × 4 after this step.
  2. Fold two halves the 4 to 2 and doubles the layers to 4. The folded square is 2 × 2 and four layers thick.
  3. Holes = 1 punch × 4 layers = 4. The punch passes through the whole stack.
  4. The four holes are at (1,1), (2,1), (1,2) and (2,2). Unfolding reflects the punch across each fold line in reverse order.
  5. Check: the hole set is closed under both reflections, which is exactly why four appear. The model verifies closure, and verifies that all 16 cells appear exactly once after folding.

Trap. The folded square has four visible cells and the punch sits in one of them, which invites the answer 1. The layers are the multiplier, not the visible cells.

Example 4 — reading opposite faces off a net (original)

A net is four squares in a row with one square attached above the second and one below the second. Which faces are opposite?

  1. Roll a cube along the row of four. Successive squares land on successive side faces, going round the cube.
  2. In a straight run, one step apart is adjacent and two steps apart is opposite. Four squares in a row go round four side faces, so square 1 is opposite square 3 and square 2 is opposite square 4.
  3. The two remaining squares, the ones above and below the second, are the top and bottom, so they are opposite each other. All six faces are now accounted for, in three pairs.
  4. Check: the model rolls a real cube over the net and confirms that the six squares receive six different face labels. That is what "folds up without overlap" means, checked rather than assumed.

Trap. Two steps apart around a corner is not the rule. The two-steps-apart rule needs a straight line; around a corner, the cube has turned.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Each card states the condition a rule needs, and what happens when it fails. These are the cases that separate a correct answer from an almost-correct one.

A punch on a fold line is shared by two layers

Punch exactly on a crease and the two layers either side share the hole. With four layers, a punch on the fold line gives two holes, not four, because each crease pairs two layers into one hole.

The repair. Count holes that are not on a crease by the multiplication, and count crease holes separately, then add. If your answer is not a multiple of the layers, a punch sat on a crease.

A half of an odd dimension is not an integer

Half of 5 is 2.5, and half of 2.5 is 1.25. The sheet is continuous, so the fold sequence continues into fractions even though no square cell does.

Why it matters. A grid model that rounds will give 2 × 8 instead of 2.5 × 8 and so return 20 instead of 21 for the careless version. The perimeter is a length, so it is computed on continuous dimensions, not on cells.

Two cuts can never reach eight pieces, however they are placed

With two cuts the axis counts cx + cy + cz = 2, so the product is at most 2 × 2 × 1 = 4. There is no clever diagonal placement that beats it, because each cut crosses the whole block and adds one to one axis count.

The condition. The formula assumes each cut crosses the entire block. A cut that stops halfway adds fewer pieces, and the formula does not apply.

A pass on area is not a proof that a tiling exists

A 4 × 4 board with one square removed has area 15, which is not divisible by 2, so no domino tiling exists. But area 16 with equal colours still does not guarantee one for every region; the two tests are necessary, never sufficient.

What settles it. Impossibility is proved by an invariant. Possibility is proved only by exhibiting a tiling, so a question asking "can it be done" needs a construction on paper.

A rectangle has two mirror lines, not four

Reflect a 3 × 2 rectangle in its diagonal and the rectangle turns onto its side: a 2 × 3 rectangle, which does not coincide with the original. Only the vertical and horizontal mid-lines are mirror lines.

What it costs. Answering 4 here is the single most common symmetry error, because a square does have both diagonals and the square is the shape everyone practises on.

The do-nothing rotation is part of the count

A figure with no symmetry at all still has rotational order 1, because the do-nothing turn maps it onto itself. Leaving it out gives order 0, and then "lines plus order equals the stabiliser" fails by one.

The check that catches it. The L tetromino has 0 lines and order 1, total 1, which is right: the identity is its only symmetry. With order 0 the total would be 0, and a group with no identity is not a group.

Five beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each card gives the belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule to use instead. These are drawn from the shapes of the wrong options in the papers named in the Sources tab, not from a survey of learners.

1. "A mirror is just a rotation I have not found yet"

Why it tempts. For a symmetric figure the two look identical, and most practice figures are symmetric.

Refutation. The L tetromino and its mirror image have identical side lengths and identical angles, and no rotation in the plane carries one to the other. Its symmetry group is the identity alone.

Correct rule. Read three labelled corners clockwise. Preserved order means rotation; reversed means mirror. Orientation is the only thing that separates them.

2. "Each fold halves the same side"

Why it tempts. The wording says "the line of symmetry", which sounds like one fixed line, and on a square every half is the same.

Refutation. On 20 × 8, halving the long edge twice gives 5 × 8, and the 8 is now the long edge. The third fold halves the 8. Halving the 20 three times gives 2.5 × 8 and perimeter 21.

Correct rule. Re-find the long edge after every fold. That is what "perpendicular to its long edge" means.

3. "Folding changes the area of the paper"

Why it tempts. The visible rectangle really does get smaller, and the smaller number is the one you can see.

Refutation. 20 × 8 is 160 square centimetres of paper. After three folds the visible rectangle is 5 × 4 = 20, and there are eight layers: 8 × 20 = 160.

Correct rule. Original area equals final area times layers. Use it as the check on every fold sequence.

4. "One punch makes one hole"

Why it tempts. The punch is a single visible action on a single visible square.

Refutation. The folded square is four layers thick. One punch makes four holes, at positions related by the two fold reflections.

Correct rule. Holes equal punches times layers, adjusted down only for punches that land on a crease.

5. "Thirty-five hexominoes means thirty-five nets"

Why it tempts. Both numbers are about six squares joined edge to edge, and both are easy to remember wrongly.

Refutation. There are 35 free hexominoes and only 11 of them fold into a cube. The other 24 overlap themselves when folded, which the page checks by rolling a cube over each one.

Correct rule. 35 counts shapes; 11 counts foldable shapes. A net with six squares is not automatically a cube.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

Four pairs get confused, and each is settled by a definition: rotation against mirror (orientation preserved against reversed), visible area against paper area (which divides by the layers), cuts against pieces (a sum against a product), and necessary against sufficient for the tiling tests. In each pair the two members differ by one condition, and the question's wording always says which one applies.

How this section gets tested, and what each pattern demands

Five patterns cover the items in this area. Each is listed with the quantity it computes, because the quantity is what the trap attacks. Every previous-year item named here carries its year, set and question number.

Pattern 1 — describe the transformation between two figures

Shape. Two or three figures P, Q, R, and four sentences, one of which correctly describes the moves.

Procedure. Read three labelled corners clockwise in each figure. Same order means rotation or translation; reversed means a mirror. Then check whether a length changed, which separates a scaling from a rigid move.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2023 GA Question 10, which asks exactly this of a two-dimensional figure taken from P to Q and then to R.

Pattern 2 — fold, then cut, then choose the unfolded pattern

Shape. A square or rectangle, a sequence of folds along dotted lines, a few cuts, and four candidate unfolded patterns.

Procedure. Count the layers to get the number of holes, then use symmetry to eliminate. A hole touching an edge in the folded figure must produce a hole touching an edge in the answer; a hole at a corner of the folded figure produces a hole at the centre or at a corner, never in the middle of a side.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Question 9, a square folded along two lines and then cut, where elimination on the position of the cut settles it faster than a full unfolding.

Pattern 3 — a net, and which cube it makes

Shape. A net of six squares with symbols or numbers on the faces, and four pictures of a cube.

Procedure. Find the three opposite pairs first, using the two-steps-apart rule. Then delete every option that shows two faces which are opposite in the net, because a view shows three mutually adjacent faces.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2025 Set 2 GA Question 4, which gives a net and asks which cube it represents.

Pattern 4 — a count with units: perimeter, area, cuts, pieces

Shape. A physical process — folding, cutting, dividing — and one number asked for, usually with centimetres or a count as the unit.

Procedure. Write down what multiplies and what adds. Folding halves a dimension and doubles layers. Cutting multiplies layers by punches, or adds one to an axis count. Then check with the conservation identity: area equals final area times layers, or pieces equals the product.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Question 9 (perimeter after three folds, answer 18) and GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Question 9 (minimum cuts for eight equal pieces, answer 3).

Pattern 5 — symmetry: lines, order, or the least change to achieve it

Shape. A figure with some squares shaded, and a question about a line of symmetry: how many lines, or the least number of squares to add so that a named line becomes one.

Procedure. Count pairs. For a named line, every shaded square must have a shaded partner on the other side; the least number to add is the number of unpaired squares. Count lines by testing the four candidate directions, not by eye.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Question 10, which asks the least number of squares to be added to make AB a line of symmetry.

Two of these five are one-mark freebies — take them first

The net-elimination pattern and the symmetry-count pattern both need one rule and no arithmetic beyond counting, and both are answerable in under a minute once the rule is automatic. The fold-and-cut pattern rewards counting layers before looking at the options at all. Only the fold-with-units pattern costs real arithmetic, and even there the conservation check catches the slip. In a paper where General Aptitude is 15 marks in ten questions, taking the cheap ones first is worth more than perfecting the expensive one.

MCQGATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q9

A rectangular paper of 20 cm × 8 cm is folded 3 times. Each fold is made along the line of symmetry, which is perpendicular to its long edge. The perimeter of the final folded sheet (in cm) is:

  1. 18
  2. 24
  3. 20
  4. 21

Answer: A — 18

20 × 8 → the long edge 20 halves → 10 × 8. Now 10 is long, so it halves → 5 × 8. Now 8 is long, so it halves → 5 × 4. Perimeter = 2(5 + 4) = 18.

Why 21 appears. Folding the 20 side three times gives 2.5 × 8, perimeter 21, and that is offered. The check. 20 × 8 = 160 and 5 × 4 × 8 layers = 160, so the tracking is right.

NATGATE CSE 2024 Set 2 GA Q9

A cube is to be cut into 8 pieces of equal size and shape. Each cut must be straight, and must not stop until it reaches the other end of the cube. What is the minimum number of such cuts required?

Answer: 3

One cut across each of the three axes. The pieces are (cx+1)(cy+1)(cz+1), so 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 with one cut per axis.

Why not 2. Two cuts give at most 2 × 2 × 1 = 4 pieces; the page enumerates every distribution and reports the maximum. Why not 4. Four cuts can reach 12 pieces, so four is not the minimum.

NATOriginal practice

A square sheet is folded in half twice, giving four layers. Two punches are made, neither on a fold line. How many holes does the unfolded sheet have?

Answer: 8

Holes = punches × layers = 2 × 4 = 8. Each punch passes through all four layers.

Why 4 appears. That is one punch's worth. Why 2 appears. That is the number of punches. The multiplication is the whole question.

MCQOriginal practice

Three corners of a figure, read clockwise, are A then B then C. In a second figure the corresponding corners read A then C then B, clockwise. The second figure is:

  1. a translation of the first
  2. a rotation of the first
  3. a scaling of the first
  4. a mirror image of the first

Answer: D — a mirror image

Translation, rotation and scaling all preserve orientation, so the cyclic order of three labelled corners survives all three. Only a reflection reverses it.

Why rotation tempts. A rotation changes which way the figure faces, which feels like a reversal. It is not: the clockwise order is untouched. Note. A mirror combined with a rotation is still a mirror, so one reversal is enough to identify it.

NATOriginal practice

A 3 × 2 rectangle (not a square). How many lines of symmetry does it have, and what is its rotational order? Give the two numbers.

Answer: 2 lines, rotational order 2

The vertical and horizontal mid-lines are mirror lines; the two diagonals are not, because reflecting a 3 × 2 rectangle in a diagonal gives a 2 × 3 rectangle lying on its side. The rotations that work are the half turn and the do-nothing turn, so the order is 2.

Why 4 and 4 tempts. That is the square. The check. 2 + 2 = 4, and the stabiliser of a non-square rectangle is indeed four of the eight operations; orbit times stabiliser is 2 × 4 = 8.

MSQOriginal practice

An 8 × 8 chessboard has two squares removed. Which statements are true?

  1. If the two removed squares are opposite corners, no domino tiling of what remains exists.
  2. If the two removed squares are adjacent corners, the remaining 62 squares have equal numbers of the two colours.
  3. If the two removed squares are of different colours, the colour test does not rule out a tiling.
  4. If the area of what remains is even, a domino tiling always exists.

Answer: A, B and C

The board starts with 32 of each colour. Two opposite corners are the same colour, leaving 30 and 32, so A holds. Two adjacent corners are of different colours, leaving 31 and 31, so B holds. Removing one of each colour keeps the counts equal, so the colour test raises no objection and C holds. D fails because equal colours and even area are both necessary and neither is sufficient.

Why D tempts. Both tests pass in many such regions, so it looks like a rule. It is a pattern in the examples, not a theorem.

The revision sheet: what to write on one page

Definitions first, then the tests, then the numbers worth knowing, then the traps. If you can reproduce this page from memory, the section is covered.

Definitions in one line each

  • Translation — add one vector to every point; only position changes.
  • Rotation — turn about a centre; orientation preserved.
  • Scaling — multiply every distance by k; areas by k².
  • Mirroring — reflect across a line; orientation reversed.
  • Line of symmetry — a mirror line that maps the figure onto itself.
  • Rotational order — turns that map it onto itself, counting the do-nothing turn.
  • Net — six squares joined edge to edge that fold into a cube.

The five tests, as formulas

lines + turn order = operations fixing the shape
original area = final area × layers
holes = punches × layers
pieces = (cx+1)(cy+1)(cz+1)
two steps apart in a straight run = opposite facesThe first line is a check on a symmetry count. The second catches a folding slip. The third is the whole of the fold-and-punch family. The fourth answers every partition question. The fifth answers every net question.

Orientation test. Three corners clockwise: preserved means rotation, reversed means mirror.

Numbers worth knowing

  • Eight operations fix a square: four rotations, four mirrors.
  • Square: 4 lines, order 4. Non-square rectangle: 2 and 2. Plus sign: 4 and 4. T: 1 and 1. L and Z: 0 lines; order 1 and 2.
  • 20 × 8 folded three times along the long edge: 5 × 4, perimeter 18. Careless: 2.5 × 8, perimeter 21.
  • 4 × 4 folded twice: four layers, so one punch gives four holes.
  • 35 free hexominoes; 11 fold into a cube.
  • 1, 2, 3 cuts give at most 2, 4, 8 pieces.
  • 8 × 8 board: 32 and 32; opposite corners removed: 30 and 32; adjacent corners removed: 31 and 31.

The traps, in the order they cost marks

  • Folding the same side every time instead of the long edge.
  • Treating a rotation as if it reversed orientation.
  • Counting the diagonals of a non-square rectangle as mirror lines.
  • Forgetting the do-nothing rotation in the turn order.
  • Reading visible area as paper area.
  • Confusing cuts with pieces.
  • Treating a passed tiling test as a proof.
  • Punching on a fold line and expecting the full multiplication.

Five quick self-questions, answers in your head

1. A figure has 3 lines of symmetry. What is the smallest possible turn order? (1, if it has no non-trivial rotation — the two counts are independent except through the total.)

2. A 12 × 6 sheet is folded twice, each time perpendicular to its long edge. What are the final dimensions? (6 × 6, then 6 × 3.)

3. Three punches through a stack of eight layers, none on a crease. How many holes? (24.)

4. In a net, two squares are two steps apart around a right-angled corner. Are they opposite? (No. The two-steps-apart rule needs a straight line.)

5. A region has 60 squares and equal black and white counts. Does a domino tiling exist? (Not necessarily. Both tests are necessary, not sufficient.)

What is computed rather than cited

Every number on the canvas is computed by this page's model on integer grid cells: the symmetry counts come from applying all eight operations to each shape and comparing cell sets for equality; the composition table comes from composing all 64 pairs and identifying each result by its action on five probe points, with associativity checked on all 512 triples; the fold model keeps, for every visible cell, the list of original cells stacked on it, so the layer count and the hole count are read off rather than asserted; the cube-net count comes from enumerating all 35 free hexominoes and rolling a cube across each one, which reproduces the classical count of 11 rather than quoting it; and the partition and tiling figures come from exhaustive enumeration. The count of passing checks is printed under the canvas. No figure in this lesson was copied from an answer key without being recomputed.
