Topic and objective

TOPIC: The variance of a sum — the three-term identity with its covariance, the difference between uncorrelated and independent, and the two distribution-free bounds that a variance buys (Markov and Chebyshev).

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Compute a mean, a variance and a covariance from a joint table of equally likely outcomes. State why Var(X + Y) is Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X, Y), and produce a pair of variables for which the last term is positive, negative and zero. Tell "uncorrelated" from "independent" with a counterexample. Read Markov's and Chebyshev's inequalities as upper bounds, say when each is tight and when each is vacuous, and turn a Chebyshev bound on a sample mean into a required sample size.

I read this topic as the part of Section 1's "Probability and Statistics" clause that the existing lessons stop short of: random variables defines Var(X), and linearity of expectation covers sums of means. This page owns sums of variances, which is the case where independence is actually needed.

Prerequisites

  • Probability, random variables and distributions — expectation, Var(X) as E[X²] − (E[X])², joint PMF. Everything here is built on that.
  • Linearity of expectation — E[X + Y] is E[X] + E[Y] with no assumption. The contrast matters: the same statement is false for variance.
  • Mean, median, mode and standard deviation — units and the square root.
  • Independence beyond two events — pairwise against mutual independence, which the exclusive-or example at scene 7 depends on.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • Variance is a squared distance, so a scale factor is squared and a shift changes nothing.
  • Sums carry a cross term. Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X, Y), always.
  • Uncorrelated is weaker than independent. Zero covariance kills the linear part of a relationship, not the relationship.
  • Markov needs a mean; Chebyshev needs a variance. Both are upper bounds, and both can be vacuous.
  • Both are tight somewhere. A bound that is met by some distribution cannot be improved by any argument that uses only what the bound uses.

1. Intuition — the two-number summary, and the third number

A mean is a balance point. Put a distribution on a ruler and the mean is where it balances; that is all it says. Two distributions can balance at the same place while one is a spike and the other is two lumps at the ends. So a summary that stops at the mean has thrown away every fact about distance. Educational interpretation

The variance repairs that by averaging the squared distance from the balance point. The square is not decoration: without it the distances above the mean and the distances below cancel, and every distribution would report the same zero. Squaring makes every distance count, at the cost of putting the answer in squared units, which is why the square root is reported as well.

Now the part that costs marks. A mean is additive without any assumption: the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, always. A variance is not. When two variables move together, the sum moves more than the parts suggest, and when they move oppositely it moves less — a sum can even be a constant while both parts are random. The missing quantity is the covariance, and the whole of the first half of this lesson is that one term.

2. Formal definitions

Variance. Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] where μ = E[X]. Equivalently E[X²] − μ². Its square root is the standard deviation.

Covariance. Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − E[X])(Y − E[Y])] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y].

Correlation. ρ = Cov(X, Y) / (σ_X σ_Y), defined when both variances are positive. Then −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1.

Uncorrelated. Cov(X, Y) = 0. Independent implies uncorrelated; the converse fails.

Markov. For X ≥ 0 with finite mean, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a for a > 0. Chebyshev. P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² for k > 0, when σ² is finite.

3. Visual understanding — what the drawing has to show

A covariance is not visible in a picture of two separate distributions, because it is a fact about the pairing. So the animation draws the twelve ordered pairs as dots with the two means as axes: the sign of the product of deviations is then a colour, and the two off-diagonal quadrants are visibly fuller than the two diagonal ones.

The second thing the drawing must show is that an inequality is a ceiling, not a value. So the bound is drawn as a second number beside the true tail, at three thresholds, and the same distribution is shown attaining the ceiling at one threshold and missing it badly at another. A picture that shows only the bound would teach the formula and not the reasoning.

4. The four moves a variance question needs

  1. Identify what varies. A single variable, a linear combination, or a sum of many. The three use different formulas.
  2. Ask about the cross term. Independent, uncorrelated, or neither. Only the first two let you drop it.
  3. Write every term before adding. n variances and n(n−1)/2 pairs. Missing a pair is the commonest arithmetic error.
  4. Keep the units. A scale factor is squared in the variance and not in the standard deviation; a shift never matters.

Move 2 is where the marks are lost, because the exam supplies variables that look independent — a first draw and a second draw from the same bag, two bits and their exclusive or — and are not.

Worked examples — every number below is computed on this page

One running example throughout: four balls numbered 1 to 4; draw two without replacement; X is the first draw and Y is the second. Twelve ordered pairs, one twelfth each. The diagonal of the joint table is empty, and that single fact is what makes X and Y dependent.

Example 1 — the joint table, and covariance by two routes

p(x,y) = 1/12 for x ≠ y, 0 on the diagonal.
E[X] = E[Y] = 2.5   E[X²] = 7.5
Var(X) = 7.5 − 6.25 = 1.25

  1. E[XY] = (sum of xy over the twelve ordered pairs)/12 = 70/12 = 5.8333.
  2. Shortcut: 5.8333 − (2.5)(2.5) = −0.4167 = −5/12.
  3. Definition: average of (x − 2.5)(y − 2.5) over the same twelve pairs. Same number.
  4. Eight pairs give a negative product and four a positive one; the negative mass wins.

ρ = −5/12 ÷ (1.25) = −1/3. The covariance has units of squared balls; the correlation does not.

Example 2 — the sum, read off the same table

S = X + Y. Group the twelve pairs by their sum:

S:   3   4   5   6   7
count: 2  2  4  2  2  (of 12)

  1. E[S] = 60/12 = 5. E[S²] = 320/12 = 26.667.
  2. Var(S) = 26.667 − 25 = 1.667 = 5/3. No formula used.
  3. Identity: 1.25 + 1.25 + 2(−0.4167) = 1.667. Agrees.
  4. Naive sum 2.5. Wrong by 0.833, because the covariance is negative.
  5. Closed form: nσ²(N−n)/(N−1) = 2(1.25)(2/3) = 1.667.

Draw all four balls and that factor is zero, because the sum is then always 10.

Example 3 — one coin, three partners

X is a fair coin, Var(X) = 0.25. Compare three partners:

Y = X     Cov +0.25  Var(S) 1.00
Y = 1−X   Cov −0.25  Var(S) 0.00
Y = X+2   Cov +0.25  Var(S) 1.00

The naive answer is 0.5 in all three cases. The truth is 1, 0 and 1. A shift changes no variance at all, so the third case is the first one moved sideways.

The second case is the one worth remembering: both parts are genuinely random and the sum is the constant 1.

Example 4 — uncorrelated but dependent

X uniform on {−1, 0, 1}; Y = X². Knowing X fixes Y exactly.

E[X]=0   E[Y]=2/3   E[XY]=E[X³]=0
Cov = 0   ρ = 0
Var X = 2/3   Var Y = 2/9

Independence fails: P(X=1, Y=1) = 1/3 while P(X=1)P(Y=1) = (1/3)(2/3) = 2/9.

Yet Var(X + Y) = 8/9 = 2/3 + 2/9 exactly, checked against the sum's own distribution: 0 with probability 2/3, 2 with probability 1/3.

Example 5 — n variables, and the count of terms

Var(ΣXi) = ΣVar(Xi) + 2Σ_{i<j} Cov(Xi,Xj)

  1. n = 3: three variances and three pairs — six terms when every pair is written out.
  2. n = 4: four variances and six pairs.
  3. Four independent copies of variance 1.25: Var = 5, and the mean of four has 1.25/4.
  4. Four perfectly correlated copies: Var = 16 × 1.25 = 20, and the mean of four has the same variance as one copy.

The square-root-n law needs zero covariance. Averaging correlated measurements removes nothing.

Example 6 — the bounds on a real shape

Markov: X = 0 (0.9), 10 (0.1), mean 1
a=10: bound 0.100, truth 0.100
a=5:  bound 0.200, truth 0.100
a=0.5: bound 2.000 (vacuous)

Chebyshev, k = 2: bound 0.25
tight family: 0.250
fair die: 0.000
10 fair flips: 0.021

The tight family puts mass 1/(2k²) at ±k and the rest at 0, so its variance is exactly 1 and its tail is exactly 1/k². No bound of this kind can be smaller.

Counterexample 1 — a sum of two random variables that is a constant

Take X a fair coin and Y = 1 − X. Both have variance 0.25, and X + Y = 1 on every outcome, so Var(X + Y) = 0.

This kills "the sum of two random things is random". A variance of zero is a statement that the variable is a constant with probability one, not that it is small.

Counterexample 2 — zero covariance with a perfect relationship

Y = X² with X uniform on {−1, 0, 1}. The relationship is deterministic: Y is a function of X. The covariance is still 0, because the two nonzero products cancel.

Covariance detects the linear part of a relationship only. Any claim of the form "Cov = 0, therefore nothing is going on" is false.

Counterexample 3 — pairwise independent, not mutually independent

Two fair bits and their exclusive or. Every pair is independent, so all three covariances are zero and the variance of the sum is exactly 3/4. But P(all three zero) = 1/4 while the product of the three marginals is 1/8.

The variance identity needs pairwise zero covariance and nothing stronger — which is why it survives here while a claim about the joint distribution would not.

Boundary 1 — k below 1 makes Chebyshev vacuous

At k = 0.5 the bound is 1/k² = 4, and a probability of at most 4 is no information. At k = 1 it is exactly 1, also no information.

The inequality is true for every k > 0 and useful only for k > 1. A question that quotes a bound above 1 is asking whether you noticed.

Boundary 2 — Markov needs a non-negative variable

Apply Markov to a variable that can be negative and it fails immediately: a fair coin taking ±10 has mean 0, so the bound would say P(X ≥ 10) ≤ 0 while the truth is 1/2.

Chebyshev escapes this by applying Markov to the squared deviation, which is non-negative by construction. That one step is the whole derivation.

The edge case worth memorising

Two facts decide almost every variance question: whether the cross term is zero, and whether a scale factor is inside or outside the square. In this lesson's running example the cross term is −5/12, not 0, and the naive answer 2.5 differs from the truth 1.667 by exactly twice that.

1. "The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances."

Why it tempts. It is true for the mean, and the mean is taught first, so the pattern carries over.

What is true. Only when the covariance is zero. The general rule has a third term, 2Cov, and it can be positive, negative or zero.

Minimal case. Y = X: Var(X + Y) = 4Var(X) = 1, against the naive 0.5.

2. "Zero covariance means independent."

Why it tempts. The implication runs the other way and is true, so the pair looks symmetric.

What is true. Independence implies zero covariance. Zero covariance implies only the absence of a linear trend.

Minimal case. Y = X² on {−1, 0, 1}: covariance 0, dependence total.

3. "The correlation measures how strong the relationship is."

Why it tempts. ρ near ±1 does indicate a strong linear relationship, and most examples in textbooks are linear.

What is true. ρ measures the linear part. A perfect curve can give ρ = 0, and ρ = ±1 holds exactly when the two lie on a line with probability one.

Minimal case. The square again: ρ = 0 with a deterministic relationship.

4. "For a difference you subtract the variances."

Why it tempts. The expression X − Y has a minus sign in it, and the eye carries the sign into the formula.

What is true. Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var Y − 2Cov(X, Y). With zero covariance the variances add, exactly as for a sum.

Minimal case. Two independent draws with variances 4 and 9: Var(X − Y) = 13, not −5.

5. "Chebyshev tells you the probability."

Why it tempts. The answer is a number, and numbers usually are the answer.

What is true. It is an upper bound that holds for every distribution with that variance. On a fair die at k = 2 it says 0.25 where the truth is 0.

Minimal case. Die, k = 2: bound 0.25, truth 0.000.

6. "Variance and standard deviation scale the same way."

Why it tempts. They carry the same information, so they look interchangeable.

What is true. Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X) and sd(aX + b) = |a| sd(X). Doubling a variable quadruples the variance and only doubles the standard deviation.

Minimal case. Var = 1.25 becomes 5 under doubling; sd 1.118 becomes 2.236.

What the exam actually asks

Three shapes cover this topic. Each one is a procedure you can run on the paper in front of you, and none of them is answered by remembering a worked example. The theory shape is the one that appears most often, because a statement about covariance can be marked without any arithmetic.

Shape 1 — a true/false statement about the definitions

The question gives two or four claims and asks which hold. The reasoning it demands: reduce each claim to a definition, then look for the one distribution that breaks it.

The wrong approach. Testing on a normal distribution. A claim about all random variables is false as soon as one small discrete counterexample exists.

The procedure. For a claim of the form "there exist X, Y with property P", try a two-point variable. For "for all X, Y", try a degenerate or perfectly correlated pair first.

Shape 2 — compute Var of a combination

The question gives Var X, Var Y and Cov, and asks for Var(aX + bY). The reasoning it demands: square the coefficients and keep the cross term.

The wrong approach. Adding the coefficients' magnitudes, or dropping the cross term because the variables "look independent".

The procedure. Var(aX + bY) = a²Var X + b²Var Y + 2abCov. Write the three numbers, watch the sign of ab, then add. This page checks the case a = 2, b = −3 both ways.

Shape 3 — a bound, or a sample size

The question gives a mean and a threshold, or a variance and a tolerance, and asks for a bound or a number of trials. The reasoning it demands: pick the inequality that uses only what is given.

The wrong approach. Using the normal quantile when the question gives no distribution. The bound must hold for every distribution, so 1.96 is not available.

The procedure. Mean only: Markov. Mean and variance: Chebyshev. Solving for n: invert σ²/(nε²) ≤ δ.

Two sentences worth keeping

"Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X, Y)" answers every computation on this topic, and it is also the fastest way to see why a claim of the form "variances always add" is false.

"Uncorrelated means no linear trend" answers every statement question, because the counterexample is always a symmetric curve.

MCQGATE CSE 2021 Set 1, Question 35

Consider the two statements.

S1: There exist random variables X and Y such that
    (E[(X − E(X))(Y − E(Y))])² > Var[X] Var[Y]
S2: For all random variables X and Y,
    Cov[X, Y] = E[|X − E[X]| |Y − E[Y]|]

(A) S1 is false, but S2 is true
(B) S1 is true, but S2 is false
(C) Both S1 and S2 are true
(D) Both S1 and S2 are false

Answer: D

Why S1 is false. The quantity inside the square is the covariance, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives (Cov[X, Y])² ≤ Var[X] Var[Y] for every pair with finite variances. So no pair can make the strict inequality true.

Why S2 is false. The right-hand side is an expectation of a product of absolute values, so it is never negative. The covariance can be negative: with Y = 1 − X and X a fair coin, Cov = −0.25 while the right-hand side is +0.25.

The distinction being tested. A bound on the square of the covariance against a claim about its sign. One counterexample settles a "for all" claim; a theorem settles an "exists" claim.

NATOriginal practice question

X and Y have Var(X) = 4, Var(Y) = 9 and Cov(X, Y) = 3. Find Var(2X − 3Y).

Answer: 61

Var(2X − 3Y) = 4Var(X) + 9Var(Y) + 2(2)(−3)Cov(X, Y) = 16 + 81 − 36 = 61.

Why 97 is wrong. It drops the cross term. The coefficients multiply the covariance, and the sign of 2 × (−3) is negative, so the term subtracts.

The trap. Both coefficients are squared, and the covariance takes the product of the coefficients with its sign, not their squares.

MCQOriginal practice question

X is uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X². Which one of the following is TRUE?

(A) X and Y are independent
(B) Cov(X, Y) = 0 and X, Y are dependent
(C) Cov(X, Y) > 0
(D) Var(X + Y) ≠ Var(X) + Var(Y)

Answer: B

E[X] = 0, E[Y] = 2/3 and E[XY] = E[X³] = 0, so Cov = 0. But P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 1/3 against the product 2/9, so the pair is dependent.

Why D is wrong. The covariance is zero, so the variances do add: 2/3 + 2/9 = 8/9, which the page confirms by enumerating the sum.

The distinction being tested. Zero covariance is exactly what licenses adding variances. It is not what licenses independence.

NATOriginal practice question

Two balls are drawn without replacement from {1, 2, 3, 4}. Find Var(X + Y), where X and Y are the two draws, correct to two decimal places.

Answer: 1.67

Var(X) = Var(Y) = 1.25 and Cov(X, Y) = −5/12, so Var(X + Y) = 1.25 + 1.25 − 0.8333 = 1.6667. The closed form nσ²(N−n)/(N−1) = 2(1.25)(2/3) gives the same number.

Why 2.50 is wrong. That is the answer for two independent draws, and the second draw is not independent of the first.

The trap. Without replacement makes the covariance negative, so the sum is steadier than independence. The correction factor (N−n)/(N−1) is the whole difference.

NATOriginal practice question

A non-negative random variable has mean 1. Give the smallest upper bound on P(X ≥ 5) that follows from the mean alone, to two decimal places.

Answer: 0.20

Markov gives P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a = 1/5 = 0.20.

Why not 0.10. That is the true value for the page's two-point variable, but the bound must hold for every non-negative variable with mean 1, and some of them attain 0.20.

The distinction being tested. A bound is a ceiling over a class of distributions, not an estimate for the one in front of you.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following are TRUE for random variables with finite variances? Select all that apply.

  1. Cov(X, X) = Var(X).
  2. Cov(aX + b, Y) = a Cov(X, Y).
  3. |ρ| ≤ 1, with equality exactly when Y is a linear function of X with probability one.
  4. Cov(X, Y) = 0 implies X and Y are independent.

Answer: A, B and C

A is the reason the diagonal of a covariance table is the variances. B follows by expanding the definition; the additive constant dies because deviations are taken from the mean. C is Cauchy–Schwarz, with the equality case being the statement that the two centred variables are proportional.

D is false. Y = X² with X symmetric on three points is the counterexample: covariance 0, dependence total.

The distinction being tested. What a covariance is blind to. It sees linear drift and nothing else.

NATOriginal practice question

Averages of n independent draws have variance σ²/n. With σ = 2, how many draws does Chebyshev require so that P(|mean − μ| ≥ 0.5) ≤ 0.05?

Answer: 320

Chebyshev on the average gives P(|mean − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). Setting that at most δ and solving gives n ≥ σ²/(ε²δ) = 4/(0.25 × 0.05) = 320.

Why 62 is wrong. That is the answer from a normal quantile, which assumes a distribution the question does not give. It is a valid answer to a different question.

The trap. Rounding down. The bound needs n at least the quotient, so a fractional value rounds up.

MCQOriginal practice question

X has variance 4. What is the variance of 3 − 2X?

(A) −8   (B) 8   (C) 16   (D) 13

Answer: C

Var(3 − 2X) = (−2)² Var(X) = 4 × 4 = 16. The shift contributes nothing and the scale factor is squared.

Why B and D are wrong. Both treat the scale factor as if it were not squared, and D adds the shift as though it were a variance.

The trap. A variance is never negative, so A is impossible on its face.

MCQOriginal practice question

X and Y are uncorrelated, with Var(X) = 4 and Var(Y) = 9. What is Var(X − Y)?

(A) 13   (B) 5   (C) −5   (D) 36

Answer: A

Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var Y − 2Cov = 4 + 9 − 0 = 13.

Why B is wrong. It subtracts the variances, which would be Var(X) − Var(Y) and is not a variance formula at all. C is negative, so it cannot be a variance. D multiplies them.

The distinction being tested. For uncorrelated variables a difference has more variance than either part, because the two sources of spread add.

Definitions, in the words that earn the mark

  • Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ². Zero exactly when X is constant with probability 1.
  • Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − E X)(Y − E Y)] = E[XY] − E X E Y. Its sign is the direction of joint drift.
  • ρ = Cov / (σ_X σ_Y), in [−1, 1], unit-free, invariant under positive rescaling.
  • Uncorrelated: Cov = 0. Independent implies it; it does not imply independent.
  • Markov: X ≥ 0 ⟹ P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a.
  • Chebyshev: P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k², finite variance.

The formulas, in one place

Var(aX + b) = a² Var(X)
Var(X ± Y) = VarX + VarY ± 2Cov
Var(ΣXi) = ΣVar(Xi) + 2Σ_{i<j}Cov
Cov(X,X) = Var(X)
Cov(aX+b, cY+d) = ac Cov(X,Y)
(Cov)² ≤ VarX · VarY
Var(mean of n iid) = σ²/n

The plus sign for a difference is the one line people get wrong: with zero covariance, X − Y is more variable than either part.

The traps, ranked by how often they cost marks

  1. Adding variances of dependent parts.
  2. Reading Cov = 0 as independence.
  3. Quoting a Chebyshev bound above 1 as a result.
  4. Using a normal quantile when no distribution is given.
  5. Forgetting the square on a scale factor, or squaring it twice.
  6. Reporting a variance where a standard deviation was asked.

Quick revision questions

  1. Why can a sum of two random variables be a constant?
  2. What does Cov = 0 forbid, and what does it allow?
  3. Which inequality needs a non-negative variable, and which one avoids that by squaring?
  4. Give a distribution that attains Chebyshev at k = 2.
  5. Why is the finite-population correction negative in its covariance?
