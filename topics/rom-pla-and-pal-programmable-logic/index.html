Topic and objective

TOPIC: ROM, PLA and PAL — the three programmable devices built from one AND plane and one OR plane, and the arithmetic that sizes them.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read any of the three devices as the same two-level picture with a different plane left programmable. Size a ROM from its input and output counts as $$m \cdot 2^n$$ bits. Size a PLA from its three numbers as $$2nk + km$$ programmable links, where n is the number of inputs, k the number of product terms and m the number of outputs. Turn a minimised sum of products into a dot pattern and read a dot pattern back into an expression. Count the product lines a pair of outputs needs on a PLA and on a PAL, and say why the PAL needs more. Recognise the two cases where a device that looks too small still works — don't cares, and a complemented output.

I read this clause as the design half of "Design of combinational and sequential circuits": not how to minimise, which the tabular-method lesson owns, but which device you implement the result on, and what that device costs.

Prerequisites

  • Boolean algebra and minimisation — sums of products, literals, minterms.
  • The tabular method — how the minimum number of product terms is found, which is the k this lesson sizes.
  • Combinational circuits — decoders and multiplexers, because the ROM's AND plane is a decoder.
  • Arithmetic: powers of two, a ceiling, and the difference between bits and bytes.

Nothing here needs a chip, a programmer or any software. Every number is computed on the page.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • One picture, three devices. AND plane, then OR plane; the devices differ in which plane you program.
  • ROM size ignores the function. It is $$m \cdot 2^n$$ bits whatever you store.
  • PLA size follows the function. $$2nk + km$$ links, with k the minimum product-term count.
  • A dot is a literal. The picture is the formula, so it can be read in both directions.
  • PAL cannot share. A term needed twice is built twice, so a PAL needs more lines than the same PLA.

Intuition first: the patch panel

Think of a board with wires running one way and wires running the other, crossing in a grid. At every crossing there is a socket. You make a connection by pushing a plug into a socket; a crossing you leave empty carries nothing. That is the whole of a programmable array: the wires are the inputs, the rows are the product terms, and the plugs are the literals.

Educational interpretation The patch panel carries the dot notation and the idea that a device is sized by its number of sockets rather than by how many you fill. It does not carry the two-plane split or the fixed-OR rule of a PAL, which the next two cards supply.

Formal vocabulary, every term in words

AND plane — the first array. Each horizontal row is one AND gate, and its output is a product term of the inputs.

OR plane — the second array. Each vertical column is one OR gate, summing the product rows dotted into it.

Product term — an AND of literals. Its literals are the variables it names, each either true or complemented.

Programmable — the link at a crossing is yours to make or omit. Fixed — the link was made at manufacture.

n, k, m — inputs, product terms, outputs. A PLA is written n × k × m.

Minterm — a product term naming every variable exactly once, so it is 1 on exactly one input word.

The three devices, defined

ROM — read-only memory. An n-to-2n decoder generates every minterm, and a programmable array stores one bit per output per address. Size: $$m \cdot 2^n$$ bits.

PROM — a ROM whose stored array is programmed once, by burning fuses. EPROM erases with ultraviolet light; EEPROM and flash, electrically. The array structure is the same in all of them.

PLA — programmable logic array. Both planes programmable, k product rows chosen by the designer. Links: $$2nk + km$$.

PAL — programmable array logic. Programmable AND plane, fixed OR plane: each output owns a private group of product lines, typically seven or eight.

The one function this lesson uses throughout

Take $$f(A,B,C,D) = \Sigma m(0,2,5,7,8,10,13,15)$$ — one at exactly the eight input words whose minterm number appears in the list. Minimised, it is $$f = B'D' + BD$$: two product terms, four literals, and no one-term cover exists (the page proves that by searching every single prime implicant).

f = B′D′ + BD   ·   k = 2, literals = 4
ROM: 2⁴ · 1 = 16 bits   ·   PLA: 2·4·2 + 2·1 = 16 + 2 = 18 linksRead k as the number of rows the AND plane must have. The ROM does not use k at all: it always has 16 rows, one per minterm, and stores 8 ones and 8 zeros.

Mathematical derivation The minimisation, the exhaustive evaluation on all sixteen inputs and the search for a smaller cover are all performed by this page when it loads; the results are in the model-check count under the canvas.

The PLA program table

Three columns describe a programmed PLA: the product term, the input literals it dots, and the outputs it feeds.

term B D | f
P1 - 0 - 0 | 1
P2 - 1 - 1 | 1Read a dash as "this variable is not in the term", a 1 as "the true line is dotted", a 0 as "the complemented line is dotted". The output column says which rows are summed into f.

Verified fact The 1 / 0 / dash convention and the true-or-complement output column are the standard PLA programming table of Mano's Digital Design, chapter 7.

Where the two size formulas come from

ROM. There are 2n addresses and m bits stored at each, so the stored array is m · 2n bits. The decoder is fixed, so it adds no programming.

PLA. 2n vertical lines (each input, true and complemented) cross k rows: 2nk links. Those k rows cross m columns: km links. Total 2nk + km.

PAL. Only the AND plane is programmable, so the fuse count is 2n times the total number of product lines, and the OR plane contributes none.

Verified fact Wakerly's Digital Design states the same counts for the Signetics 82S100: 2 × 16 × 48 = 1536 fuses in the AND array and 8 × 48 = 384 in the OR array.

The assumption that makes k a single number

A PLA has one AND plane shared by all outputs, so a product term used by three outputs occupies one row, not three. That is why k is counted across the outputs: list every distinct product term in the whole set of functions, count each once, and k is that count. Counting each output separately gives a larger k and a device that looks bigger than it needs to be.

Mathematical derivation Two outputs F1 = C + A′B and F2 = C′ + A′B need three distinct terms, not four, so k = 3. The same pair on a PAL needs four lines, because the fixed OR plane forbids sharing A′B between the outputs.

Example 1 — size one function on all three devices

Implement $$f(A,B,C,D) = \Sigma m(0,2,5,7,8,10,13,15)$$ on a ROM, on a PLA and on a PAL. Give the size of each.

  1. Minimise: $$f = B'D' + BD$$, so k = 2 and the literals number 4. Two terms, because no single product term covers all eight minterms.
  2. ROM: 24 · 1 = 16 bits, and it stores eight ones. The device is sized by n and m, not by k.
  3. PLA: 2·4·2 + 2·1 = 16 + 2 = 18 links. 2nk in the AND plane, km in the OR plane.
  4. PAL: 2·4·2 = 16 AND-array fuses, and no OR-plane fuses. One output, so no sharing to lose.

Why 32 bits appears. Using 2n words of m bits with n and m swapped. Why 20 links appears. Counting the four input lines twice and forgetting the complement lines are not extra inputs.

Example 2 — two outputs that share a term

Implement $$F_1 = C + A'B$$ and $$F_2 = C' + A'B$$ on a PLA and on a PAL. Give k for each device.

  1. Minimise each output: two terms each. Each function is genuinely two terms; neither reduces to one.
  2. List the distinct terms across both: C, C′, A′B — three. A′B is identical in both functions, so it is one row.
  3. PLA: k = 3, so 2·3·3 + 3·2 = 18 + 6 = 24 links.
  4. PAL: A′B cannot be shared, so the lines are 2 + 2 = 4. Each output owns a private group of product lines.

Why 4 for the PLA. Counting each output separately, which is the single most common error in this topic. Why 6 for the PAL. Assuming the shared term must be triplicated rather than duplicated.

Example 3 — a function that does not fit

A PLA has four inputs, three product terms and one output. Can it implement four-variable parity? If not, what does parity need?

  1. Parity is 1 on an odd number of ones: eight minterms out of sixteen. 2n−1 of them, by symmetry.
  2. No two of those minterms differ in one bit, so nothing merges. Two minterms merge only if they differ in exactly one position.
  3. The minimum is therefore eight terms, and the page confirms that no seven-term cover exists.
  4. It does not fit: k = 3 is a physical row count. An eight-term device is 2·4·8 + 8·1 = 72 links.

Why 4 appears. Reading 2n−1 as 2n/2 and then halving again. Why "try harder" fails. No programming can create a ninth row.

Example 4 — the escapes, in numbers

Two functions must fit a small PLA. (a) f = Σm(0,1,2,3,8,9) with 10 and 11 never occurring. (b) The function that is 0 only at minterm 5. What is the smallest k for each?

  1. (a) without don't cares: B′C′ + A′B′ is two terms. The two groups of four are not adjacent, so they stay separate.
  2. (a) with 10, 11 as don't cares: the single term B′ covers all six required ones. k = 1.
  3. (b) directly: A + B′ + C + D′, four terms. A function that is 1 almost everywhere is a long OR.
  4. (b) by complement: the complement is the single minterm A′BC′D, so k = 1 plus an output inverter.

Why 2 for (b). Reading "complement" as "swap the ones and zeros and re-minimise", which is right — but only the complemented function is programmed, and only if the device can invert its output.

Example 5 — size a device from a specification

A combinational block has eight inputs, four outputs and six distinct product terms after minimisation. Compare the ROM, the PLA and the PAL, assuming the four outputs need twelve product lines on a PAL.

  1. ROM: 28 · 4 = 256 · 4 = 1024 bits, or 128 bytes.
  2. PLA: 2·8·6 + 6·4 = 96 + 24 = 120 links.
  3. PAL: 2·8·12 = 192 fuses, and no OR-plane fuses. Twelve lines because the shared terms are duplicated.
  4. Which is smaller is a question about cost per link, not about the raw numbers. Bits and links are different units.

Why 96 fuses appears. Forgetting the OR plane in the PLA count and reporting only the AND plane. Why 2048 bits appears. Using 28 · 8, counting the inputs as bits.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Each card states the condition a rule needs and what changes when the condition fails. These are the cases that separate a correct answer from an almost-correct one.

An output dotted to nothing, and one dotted to everything

An output column with no dots at all is the constant 0, because nothing is summed into it. A row with no dots is the constant 1, because an AND gate with no inputs is always 1 — Wakerly's standard method for programming a constant output.

Counterexample to "a blank row is a mistake". It is a legitimate constant. The mistake is a row that dots a variable and its complement, which makes the term always 0.

A PAL output with eight terms

Each PAL16L8 output owns eight rows, of which seven feed the OR gate and the eighth enables the output buffer. So the ceiling is seven product terms per output, whatever the device's total row count.

Counterexample to "the PAL has 64 rows, so one output can use 64". The fixed OR plane wires each output to its own group. The device total and the per-output ceiling are different numbers.

k counted per output instead of across outputs

Two outputs needing two terms each need k = 3 when one term is shared, and k = 4 only when no term is shared. The device is sized by the union, not by the sum.

Counterexample to "add the per-output counts". That is the PAL rule, not the PLA rule, and applying it to a PLA overstates the device by exactly the number of shared terms.

A don't care that can occur

Treating minterm 10 as a don't care lets the term B′ cover it and drops k from 2 to 1. If input 10 can actually reach the circuit, the same programming now gives the wrong output there.

Counterexample to "don't cares are free". They are a promise about the input, not a property of the function. A question that says the combination never occurs is licensing the smaller k; one that does not, is not.

Output inversion without an inverter

The complement trick needs a programmable output inverter — the XOR gate with one input tied to a fuse that most PLAs carry. A device without one cannot take the route, and the direct four-term form is the answer.

Counterexample to "just program the complement". Programming the complement of f gives f′ at the pin, not f. The inverter is what turns one into the other.

An n-input ROM whose function is a single minterm

A function that is 1 at exactly one address still costs the full 2n rows, and it stores a single 1 among 2n − 1 zeros. No minimisation reduces the device, because the decoder is fixed.

The rule. ROM size depends on n and m only. It is the one device in this topic whose size is independent of the function, and that is a feature, not an oversight.

Two ROMs of different word widths

A 4K × 8 ROM and a 32K × 1 ROM both hold 32,768 bits, but they are not interchangeable: the first has twelve address lines and eight data lines, the second fifteen and one.

Counterexample to "same bits, same device". Equal capacity with different geometry. A question that gives address and data line counts is asking for geometry, not for capacity.

EPROM is not a different array

PROM, EPROM, EEPROM and flash differ only in how the stored array is written and erased. All four keep the fixed decoder and the programmable OR plane, so all four are sized by m · 2n.

Counterexample to "an erasable ROM is a PLA". Erasability is a property of the storage cell. It says nothing about which plane is programmable.

Six beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each card gives the belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule to use instead. These come from the shapes of wrong options in this topic's questions, not from a survey of learners.

1. "Minimising saves ROM"

Why it tempts. Minimisation is the previous chapter, so it feels like the thing to do first.

Refutation. A two-term function and a sixteen-term function both occupy 16 bits in a four-input ROM, because every address has a stored bit whether it is 1 or 0.

Correct rule. Minimise for a PLA or a PAL, where k is the size. For a ROM, read n and m and multiply.

2. "PLA and PAL differ only in name"

Why it tempts. The letters are almost the same, and both have a programmable AND plane.

Refutation. Two outputs sharing A′B need three lines on a PLA and four on a PAL. The fixed OR plane is the whole difference.

Correct rule. Programmable AND and OR: PLA. Programmable AND, fixed OR: PAL. Write the two planes down before answering.

3. "The ROM's AND plane is programmable"

Why it tempts. The stored array is programmable, so the whole device feels programmable.

Refutation. The AND plane of a ROM is a complete decoder: all 2n minterms are generated whether the function uses them or not.

Correct rule. ROM: fixed decoder, programmable OR. That fixed decoder is exactly why the size is exponential in n.

4. "k is the number of minterms"

Why it tempts. Both are counts of rows, and the ROM's rows are minterms.

Refutation. The worked function has eight minterms and k = 2, because a product term may cover many minterms at once.

Correct rule. k is the number of product terms in the minimised expression, counted across all outputs.

5. "2n input lines means 2n inputs"

Why it tempts. The crossbar shows twice as many vertical lines as the specification has inputs.

Refutation. The extra lines are the complements. Four inputs give eight lines, and the device is still called a four-input PLA.

Correct rule. n inputs, 2n lines. In the link count that factor of two is already inside 2nk.

6. "A bigger k always means a bigger function"

Why it tempts. More rows sounds like more logic.

Refutation. Parity with k = 8 is a very small function with an expensive form; a four-term function with k = 4 may compute far more. k measures the shape of the expression, not the size of the function.

Correct rule. k is the price of a two-level form. Parity is the classic case where the form, not the function, is expensive.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

Three pairs get confused, and each is settled by writing a definition rather than by intuition: fixed against programmable plane, minterms against product terms, and across outputs against per output. In each pair the two members differ by one countable thing — a fuse, a row, a shared term — and the question always says which it wants if you read the unit before computing.

Four patterns cover this topic

Two are arithmetic and two are reading. The arithmetic ones give a small integer; the reading ones give an expression. A verified GATE ECE 2017 Set 2 question asked the second kind — which Boolean function a drawn PLA implements — and the reading skill is identical in CSE questions of the same shape.

Pattern 1 — size a device from a specification

Shape. "A ROM has 12 address lines and 8 data lines. Its size in bits is ___." Or: "A PLA has 16 inputs, 32 product terms and 8 outputs. The number of programmable fuses is ___."

Procedure. Name the device, write its formula, substitute. ROM: m · 2n. PLA: 2nk + km. PAL: 2n × lines, and no OR fuses.

The trap. Reading "8 data lines" as 8 bits per address when the question said words, or forgetting the OR plane in a PLA count.

Pattern 2 — read a drawn dot pattern

Shape. A PLA is drawn with dots, and the question asks which function it implements, or what one product term is.

Procedure. For each row, read the dotted literals and AND them. For each column, read the dotted rows and OR them. Then simplify if an option set demands it.

The trap. Reading a complemented line as a true one. The label above the vertical line is the literal, not the variable.

Pattern 3 — does the function fit the device

Shape. "Can this pair of functions be implemented on a 3 × 4 × 2 PLA?" Or: "the minimum number of product terms required is ___."

Procedure. Minimise all outputs, count distinct terms once, compare with k. If it does not fit, try the complement of one output, or check for don't cares.

The trap. Counting per output. A pair needing 3 and 2 separately may need only 4 together, and a device sized 4 then fits.

Pattern 4 — a statement set about the three devices

Shape. Four or five statements about which plane is programmable, which device can share a term, which is erasable, which needs minimisation. Select all that are true.

Procedure. Test each against a definition you can state aloud: ROM fixed AND, PLA both, PAL fixed OR. Sharing only with a programmable OR plane. Erasability is about the storage cell, not the array.

The trap. "PROM contains a programmable AND array" is false — that is a PLA. The two are swapped more often than any other pair in the topic.

The four-step procedure, and where each step can go wrong

1. Fix n, k and m. Read the three numbers from the specification, and say which is which. Fails when the middle number is read as outputs, or a PAL's per-output group size is read as its total row count.

2. Minimise jointly. Minimise every output, then take the union of product terms. Fails when each output is minimised and counted separately, which overstates k by the number of shared terms.

3. Count the right unit. Bits for a ROM, links for a PLA, fuses for a PAL's AND plane only. Fails when an OR plane is counted on a PAL, which has none.

4. Check the fit. Compare k with the rows, and terms per output with the PAL group size. Fails when a device is declared big enough on its total rows while one output needs more than its group allows.

Why this topic rewards a picture over a memory

There are only two formulas and one picture, and every question in the topic is one of them applied to numbers you are given. NATOriginal practice

A ROM has 10 address lines and 4 data lines. Its storage capacity, in bits, is ______.

Answer: 4096

210 = 1024 addresses, each storing 4 bits: 1024 × 4 = 4096 bits.

Why 40 appears. Multiplying the line counts instead of the word count by the word width. Why 1024 appears. Reporting the address count, which is words, not bits.

MCQOriginal practice

Which statement about the two planes is correct?

  1. A ROM has a programmable AND plane and a fixed OR plane.
  2. A PLA has a fixed AND plane and a programmable OR plane.
  3. A PAL has a programmable AND plane and a fixed OR plane.
  4. All three have both planes programmable.

Answer: C

A PAL programs the AND plane and hard-wires each output OR gate to its own private group of product lines.

Why A. That is the ROM's arrangement reversed. Why B. That is the ROM, not the PLA. Why D. Only the PLA has both.

NATOriginal practice

A PLA has 12 inputs, 24 product terms and 6 outputs. The total number of programmable links is ______.

Answer: 720

2nk + km = 2 × 12 × 24 + 24 × 6 = 576 + 144 = 720.

Why 576. The AND plane alone, forgetting the OR plane. Why 144. The OR plane alone. Why 288. Using n instead of 2n for the input lines.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. A PLA can implement a function that a PAL of the same size cannot, if the function's outputs share product terms.
  2. Minimising a function before storing it in a ROM reduces the ROM's size.
  3. An n-input ROM can store any function of those n inputs.
  4. A PAL has no programmable links in its OR plane.
  5. Four-variable parity can be implemented with two product terms.

Answer: A, C and D

Sharing is what a programmable OR plane buys, so a PLA fits designs a PAL cannot (A). The decoder is complete, so every function of the inputs is storable (C). The PAL's OR plane is fixed, so it has no fuses there (D).

Why B is false. ROM size is m · 2n whatever is stored. Why E is false. Parity needs 2n−1 terms: eight for four variables, as the page proves by searching every smaller cover.

NATOriginal practice

Two functions of three variables must be implemented together. Their minimised forms are F1 = C + A′B and F2 = C′ + A′B. The minimum number of product terms the pair requires on a PLA is ______.

Answer: 3

The distinct terms across both functions are C, C′ and A′B: three. The term A′B is identical in both, so it occupies one row dotted into both output columns.

Why 4. Adding the two per-output counts, which is the PAL rule. Why 2. Counting only one function.

MCQOriginal practice

A PLA is drawn with four inputs A, B, C, D and two product rows. Row 1 dots A, C′ and D; row 2 dots A′, B and C′. Both rows dot output f. Which function does it implement?

  1. f = AC′D + A′BC′
  2. f = ACD + A′BC
  3. f = (A + C′ + D)(A′ + B + C′)
  4. f = AC′D · A′BC′

Answer: A

Read each row as an AND of the dotted literals, and the column as their OR: f = AC′D + A′BC′. The two terms share C′, so a four-input, three-term PLA would also do.

Why B. Dropping the complements. Why C. Reading the OR plane as a product of sums, which would need an AND at the output. Why D. Reading the sum as a product.

Revision sheet

Definitions, counts, formulas, decision procedures and traps, in the order you would use them. Nothing here needs the animation.

The three devices

  • ROM — fixed 2n-line decoder (AND plane) plus a programmable OR plane. Size m · 2n bits. No minimisation needed; every function of the inputs fits.
  • PLA — both planes programmable, n × k × m. Links 2nk + km. Minimise first; terms are shared across outputs.
  • PAL — programmable AND, fixed OR in private groups (typically 7–8 terms per output). Fuses 2n × lines; no OR fuses; no sharing.
  • PROM / EPROM / EEPROM / flash — all ROMs; they differ in how the stored array is written and erased.

Dot notation

  • A dot at (row, vertical line) puts that literal into that product term.
  • A dot at (row, output column) adds that term to that output.
  • No dot: the variable is absent from the term.
  • No dot anywhere on a row: the term is the constant 1.
  • No dot anywhere in a column: the output is the constant 0.
  • A row dotting a variable and its complement: the term is the constant 0 — always a mistake.

The two formulas and one count

ROM: m · 2^n bits
PLA: 2nk + km links
PAL: 2n · (product lines) fuses, and no OR-plane fusesn inputs, k product terms, m outputs. For a PLA, k is counted across all outputs with each distinct term counted once. For a ROM the exponent is the input count, never the output count.

Plugged in: four inputs, two terms, one output gives 16 bits and 18 links. Eight inputs, four outputs, six terms gives 1024 bits and 120 links. Sixteen by forty-eight by eight gives 1920 links.

Numbers from this lesson worth remembering

  • One function, all three devices: 16 bits, 18 links, 16 AND fuses.
  • The sharing pair: 3 PLA lines against 4 PAL lines, 24 links against 24 fuses.
  • Four-variable parity: 8 terms, 72 links, and no 7-term cover exists.
  • Don't cares: 2 terms become 1. Inversion: 4 terms become 1 plus an inverter.
  • 82S100: 1536 + 384 = 1920. PAL16L8: 64 × 32 = 2048 fuses, 7 terms per output.
  • Four-variable functions: 65,536, all storable in one 16-bit ROM.

Decision procedure, in four lines

  1. Fix n, k and m from the specification. The middle number is product terms; a PAL's group size is not its row count.
  2. Minimise jointly and count distinct terms once. Sharing is the PLA's whole advantage.
  3. Count the unit asked for: bits, links or fuses. A PAL has no OR-plane fuses to count.
  4. Check the fit, and if it fails try don't cares, then inversion. Both change the function you program, not the device.

One sentence, and the five slips

The ROM stores the answer, the PLA stores the terms, and the PAL stores the terms without sharing them.

The five slips: minimising for a ROM · treating PLA and PAL as the same · calling a ROM's AND plane programmable · using the minterm count as k · counting k per output instead of across outputs. Write the device's two planes at the top of your working and none of the five survives.

  • John F. Wakerly, Digital Design: Principles and Practices, section 5.3 — "an n × m PLA with p product terms contains p 2n-input AND gates and m p-input OR gates"; the Signetics 82S100 with 16 inputs, 48 AND gates and 8 outputs, giving 2 × 16 × 48 = 1536 fuses in the AND array and 8 × 48 = 384 in the OR array; and the PAL16L8 with 64 rows crossing 32 columns, 2048 fuses, seven terms per output. Also the statement that the worst case for two-level AND-OR design is n-input parity, which needs 2n−1 product terms.
  • A university lecture on programmable logic devices (Concordia COEN 312) confirming the same three-way split: ROM fixed AND and programmable OR, PAL programmable AND and fixed OR, PLA both programmable.

What the research did not cover, and the assumptions the model makes

The PLA link count is the count of possible crossings, 2nk + km, and excludes the output inverters that many PLAs carry; a question that counts those fuses will be larger by m. The PAL figures use the PAL16L8 as the standard example, whose eight rows per output are seven OR inputs plus one output-enable term; other PAL parts have different group sizes, so the per-output ceiling must be read from the part, not assumed to be seven. The 82S100 and 16L8 numbers are taken from Wakerly's description rather than from a manufacturer datasheet, and no datasheet was opened in this session. The lesson treats a don't care as an input combination that never occurs, which is the assumption that licenses the smaller k; if the combination can occur the programming is wrong, not merely smaller. Nothing here was checked against a printed edition page by page, and no claim is made about how often this material appears in GATE CSE papers — only that the design clause it belongs to is in the syllabus.
