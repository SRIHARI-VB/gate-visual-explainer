Topic and objective

TOPIC: Group actions — a group whose elements move the points of a set — and the three objects every action produces: the orbit, the stabiliser, and the average that counts orbits, Burnside’s lemma.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State the two axioms of an action and see them checked on twelve real actions; compute an orbit as the reachable set and see orbits partition the set; compute a stabiliser and verify it is a subgroup that need not be normal; use |G| = |orbit| × |stabiliser| on every point; count orbits by averaging fixed points instead of dividing; run that average on 16 and 81 colourings and on 64 six-bead strings to get 6, 24 and 14; and recognise the same two results inside Cayley’s theorem and in the count of conjugacy classes.

I read the request as the machinery that turns “how many distinct objects” into an average. Monoids and groups owns the axioms, subgroups, cosets and Lagrange; kernels and quotients owns the map and the quotient; counting names Burnside once, to explain why naive division fails, and sets it aside. This page owns what all three leave: the action, the orbit, the stabiliser and the counting theorem.

GATE relevance: class B

Section 1 of the GATE 2027 Computer Science syllabus prints “Monoids, Groups” and, under Combinatorics, “counting”. The words group action, orbit, stabiliser and Burnside are not printed.

They are the vocabulary that turns a group question into a counting question, and “how many distinct colourings up to rotation” is a standard counting shape. This page is classified B — the machinery behind two named syllabus objects. VERIFIED FACT for the syllabus wording.

Prerequisites

Needed: what a group is, the order of an element, subgroups and cosets — all in Monoids and groups. Also a function, its image and injectivity, in Functions and their properties.

Helpful: kernels and quotients, because conjugation — the g h g-1 move — is built there and used here twice, for normality and for the conjugacy action.

Not needed: Sylow theory, the orbit-counting version for infinite groups, Pólya’s enumeration theorem, or character theory. None is used or assumed here.

1. Intuition, and 2. the formal definition

Intuition. A kaleidoscope. The mirrors can be turned and flipped, and whatever you do to the tube, some patterns look identical. The group is the set of moves; the set is the patterns; the orbit of a pattern is every pattern you can reach from it. Counting the genuinely different patterns means counting orbits, and the trap is that symmetric patterns appear fewer times in the list of all patterns, so dividing by the number of moves is wrong. EDUCATIONAL INTERPRETATION

Formal. Let G be a group and X a set. An action of G on X is a map sending a pair (a group element g, a point x) to a point written g·x, obeying two rules: e·x = x for every x, where e is the identity; and (gh)·x = g·(h·x) for every g, h and x. Nothing else. There is no bijection between G and X, and no size relation. VERIFIED FACT

The orbit of x is the set of all g·x as g runs over G. The stabiliser of x is the set of g with g·x = x. Two definitions, and the rest of the page is consequences.

3. Visual understanding: two blocks, not one

Draw the six segments of a square: four sides and two diagonals. Apply all eight symmetries and watch where each segment can go. A side reaches only the other sides; a diagonal reaches only the other diagonal.

That picture is the whole idea of an orbit, and it immediately corrects the common assumption that one action gives one block. This action gives two, of sizes 4 and 2. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

4. Worked examples on this page

D4 on four corners (one orbit of 4, stabiliser 2, 8 = 4 × 2); D4 on six segments (orbits 4 and 2, stabilisers 2 and 4); S3 on three points (orbit 3, stabiliser 2); C4 on the sixteen two-colourings (average 24/4 = 6); D4 on the eighty-one three-colourings (168/8 = 21); C6 and D6 on sixty-four six-bead strings (14 and 13).

Each is computed from a move table, so “this is an action” and “this is the orbit” are verdicts here, not assertions.

5. Boundaries: what the definitions do and do not give

Do: the identity fixes everything, orbits partition the set, the stabiliser is a subgroup, orbit times stabiliser equals the group order, and the average of fixed points is the number of orbits.

Do not: give one orbit, give equal orbit sizes, make the stabiliser normal, or make “divide the set size by the group order” correct. Burnside’s lemma needs a finite group; the version for infinite groups is a different statement and is not used here. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

6. Edge cases to hold on to

One point. If the set has a single point, the orbit has size 1, the stabiliser is the whole group, and the count of orbits is 1 — the average gives |G|/|G| = 1.

Trivial group. If the group has one element, every orbit has size 1, and the number of orbits is the size of the set. This is the case where naive division happens to be right.

The same number twice. Four corners and two colours give 6 by rotation and 6 with reflections as well. That coincidence is not a rule: at six beads the two answers are 14 and 13.

7–9. GATE, practice, consolidation

GATE application. Two shapes only: count distinct objects under a symmetry group, and use the size identity to get one of orbit size, stabiliser size or group order from the other two. Both reduce to naming the group and the set.

Example 1: the action every question is built on

Action. The eight symmetries of a square act on its four corners. Follow corner 0: the identity and the diagonal reflection leave it at 0; the quarter turn sends it to 1; the half turn to 2; the three-quarter turn to 3; the other three reflections send it to 3, 2 and 1.

Orbit {0,1,2,3}, size 4. Stabiliser {e, s}, size 2. Product 4 × 2 = 8, the order of D4. Orbits one, so the action is transitive. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Why it is the reference example. Every quantity on this page is visible at once, and the stabiliser is small enough to list. If you can explain this action, you can explain the theorem.

Example 2: two orbits of different sizes

The same group on the six segments. The four sides form one orbit; the two diagonals form another. A side is fixed by two symmetries, a diagonal by four.

8 = 4 × 2 and 8 = 2 × 4. The two rows multiply to the same group order with the factors swapped, which is exactly the trade the theorem describes. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 3: counting by averaging

Two colours on the four corners, up to rotation. The four rotations fix 16, 2, 4 and 2 colourings. Sum 24, divide by 4: 6.

The page also lists the sixteen colourings and groups them, and finds six blocks. Two independent computations, same number. The naive 16/4 = 4 is wrong, and 16/8 = 2 with reflections is wrong too.

Example 4: the same average on a necklace

Six beads, two colours, rotations allowed. The six rotations fix 64, 2, 4, 8, 4 and 2 strings: sum 84, and 84/6 = 14.

Allow flipping as well and the twelve symmetries sum to 156, so 156/12 = 13. The difference of one is a single pair of necklaces that are mirror images and not rotations of each other.

Example 5: the formula, and where it comes from

With k colours on four corners, rotations give (k4 + k2 + 2k)/4 and the full symmetry group gives (k4 + 2k3 + 3k2 + 2k)/8.

Each term is one symmetry type: k4 for the identity, k2 for the half turn, k for each quarter turn, k3 for each diagonal reflection, k2 for each side-midpoint reflection. Both are checked against enumerating every colouring for k from 1 to 4. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Counterexample: “divide by the group order”

Sixteen two-colourings of the square, eight symmetries. Sixteen divided by eight is 2. The true number of distinct colourings is 6. The division is not merely inaccurate; for six beads it does not even give a whole number, since 64/6 = 10.67 against a true 14.

The reason: the all-one-colour painting appears once in the list of sixteen, a two-and-two painting appears four times, a three-and-one painting appears four times. Different multiplicities mean no single divisor exists. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Assumption: the group is finite

Burnside’s average divides by the order of the group, so the group must be finite. Every group on this page is finite (orders 4, 6, 8 and 12).

The orbit-stabiliser identity itself is also a statement about finite sizes. For an infinite group the counting version needs different language, and nothing on this page applies to it.

Boundary: the trivial group, and one point

If G has one element, every orbit has size 1 and the number of orbits is the size of the set. Here the naive division is right, which is why it survives in the cases people first try.

If the set has one point, the orbit is that point and the stabiliser is the whole group; the average gives |G|/|G| = 1.

Boundary: which group is meant

“Necklace” normally allows turning but not flipping: six beads, two colours, 14. “Bracelet”, or a phrase like “you may turn it over”, allows flipping: 13.

On a square, rotations alone give 24 with three colours and the full symmetry group gives 21. Reading which group is intended changes the answer, and no amount of arithmetic repairs a wrong group.

Edge case: a free action

A group acting on itself by left multiplication is free: only the identity fixes an element. Every orbit has size equal to the group order, so there is exactly one orbit and the count is 1.

Here the naive division happens to be right again — 6/6 = 1 — because all multiplicities are equal. That is the one situation in which dividing works.

Closely confused pair: orbit size against stabiliser size

On a diagonal of the square: the orbit has size 2 and the stabiliser has size 4. On a corner: orbit 4, stabiliser 2. Both satisfy 8 = product, so the identity cannot tell you which is which.

The orbit is where the point can go; the stabiliser is what leaves it alone. A point that can go many places is usually left alone by few symmetries, and the product is fixed.

1. “The number of distinct objects is the total divided by the group size”

False, and the counterexample is on this page: 16 colourings, 8 symmetries, answer 6 and not 2. The division assumes every object is counted the same number of times, and a symmetric object is counted fewer times.

The repair is the average: sum the colourings fixed by each element and divide by the group order. That weighting is exactly what the multiplicities need. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

2. “One action has one orbit”

False. D4 on the six segments has two orbits, of sizes 4 and 2. Any two points in one orbit are reachable from each other; a side and a diagonal are not, because a symmetry preserves lengths and a side and a diagonal have different lengths.

Transitive is a property of an action, and it must be checked, not assumed.

3. “A stabiliser is normal because it is a subgroup”

False. The stabiliser of a corner in D4 is {e, s}, where s is the reflection across the diagonal 0–2. Conjugating it by the quarter turn gives {e, r2s}, the stabiliser of corner 2. Two different subgroups, so it is not normal.

What is true: the stabilisers of points in the same orbit are conjugate to each other. That is why they all have the same size, and why none of them is normal unless the orbit has one point. Checked on 98 pairs here.

4. “An orbit and a coset are the same thing”

They have the same size and the same structure, and that is where the resemblance stops. A coset is a subset of the group; an orbit is a subset of the set being acted on. The cosets of the stabiliser in G correspond one-to-one with the points of the orbit — that correspondence is the proof of the size identity, not an identification of the two objects.

Keeping them apart is what stops “the orbit is a subgroup” from being written in an answer.

5. “If two numbers agree at four corners they agree in general”

Two colours on four corners give 6 either way, by rotation and with reflections. It is tempting to conclude that flipping never matters. At six beads and two colours the answers are 14 and 13, and with three colours on a square they are 24 and 21.

The agreement at four corners and two colours is a coincidence of that one row of the table. The general statement is that reflections can only merge orbits, so the reflection count is never larger.

Pattern 1: “how many distinct objects”

The question. A number of colourings, labelings or shapes, with a symmetry rule saying when two count as the same.

The method. Name the group (rotations, or rotations and reflections), name the set (colourings of four corners, of six positions, of edges), count the fixed points of each element, add, divide by the group order. The fixed-point count of a permutation with c cycles is kc for k colours.

The common wrong approach. Dividing the total number of colourings by the group size. It gives 2 instead of 6 on the square and a non-integer on six beads. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Pattern 2: the size identity as an equation

Two of the three numbers are given and the third is asked: orbit size from group order and stabiliser size, or stabiliser size from group order and orbit size.

Read it as |G| = |orbit| × |stab|, and check that the answer divides the group order. A stabiliser size that does not divide it is impossible.

Pattern 3: is this a valid action?

A candidate rule is given, and one of the two axioms fails. The usual failure is the identity not fixing everything, or the composition rule holding for single moves but not for products.

Test on one element first: compute the product in both orders, g·(h·x) and (gh)·x. One disagreement settles it.

Pattern 4: orbit or stabiliser, read off a table

A group is given as permutations and a point is named. The orbit is the set of images of that point; the stabiliser is the set of permutations fixing it. Both are read off the same table, in one column and one row.

A shortcut for the stabiliser size: it equals the group order divided by the orbit size, so count the images first and divide.

Pattern 5: conjugacy classes and centralisers

Conjugation is an action of a group on itself, so its orbits are the conjugacy classes and its stabilisers are the centralisers. A question asking for the number of classes is a Burnside question in disguise.

S3 has 3 classes (18/6) and D4 has 5 (40/8), both computed here from the fixed-point counts.

What a full-marks answer contains

The named group and its order; the named set and its size; the fixed-point count of each element, or the orbit and stabiliser of the named point; the arithmetic written out with the division by the group order explicit; and, when a number looks surprising, the check that every orbit size divides the group order.

MCQ original practice

The eight symmetries of a square act on its six segments (four sides and two diagonals). How many orbits does this action have?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 6

Answer: (2), two orbits. A symmetry preserves lengths, and the sides all have length equal to the side of the square while the diagonals all have length equal to the diagonal. So no symmetry carries a side to a diagonal: the four sides form one orbit of size 4 and the two diagonals another of size 2.

Option 1 is the transitive assumption, and it is wrong here. Option 3 counts the two lengths plus something else with no derivation. Option 6 is the number of segments, which is the count of orbits only for the trivial group.

NAT original practice

The group D4 of order 8 acts on the four corners of a square. What is the size of the stabiliser of one corner?

Answer: 2. The identity fixes every corner, and exactly one reflection fixes a given corner, namely the one across the diagonal through that corner and the opposite one. The other six symmetries move it. So the stabiliser is {e, s} and its size is 2, consistent with 8 = 4 × 2.

The two standard wrong answers are 1, which assumes the action is free, and 4, which is the orbit size: the two are swapped in the size identity.

MCQ original practice

Each corner of a square is painted one of two colours. Two paintings count as the same if a rotation of the square carries one to the other. How many distinct paintings are there?

  1. 4
  2. 6
  3. 8
  4. 16

Answer: (2), 6. The four rotations fix 16, 2, 4 and 2 colourings of the sixteen, so the average is 24/4 = 6. The blocks are: all one colour (2), one odd corner (2), two adjacent (1), two opposite (1) — six in total.

Option 1 is 16 divided by 4, the naive division. Option 4 is 16 divided by 8, the naive division with reflections. Option 3 is a decoy: with reflections as well the count is still 6 here, not 8.

NAT original practice

A necklace has six beads in a circle, each bead one of two colours. Two necklaces are the same if a rotation of the circle carries one to the other. How many distinct necklaces are there?

Answer: 14. The six rotations fix 64, 2, 4, 8, 4 and 2 strings of the 64: the identity fixes all, a one-bead or five-bead turn forces all beads equal, a two-bead or four-bead turn forces period two, and a half turn forces period three. The sum is 84 and 84/6 = 14.

The trap is 64/6 = 10.67, which is not even an integer. If the question allowed flipping over as well the answer would be 13, not 14.

MSQ original practice

A group G acts on a finite set X. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. The stabiliser of any point is a subgroup of G.
  2. The stabiliser of any point is a normal subgroup of G.
  3. Two orbits of the same action may have different sizes.
  4. The number of orbits equals the average over G of the number of points fixed by each element.

Answer: (1), (3) and (4). (1) is closure and inverses: if g and h fix x then so does gh, and so does g-1. (3) is the six segments of a square, with orbits of sizes 4 and 2. (4) is Burnside’s lemma, which the page checks against brute force on twelve actions.

(2) is false: the stabiliser of a corner in D4 is {e, s}, and conjugating it by the quarter turn gives a different subgroup. Stabilisers of one orbit are conjugate, and conjugates are equal only when the orbit has a single point.

NAT original practice

Each corner of a square is painted one of three colours. Two paintings count as the same if a rotation carries one to the other, and reflections are not allowed. How many distinct paintings are there?

Answer: 24. With k = 3 the rotation formula (k4 + k2 + 2k)/4 gives (81 + 9 + 6)/4 = 96/4 = 24. The page also counts the 81 colourings by search and groups them into 24 blocks.

The trap is 21, which is the answer when reflections are also allowed — the difference of three is the three paintings that are mirror images only. The other trap is 81/4, which is not an integer.

MCQ original practice

Six positions in a circle are each given one of three colours. A permutation g of the positions consists of the three transpositions (1 2)(3 4)(5 6). How many colourings are fixed by g?

  1. 3
  2. 9
  3. 27
  4. 729

Answer: (3), 27. A colouring is fixed when each cycle of g is monochromatic, so each cycle contributes one free colour choice. There are three cycles and three colours, so 33 = 27.

Option 1 counts cycles instead of colourings. Option 9 uses two colours by habit, or counts cycles squared. Option 729 is 36, the count of all colourings, which would be right only for the identity.

The six facts to carry

1. An action is two rules: e·x = x, and (gh)·x = g·(h·x). Nothing else is required.

2. The orbit of x is everything reachable; orbits partition the set, so their sizes add to the set size.

3. The stabiliser of x is the set of elements fixing it. It is always a subgroup, and it need not be normal.

4. |G| = |orbit| × |stab|, so each of the two divides the group order.

5. Burnside: number of orbits = (sum over elements of the points each fixes) / |G|, for a finite group.

6. A permutation with c cycles fixes kc colourings, which is where every fixed-point count comes from.

The two formulas

Decision procedure for an exam question

1. Name the group and its order.

2. Name the set and its size.

3. Count the points fixed by each element, using cycle counts.

4. Add, divide by the group order, and check the answer is a whole number no larger than the set size.

If the question gives two of orbit size, stabiliser size and group order, use the product instead and skip step 3.

The four traps, one line each

Dividing the total by the group order: 16/8 = 2, answer 6.

Using the wrong group: 14 against 13, 24 against 21.

Swapping orbit and stabiliser: 8 = 2 × 4 on a diagonal.

“Subgroup therefore normal”: the corner stabiliser is conjugate to another subgroup.

One line to carry into the exam

Orbits are what you count, stabilisers are how much symmetry one point has, and the average over the group joins the two. Name which of the three a question is asking, and the method follows.
