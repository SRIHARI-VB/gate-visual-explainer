Topic and objective

TOPIC: Number representation and arithmetic — fixed point (unsigned and signed, with an explicit scale); sign-magnitude, ones' complement and two's complement; the range, the step and the count of distinct values of each; negation, sign extension and one-pass negation; addition and subtraction with a single adder; the carry flag against the overflow flag; normalised binary scientific notation; the IEEE 754 single and double formats, their biases and hidden bit; the four exponent regimes, denormals, zero, infinity and NaN; precision, ulp, rounding and absorbed additions.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a bit string, a width and a representation, state its value; given a value, produce its bits; and given an arithmetic operation, say whether the result is correct, what the carry flag holds, and whether overflow occurred. For IEEE 754: decode any 32-bit or 64-bit pattern, encode any value that the format can hold, name the special values, and compute the range and the gap at a given magnitude.

I am treating this as the whole of the syllabus item "Number representation and arithmetic (fixed and floating point)" and as the representation layer that the next two Digital Logic lessons and the whole of Computer Organization sit on. Boolean algebra and gate minimisation belong to the previous lesson; adders, multiplexers and flip-flops belong to the next one. Arithmetic *algorithms* — Booth recoding, restoring division, carry-lookahead — are not in this lesson.

Prerequisites

  • Positional binary: reading 1011 as 8 + 0 + 2 + 1. Nothing beyond counting in binary and knowing that the leftmost bit is the most significant.
  • Powers of two up to about 210, and negative powers such as 2−3 = 0.125.
  • Boolean algebra and minimisation — not needed for the arithmetic, but the previous lesson explains what a bit column is and why a complement is a row of inverters.
  • No programming, no calculus. Hexadecimal appears only as a compact way to write 32 bits.

The five ideas

  1. A bit pattern has no meaning until you name the representation. The same 10110110 is 182, 11.375 or −74 depending on the convention.
  2. Two's complement is arithmetic modulo 2n: the pattern arithmetic is always right, and only the flag logic needs to know about signs.
  3. Carry and overflow are different questions — one about the fifth bit, one about the sign — and neither implies the other.
  4. A float is a sign, a biased exponent and a fraction with an implied leading 1. The bias exists so that the exponent has no sign bit and so that the bit order is the numeric order.
  5. The exponent field, not the magnitude, decides whether a pattern is zero, denormal, normal, infinite or NaN.

Conventions used throughout

Bits are written most significant first, in groups of four. n is the width in bits and f is the number of fraction bits, so a fixed-point format has i = n − f integer bits. Two's complement is written with an apostrophe and means the representation, not the operation. For a floating-point number, S is the sign bit, E is the stored exponent field and e = E − bias is the true exponent; F is the stored fraction and the significand is 1.F for normal numbers and 0.F when E = 0. "ulp" means one unit in the last place, the gap between neighbouring representable values. Hexadecimal is written 0x… or with a trailing H, exactly as the papers write it.

1. Intuition — a ruler with a fixed set of marks

A fixed-point number is a ruler. The marks are at a fixed spacing — 0.125 apart if the number has three fraction bits — and every value you can write down must be one of the marks. Two bits patterns that look different are two different marks; two real numbers closer together than the spacing cannot be told apart at all. That is all a representation is: a finite set of marks, plus a rule for naming them.

A floating-point number is a different kind of ruler: one whose spacing changes. Near 1 the marks are 2−23 apart, near 2 they are twice as far apart, near 1024 twice as far again. The advantage is that the ruler reaches 1038; the cost is that far from zero the marks are coarse. Every question in this topic is a question about which marks a format has.

Educational interpretation The ruler is an analogy for orientation. Nothing after this card depends on it, and the ranges and gaps it mentions are computed in the Animation tab rather than asserted.

2. Formal definition — fixed point

X = Σk=−fi−1 bk · 2kThe value of an n-bit fixed-point pattern with f fraction bits, where i = n − f. Each bit bk is 0 or 1 and carries the weight 2k. The point sits between position 0 and position −1, and it never moves, which is why the format is called fixed.
0 ≤ X ≤ 2i − 2−f,   step = 2−fThe range and the spacing. The maximum is one step below 2i, because the largest pattern is all ones. This is the answer to GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q7.

Derivation The largest pattern has every bit set, so its value is the sum of all the weights, which is a geometric series equal to 2i − 2−f.

3. Formal definition — the three signed encodings

sign-magnitude: (−1)bn−1 · (bn−2…b0)The top bit is a sign and the rest is an unsigned magnitude. Range −(2n−1 − 1) to 2n−1 − 1, and two patterns for zero.
two's complement: −bn−1·2n−1 + Σk=0n−2 bk·2kThe top bit carries a negative weight. Range −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1, one zero, and 2n distinct values. Negation is complement-and-add-one.
ones' complement: the negation is the bitwise complementRange −(2n−1 − 1) to 2n−1 − 1, two patterns for zero, 2n − 1 distinct values. Historically important, and the reason the "end-around carry" exists; no modern machine uses it for integers.

Verified fact That two's complement is the representation used by current general-purpose machines is stated by the standard reference on signed number representations and follows from the single-zero and single-adder properties shown in scenes two to four.

4. Visual understanding — the wheel, and the two flags

All 2n patterns of an n-bit word form a ring when you read them as unsigned and step by one. Two's complement relabels that ring by subtracting 2n from everything past the halfway point, so the ring reads 0, 1, 2, … up to 2n−1 − 1 and then continues −2n−1, …, −1. Addition walks around the ring; negation reflects across it.

overflow ⇔ Cout ⊕ Cin to the sign columnTwo flags leave an n-bit adder. Cout is the (n+1)-th bit of an unsigned sum. Overflow is the exclusive-or of the carry into the sign column and the carry out of it, which is the same as saying: two operands of one sign produced a result of the other sign.

Neither flag implies the other: adding two negative numbers always carries out and usually does not overflow; adding two large positives never carries out and can overflow.

5. Formal definition — IEEE 754

normal: (−1)S · (1.F) · 2E−127,   1 ≤ E ≤ 254Single precision: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits, bias 127. The 1 before the point is implied and not stored, so the significand carries 24 significant bits.
denormal: (−1)S · (0.F) · 2−126,   E = 0, F ≠ 0The hidden bit becomes 0 and the exponent is pinned at −126. The pattern with E = 0 and F = 0 is zero. With E = 255, a zero fraction is infinity and a non-zero fraction is NaN.
double: 1 / 11 / 52, bias 1023,   normal (−1)S · (1.F) · 2E−102364 bits in total. Same four regimes, with 2−1022 as the smallest normal and 2−1074 as the smallest denormal.

Verified fact The field widths, the biases, the hidden bit, the denormal rule and the special values are those of the IEEE 754 standard for binary32 and binary64. The lesson quotes no clause number, because the standard document itself was not opened for this run.

6. Worked examples — one line each, with the reason

Fixed point. 10110.110 with three fraction bits is 16 + 4 + 2 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 22.75. Why: the weights are 24 down to 2−3, and only the positions holding a 1 contribute.

Sign extension. −28 in sixteen bits is 1111 1111 1110 0100. Why: +28 is 0000 0000 0001 1100; complementing gives 1111 1111 1110 0011 and adding one gives the answer. Zero-padding would give 0000 0000 1110 0100, which is +228.

Decode. S=1, E=10000001, F=1111… gives −7.75. Why: E = 129 so e = 2; 1.1111₂ = 1.9375; 1.9375 × 4 = 7.75, and the sign makes it negative.

Encode. −14.25 is 0xC1640000. Why: 14.25 = 1110.01₂ = 1.11001₂ × 23; the exponent field is 3 + 127 = 130 = 10000010; the fraction is 11001 followed by eighteen zeros; the sign bit is 1. Grouping into nibbles: 1100 0001 0110 0100 0000 … = C1640000.

7. Boundaries — what each rule assumes

  • The complement identity assumes a fixed width. "Invert and add one" is a statement about n-bit patterns. In a wider word the same value has a different pattern, and the identity still holds at that width — which is exactly why sign extension is needed when you widen.
  • The overflow rule assumes two's complement. In sign-magnitude, overflow is a statement about the magnitude field, and the sign-bit rule gives wrong answers. In ones' complement there is also an end-around carry to handle.
  • The IEEE formula assumes E in 1…254. With E = 0 the hidden bit is 0 and the exponent is −126, and with E = 255 the value is not a number at all. Applying the normal formula outside its range is the commonest single error in this topic.
  • Precision is a property of the significand, range of the exponent. Adding exponent bits extends the range and changes nothing about precision; adding fraction bits does the reverse.
  • Exactness is a property of the value, not the format. 0.5, 0.25, 14.25 and every integer up to 224 are exact in single precision. 0.1, 0.2 and 1/3 are not exact in any binary format, at any width.

8. Edge cases that decide marks

  • The most negative value. In two's complement −2n−1 is its own negation, so negating it, or taking its absolute value, overflows. It is the one pattern for which "invert and add one" returns itself.
  • Subtracting the most negative value. A − (−2n−1) cannot be computed as "add the negation", because that negation does not exist at this width; the flag is raised spuriously. This lesson's model finds the exception by search over every pair of widths three to eight.
  • Signed zero. +0 and −0 have different bit patterns and compare equal. A question that asks how many distinct patterns represent zero and a question that asks how many distinct values are representable are different questions.
  • NaN ≠ NaN. The only value in IEEE 754 that is not equal to itself. It is also the reason a comparison of two floats can be false in both directions.
  • The smallest normal is not 2−127. With E = 1 the value is 1.0 × 21−127 = 2−126; 2−127 is a denormal, representable but with fewer significant bits.
  • Ties to even. 224 + 1 is exactly halfway between 224 and 224 + 2, and rounds down to the even neighbour; 224 + 3 rounds up. Both are correct roundings, and the direction is not "always up".

9. Consolidation — the six sentences to keep

A pattern means nothing without its representation. Two's complement is arithmetic modulo 2n with a lopsided range, because one pattern is its own negation. One adder serves both addition and subtraction, and the discarded carry is not an error. Carry is about the fifth bit, overflow is about the sign, and neither implies the other. A float is a sign, a biased exponent and an implied-1 fraction, and the exponent field alone decides whether the value is ordinary, denormal, infinite or NaN. Precision is 24 bits in single and 53 in double, and no width makes one tenth exact.

Example 1 — fixed point, and the range question in words

Question. An 8-bit unsigned fixed-point format uses 3 bits for the fraction. Give the value of 10110110, the largest value, the smallest non-zero value, and the value that would follow the largest.

Value. Split as 10110.110. Weights: 16 + 0 + 4 + 2 + 0 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0 = 22.75.

Largest. All ones, 11111.111 = 31.875 = 25 − 2−3. Smallest positive. 00000.001 = 0.125 = 2−3. The value after the largest. 32.0 is not representable — it needs six integer bits. Adding one step to 11111.111 carries out of the word and leaves 00000.000, so the format wraps to zero.

The lesson. The upper bound of the format is a value it cannot hold, and the gap between "largest" and "next" is not a step but an absence. This is the arithmetic counterpart of the overflow flag.

Example 2 — the three encodings of one value

Write −6 in 8 bits, three ways.

sign-magnitude: 1000 0110Sign 1, magnitude 000 0110 = 6.
ones' complement: 1111 1001+6 is 0000 0110; invert every bit.
two's complement: 1111 1010Invert and add one. Note that 1111 1010 in 8 bits and 1010 in 4 bits are the same value at different widths.

GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 Q25 asks which patterns are valid two's complement representations of −6 at 8 and 16 bits: 1111 1010 and 1111 1111 1111 1010 are; 1000 1010 and 1000 0000 0000 1010 are not, because they are sign-magnitude-like patterns with an extra leading 1.

Example 3 — subtraction, column by column

Compute 0000 1011 − 0011 0100 in 8-bit two's complement, that is 11 − 52.

The negation of 0011 0100 is 1100 1100 (invert to 1100 1011, add one). Then 0000 1011 + 1100 1100 = 1101 0111 with no carry out. Read 1101 0111 back: the top bit is 1, so invert and add one to get 0010 1001 = 41, so the value is −41. And 11 − 52 = −41.

The lesson. The borrow never appears. Subtraction is addition of the negation, and the only care needed is that the negation is formed at the same width as the operands.

Example 4 — the flag that was not asked for

In 4 bits, add 1011 and 1110.

1011 is −5, 1110 is −2, and the true sum is −7 = 1001, which is representable. The column addition produces a carry out of the top column: 1011 + 1110 = 1 1001. So the carry flag is 1 and the overflow flag is 0.

If the question had said the operands were unsigned, 11 + 14 = 25, and the correct answer would be 9 with a carry — the carry flag being the signal that the fifth bit exists. Same circuit, same bits, two different correct answers. This is the pair that the 2017 and 2022 papers test.

Example 5 — decode a single-precision constant without a calculator

Question. What is 0xC0F80000 as a decimal number?

Step 1, expand to bits. C = 1100, 0 = 0000, F = 1111, 8 = 1000, then five zeros: 1100 0000 1111 1000 0000 0000 0000 0000.

Step 2, cut the fields. S = 1; E = 1000 0001 = 129; F = 1111 followed by nineteen zeros.

Step 3, unbiased exponent. 129 − 127 = 2.

Step 4, significand. 1.1111₂ = 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.0625 = 1.9375.

Step 5, multiply. −1 × 1.9375 × 4 = −7.75. This is GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q24 in the order the exam gave it: the three fields were printed and only the arithmetic was left.

Example 6 — encode a decimal value, and check it

Encode 3.5 in single precision and verify by decoding.

3.5 = 11.1₂ = 1.11₂ × 21. Exponent field 1 + 127 = 128 = 10000000. Fraction 110 then zeros. Sign 0. So 0100 0000 0110 0000 … = 0x40600000.

Check. S = 0, E = 128 → e = 1, significand 1.75, and 1.75 × 2 = 3.5. Always decode your own answer. The check costs fifteen seconds and catches the two commonest errors, a bias that was not subtracted and a fraction that included the hidden bit.

Example 7 — where the precision goes

In single precision, compute 16777216 + 1.

16777216 = 224. At that magnitude the gap between neighbouring singles is 2, so the two candidates for the sum are 224 and 224 + 2, and the exact sum lies exactly halfway. Ties go to the even neighbour, which is 224, so the answer is 16777216 — the one added vanishes.

In double precision the same sum is exact, because 224 + 1 needs 25 significant bits and double has 53. The lesson: "the computer got it wrong" is the wrong description; the format had no mark there, and rounding chose the nearest one.

Example 8 — comparing two floats without decoding them

Which is larger, 0x41200000 or 0x40E00000?

Both are positive, so the biased exponent makes the bit patterns order the same way as the values. 0x41200000 > 0x40E00000 as unsigned integers, so it is the larger number. (They are 10.0 and 7.0.)

Why this is worth knowing. It turns a class of multiple-choice questions into one integer comparison, and it explains why the exponent is biased rather than stored in two's complement. It does not work across the sign bit, and it does not work when one operand is a NaN.

Edge cases, boundary cases and the exceptions that break a rule

Each of these is a case where a rule that is correct almost everywhere gives the wrong answer. They are collected here because they are exactly what a well-set question asks about.

The integer boundaries

  • −2n−1 is its own negation. In 4 bits, 1000 is −8 and negating gives 1000 again. So |−8| is not representable, and a program that takes the absolute value of the most negative integer is relying on undefined behaviour in C.
  • Subtracting −2n−1 raises a spurious overflow. A − (−2n−1) is computed as A + (−2n−1), because the negation cannot be formed; the pattern arithmetic still gives the right answer when the result fits, but the flag is set. The model searches all pairs of widths 3 to 8 and finds 252 such cases.
  • Adding two negatives always carries out. In 8 bits, 1111 1111 + 1111 1111 = 1 1111 1110, which is −2 — correct, with a carry. Any rule that equates carry with overflow fails on this line.
  • Overflow with no carry. 0111 + 0001 = 1000: +7 + 1 = −8, no carry out, overflow set.
  • A full-range negation. With n bits there are 2n patterns and 2n values in two's complement, so nothing is wasted — but the range is not symmetric, and a question that asks for "the number of positive and negative values" wants 2n−1 − 1 and 2n−1.

The floating-point boundaries

  • E = 0 with F = 0 is zero, and the sign bit still applies: 0x80000000 is −0. It compares equal to +0, and 1/−0 is −∞.
  • E = 0 with F ≠ 0 is denormal: significand 0.F at exponent −126. The smallest is 0x00000001 = 2−149.
  • E = 255 with F = 0 is ±∞. E = 255 with F ≠ 0 is NaN, and there are 223 − 1 distinct NaN patterns — the standard calls the field a payload, and none of them equals itself.
  • The largest finite single is 0x7F7FFFFF = (2 − 2−23) × 2127 ≈ 3.4028 × 1038. One bit more in the fraction would make it 2128, which is not a finite value, so the significand stops short of 2.
  • The smallest normal is 0x00800000 = 2−126, not 2−127, because the exponent field cannot be 0 for a normal number.
  • The gap is not constant. At 1 it is 2−23; at 224 it is 2; just below 2−126 the denormals are evenly spaced at 2−149, which means fewer and fewer significant bits as you approach zero.

Cases where the question's own wording decides

  • "How many distinct values" versus "how many bit patterns". For sign-magnitude and ones' complement the two differ by one, because of the second zero. For two's complement they are equal.
  • "Smallest" usually means most negative, not smallest magnitude. The 2013 question's smallest 8-bit value is −128; its smallest magnitude is 0.
  • "The decimal value" of a bit string, with no format named, is the unsigned reading. If the question intends two's complement it will say so, or give a width and a sign convention.
  • "The value rounded to two decimal places" on a float decode means the exact value of the pattern, then rounded. It never means the nearest float.
  • "A 16-bit signed integer" in a GATE question means two's complement unless another representation is named. Sign-magnitude and ones' complement are named explicitly when they are meant.

The two exception tables worth memorising

Integers: one self-negating pattern (−2n−1), one wasted pattern in the two older encodings, one carry that is not an overflow, one overflow that is not a carry, and one subtraction that cannot be done by negation. Floats: two reserved exponent values (0 and 255) covering five kinds of value (zero, denormal, normal, infinity, NaN), two zeros that compare equal, and one value that never equals itself.

Eight wrong beliefs, each with the smallest counterexample

A misconception is only defeated by a case it cannot explain. Each card gives the belief, the counterexample in bits, and the correct statement. The last card is the pair of concepts most often confused with each other in this topic.

1. "The carry flag means overflow"

Counterexample. In 4 bits, 1011 + 1110 = 1 1001. Carry out 1, no overflow: −5 + (−2) = −7, which is representable.

Correct. The carry out is the fifth bit of an unsigned sum. Overflow is Cout ⊕ Cinto sign, equivalently: same-sign operands, opposite-sign result. Neither flag implies the other in either direction.

2. "Overflow always sets the carry"

Counterexample. 0111 + 0001 = 1000 in 4 bits: +7 + 1 = −8. No carry out at all, overflow set.

Correct. The carry out of the top column is 0 here because the two operands' top bits are 0 and 0. The carry into the sign column is 1, which is exactly what the exclusive-or test reports.

3. "Negating a number always works"

Counterexample. In 8 bits, −128 is 1000 0000. Invert: 0111 1111. Add one: 1000 0000. The same pattern.

Correct. Negation is an involution on the 2n patterns with exactly one fixed point, and that fixed point is the most negative value. Its absolute value is not representable, which is why signed overflow is undefined behaviour in C.

4. "Widening a two's complement number pads with zeros"

Counterexample. −28 as 0000 0000 1110 0100 is +228 in 16 bits. The value changed.

Correct. Copy the sign bit into every new position: 1111 1111 1110 0100. Zero padding is correct only for unsigned values, where there is no sign to copy.

5. "E = 129 means the exponent is 129"

Counterexample. The 2021 decode gives 1.9375 × 2129 if you skip the bias — about 1.3 × 1039, which is not even representable.

Correct. The stored field carries the bias. e = E − 127 in single, E − 1023 in double, and e = −126 when E = 0.

6. "The fraction field includes the leading one"

Counterexample. Writing 1.11001 into the 23 fraction bits of 0xC1640000 would put a 1 in the top fraction position, giving 0xC1A40000 — a different, larger number.

Correct. For a normal number only the bits after the point are stored; the leading 1 is implied. For a denormal the implied bit is 0, which is a different rule, not an exception to this one.

7. "0xFFFFFFFF is the largest float"

Counterexample. Its exponent field is 255 and its fraction is non-zero, so it is a NaN. Adding it to anything gives NaN.

Correct. The largest finite single is 0x7F7FFFFF. Before naming any value, cut the exponent field and check whether it is 0 or 255.

8. "Floating point can represent one tenth"

Counterexample. The nearest single to 0.1 is 13421773/227 = 0.10000000149…, and the nearest double is 3602879701896397/255. Neither is one tenth.

Correct. A binary fraction is a multiple of 2−f, and 1/10 has a factor of 5 in its denominator, so no f makes it exact. Values whose denominators are powers of two — 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 14.25 — are exact.

Closely confused pair: range against precision

Range is how far from zero the format reaches; precision is how finely it can divide an interval once it gets there. They are set by different fields and they trade against each other.

single: range ±3.4 × 1038, precision 24 significant bits (gap 2−23 at 1)8 exponent bits set the range, 23 stored fraction bits plus the hidden one set the precision.
double: range ±1.8 × 10308, precision 53 significant bits (gap 2−52 at 1)Adding 3 exponent bits multiplies the range by about 2768; adding 29 fraction bits multiplies the precision by 229.

The distinction that earns marks. A question that says "the value cannot be represented" is usually a range question, and the answer is an infinity or a denormal. A question that says "the answer is slightly wrong" is a precision question, and the answer involves an ulp, a rounding, or an absorbed addend. Single precision covers about 38 decades; double covers about 308. Neither covers 1/10 exactly, because that is a precision fact and no range helps it.

Closely confused pair: carry against overflow, in one table

Both come out of the same adder, and a question that says "the result is wrong" is asking about one of them. The table separates them by what they are evidence for.

Carry flag
The (n+1)-th bit of an unsigned sum. Means the true sum exceeded 2n − 1.
Overflow flag
A sign violation in a signed sum. Means the true sum left [−2n−1, 2n−1 − 1].
Both set
Adding two large positives as unsigned and as signed. 1000 0000 + 1000 0000: carry out 1, and as signed −128 + −128 overflows.
Carry only
Two negatives that fit: 1111 1111 + 1111 1111 = −2.
Overflow only
0111 + 0001 = 1000: +7 + 1.
Neither
Almost everything else, including every addition of operands with opposite signs.

How this topic is tested, and what each pattern demands

Six patterns cover almost every question this topic has produced. For each one: what the question looks like, the procedure that answers it, and the step where marks are usually lost. The frequency statements are observations about the papers listed in the Sources tab; they are not a prediction for 2027.

Pattern 1 — convert a value to bits, or bits to a value

Looks like: "In 16-bit two's complement representation, the decimal number −28 is …" (2019 Q4) or "The 16-bit two's complement representation of an integer is 1111 1111 1111 0101; its decimal representation is …" (2016 Set 1 Q7).

Procedure. Width first, then sign, then complement. To go from bits to a value: if the top bit is 0 the value is the unsigned reading; if it is 1, complement the whole word, add one, and prefix a minus.

Where marks are lost. Using eight bits when the question said sixteen. The 2019 wrong option 0000 0000 1110 0100 is exactly the eight-bit answer padded with zeros.

Pattern 2 — range, count, or smallest and largest

Looks like: "The smallest integer that can be represented by an 8-bit number in 2's complement form is …" (2013 Q4); "Let X be the number of distinct 16-bit integers in 2's complement and Y in sign-magnitude; X − Y is …" (2016 Set 2 Q9); the fixed-point range question (2017 Set 1 Q7).

Procedure. Write the range formula for the named representation and substitute. Two's complement: −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1. Sign-magnitude and ones': ±(2n−1 − 1). Fixed point: 0 to 2i − 2−f.

Where marks are lost. Reading "smallest" as "smallest magnitude", and writing 2i as the fixed-point maximum.

Pattern 3 — overflow and underflow in an operation

Looks like: four bit patterns given, "which of the following operations results in an arithmetic overflow?" — an MSQ in 2022, 2024, 2025 and 2026, and an MCQ earlier.

Procedure. Convert each operand to its value, compute the true result in ordinary integers, and compare with the range. This is faster and safer than the sign rule, and it works for sign-magnitude as well, where the sign rule does not apply.

Where marks are lost. Answering with the carry flag, and forgetting that a subtraction can overflow too.

Pattern 4 — decode an IEEE 754 pattern

Looks like: three named fields (2021 Set 1 Q24), a 32-bit binary string (2017 Set 2 Q12), or a hex constant (2023 Q45, 2025 Set 2 Q49, 2026 Set 2 Q34).

Procedure. Expand to 32 bits, cut 1/8/23, subtract the bias, write the significand as 1 + a sum of halves, multiply.

Where marks are lost. The bias, and reading the fraction as a decimal fraction. Both are one-line checks at the end.

Pattern 5 — encode a value, or name a boundary value

Looks like: "A float variable is assigned −14.25; its representation in hexadecimal is …" (2014 Set 2 Q45); "the smallest normalized positive number has exponent field … and fraction …" (2021 Set 2 Q4).

Procedure. For an encode: binary, normalise, exponent field = e + bias, fraction = the bits after the point, sign. For a boundary: set E to the smallest or largest legal value and F to all zeros or all ones.

Where marks are lost. Forgetting that the smallest normal has E = 1, not E = 0; and writing the hidden 1 into the fraction field.

Pattern 6 — arithmetic on two floats, and precision loss

Looks like: three registers holding hex constants, "which of the following is true about X + Y = Z?" (2022 Q41, 2025 Set 2 Q49, 2026 Set 1 Q36); or a product of two floats (2023 Q45).

Procedure. You rarely need the exact value. Compare exponents first: a sum of two numbers whose exponents differ by more than 24 cannot change the larger one. For a product, the exponent field adds and the biases must be subtracted once. Then check the sign and the special cases.

Where marks are lost. Adding the two exponent fields and forgetting to subtract 127 once. And assuming that a small addend survives.

The four traps, in the order they cost marks

One: the bias. E is a field, not an exponent. Two: the hidden bit. 1.F for normal, 0.F when E = 0. Three: carry is not overflow — the sign decides overflow, not the fifth bit. Four: the exponent field decides specialness, so check for 0 and 255 before you compute anything. Each trap is a check that takes a second and can be done after the answer is written, which is why it is worth doing even in the last minute.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2013, Q4 1 mark

The smallest integer that can be represented by an 8-bit number in 2's complement form is

  1. −256
  2. −128
  3. −127
  4. 0

Answer: (B) −128. The range is −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1, so with n = 8 it is −128 to 127. The pattern 1000 0000 is −128: the top bit carries −128 and every other bit is 0.

Why the others fail. −256 needs nine bits (−28). −127 is representable but is not the smallest. 0 is not negative at all.

Trap. "Smallest" means most negative. A question that wants the smallest magnitude says so, or says "smallest non-negative".

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2016 Set 2, Q9 1 mark

Let X be the number of distinct 16-bit integers in 2's complement representation. Let Y be the number of distinct 16-bit integers in sign-magnitude representation. Then X − Y is ______.

Answer: 1. Two's complement uses every one of the 216 patterns for a different value, so X = 65536. Sign-magnitude spends two patterns on zero (0000…0 and 1000…0), so Y = 65535.

Why it cannot depend on the width. The waste is exactly one pattern at every width, because there is exactly one duplicate zero. So X − Y = 1 for n = 4, 16 or 64.

Trap. Counting patterns rather than values. Both representations have 216 patterns; they differ in how many distinct values those patterns name.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, Q7 1 mark

The n-bit fixed-point representation of an unsigned real number X uses f bits for the fraction part. Let i = n − f. The range of decimal values for X in this representation is

  1. 2−f to 2i
  2. 2−f to 2i − 2−f
  3. 0 to 2i − 2−f
  4. 0 to 2i

Answer: (C) 0 to 2i − 2−f. The smallest pattern is all zeros, which is 0. The largest is all ones, whose value is the sum of the weights 2i−1 + … + 2−f = 2i − 2−f.

Why the others fail. (A) and (B) exclude zero, which the format certainly holds. (D) is one step too large: 2i needs the fraction bits to be zero and the integer part to be 2i, which the i integer bits cannot hold.

Check on numbers. n = 8, f = 3: 0 to 32 − 0.125 = 31.875, which is exactly what the animation prints.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2019, Q8 1 mark

Consider Z = X − Y, where X, Y and Z are all in sign-magnitude form. X and Y are each represented in n bits. To avoid overflow, the representation of Z would require a minimum of

  1. n bits
  2. n − 1 bits
  3. n + 1 bits
  4. n + 2 bits

Answer: (C) n + 1 bits. An n-bit sign-magnitude number holds magnitudes up to 2n−1 − 1. The worst case is (2n−1 − 1) − (−(2n−1 − 1)) = 2n − 2, a magnitude needing n bits, plus one for the sign.

Why the others fail. n bits hold magnitudes only up to 2n−1 − 1, and 2n − 2 is larger than that for every n ≥ 2. n + 2 works but is not minimal. n − 1 is smaller than the inputs.

Cross-check. In two's complement the same difference also needs n + 1 bits, but for a different reason: the range is asymmetric, and (−2n−1) − (2n−1 − 1) = −2n + 1 needs n + 1. Same answer, different derivation — say which one you are using.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2021 Set 2, Q4 1 mark

The format of the single-precision floating-point representation of a real number as per the IEEE 754 standard is: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 mantissa bits. Which one of the following choices is correct with respect to the smallest normalized positive number represented using the standard?

  1. exponent = 00000000 and mantissa = 000…0
  2. exponent = 00000000 and mantissa = 000…01
  3. exponent = 00000001 and mantissa = 000…0
  4. exponent = 00000001 and mantissa = 000…01

Answer: (C). A normalized number needs an exponent field in 1…254, so the smallest exponent field is 1, giving e = 1 − 127 = −126. The smallest significand is 1.000…0, so the mantissa bits are all zero. The value is 2−126 ≈ 1.18 × 10−38.

Why the others fail. (A) is zero. (B) is the smallest denormal, 2−149 — the smallest positive number, but not a normalized one. (D) is 2−126 × (1 + 2−23), slightly larger than needed.

Trap. "Smallest positive" and "smallest normalized positive" have different answers. Read the word normalized; it is doing all the work.

NAT Original practice not an official question

The 32-bit IEEE 754 single-precision representation of a number is 0xC2710000. The number in decimal representation is ______ (two decimal places).

Answer: −60.25. Expand: 1100 0010 0111 0001 then zeros. S = 1. E = 1000 0100 = 132, so e = 132 − 127 = 5. F = 1110 0010…, so the significand is 1.1110001₂ = 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.0078125 = 1.8828125. Then 1.8828125 × 32 = 60.25, negative.

Why this is original. The pattern appears in a 2026 paper, but the answer here was computed from the field definitions on this page rather than taken from any key, so it is labelled original. The arithmetic is checked in the Animation tab.

MSQ Original practice not an official question

In 8-bit two's complement, which of the following additions produce an arithmetic overflow?

  1. 0111 1111 + 0000 0001
  2. 1111 1111 + 1111 1111
  3. 1000 0000 + 1111 1111
  4. 0100 0000 + 0100 0000

Answer: (A), (C) and (D). Convert each operand and compare the true sum with the range [−128, 127]. (A) 127 + 1 = 128, out of range. (C) −128 + −1 = −129, out of range. (D) 64 + 64 = 128, out of range. (B) −1 + −1 = −2, comfortably inside, so (B) is the only one that does not overflow.

Why (B) is the trap. 1111 1111 + 1111 1111 carries out of the top column, and a carry is exactly what people mistake for overflow. Two negatives whose sum is still representable is the cleanest counterexample there is, and it is the reason the sign rule exists.

Method. Do not reason from the carry bit. (B) carries and does not overflow; (A), (C) and (D) do not carry out of the top column in every case, and all three overflow.

NAT Original practice not an official question

The decimal value 3.5 is stored in IEEE 754 single precision. Give its 32-bit pattern in hexadecimal, as eight hex digits without the 0x prefix.

Answer: 40600000. 3.5 = 11.1₂ = 1.11₂ × 21. Exponent field 1 + 127 = 128 = 1000 0000. Fraction 110 followed by twenty zeros. Sign 0.

Bits: 0 10000000 11000000000000000000000 → 0100 0000 0110 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 = 0x40600000.

Check by decoding. E = 128 → e = 1; significand 1.75; 1.75 × 2 = 3.5. A decode that returns the value you started from is the only proof an encode needs.

MSQ Original practice not an official question

Which of the following statements about IEEE 754 single precision are TRUE?

  1. Every pattern with exponent field 0 is a denormal number.
  2. The largest finite value has fraction bits all ones and exponent field 254.
  3. Two different bit patterns can represent the value zero.
  4. A pattern with exponent field 255 and a non-zero fraction equals itself.

Answer: (B) and (C). (B) is 0x7F7FFFFF = (2 − 2−23) × 2127, the largest finite single. (C) is true: 0x00000000 and 0x80000000 are both zero.

Why the others fail. (A) fails at exactly one pattern: exponent field 0 with fraction 0 is zero, not a denormal. (D) is a NaN, and a NaN is not equal to itself — that is the standard's own test for one.

The distinction. "Exponent field 0" and "denormal" are not synonyms; nor are "exponent field 255" and "infinity". Each reserved field covers two cases separated by the fraction.

NAT Original practice not an official question

In single precision, how many distinct bit patterns represent a NaN?

Answer: 16777215, that is 223 − 1, doubled for the sign if you count the sign bit separately: 2 × (223 − 1). A NaN needs exponent field 255 and a non-zero fraction. There are 223 fraction patterns and exactly one of them is all zeros, which gives infinity instead.

Why the count matters. It shows that "NaN" is a family, not a value: the fraction field is a payload, and two NaNs with different payloads are both NaN and neither equals anything. The zero payload with exponent 255 is infinity, so it is excluded.

NAT Original practice not an official question

In 12-bit two's complement, what is the decimal value of the pattern 1111 0110 1010?

Answer: −150. The top bit is 1, so the value is the unsigned reading minus 212. The unsigned reading is 0xF6A = 3946, and 3946 − 4096 = −150.

Second method, as a check. Complement and add one: 0000 1001 0101 + 1 = 0000 1001 0110 = 150, and the sign makes it −150. Two methods that agree is the fastest confidence available in an exam.

MCQ Original practice not an official question

Two single-precision values are stored as 0x4B800000 and 0x4B800001. Which statement is true?

  1. They are the same value, because the patterns differ by one.
  2. They differ by 1.0, and no value lies between them.
  3. They differ by 2.0, and no value lies between them.
  4. The first is NaN, because its exponent field is large.

Answer: (C). 0x4B800000 has E = 150, so e = 24 and the value is 224 = 16777216. The next pattern adds one to the fraction, which adds one ulp, and the ulp at exponent 24 is 224−23 = 2. So the second value is 16777218.

Why the others fail. (A) and (B) both assume the gap is 1 at every magnitude; it is 1 only while the exponent is 23 or less. (D) is false: an exponent field of 150 is ordinary, and only 255 is special.

The distinction. One bit of pattern change is not one unit of value. The value of a step in the last place is 2e−23, so it doubles at every power of two — which is the whole difference between fixed point and floating point.

Revision sheet

Everything below is computed, not quoted: the ranges follow from the weight formulas, the constants follow from the field definitions, and the checks in the Animation tab reproduce them. Print this page before the exam; it is one screen.

Integer representations at width n

Unsigned
0 to 2n − 1; 2n values.
Sign-magnitude
−(2n−1 − 1) to 2n−1 − 1; 2n − 1 values; two zeros.
Ones' complement
Same range and count as sign-magnitude; negation is a plain invert.
Two's complement
−2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1; 2n values; one zero.
Negation
Invert and add one, at the same width. −2n−1 is its own negation.
Widening
Copy the sign bit leftwards. Zero-padding is only for unsigned values.

Fixed point at width n, f fraction bits

Value
Σ bk 2k for k from −f to i−1, where i = n − f.
Range
0 to 2i − 2−f.
Step
2−f; the resolution of the format.
Trade-off
At fixed n, more fraction bits mean finer steps and a smaller maximum.

Flags

Carry
The (n+1)-th bit of an unsigned sum. Meaningful only for unsigned operands.
Overflow
Cout ⊕ Cinto sign, equivalently same-sign operands with an opposite-sign result.
Never overflow
Adding operands of opposite signs.
Always carries
Adding two negatives — and this usually does not overflow.

IEEE 754 at a glance

Single
1 / 8 / 23, bias 127, 24-bit significand.
Double
1 / 11 / 52, bias 1023, 53-bit significand.
Normal
(−1)S · 1.F · 2E−bias, with 1 ≤ E ≤ 254 (2046 in double).
Denormal
(−1)S · 0.F · 2−126 when E = 0 and F ≠ 0.
Zero
E = 0, F = 0; two patterns, comparing equal.
Infinity / NaN
E = 255: F = 0 is ±∞, F ≠ 0 is NaN, and NaN ≠ NaN.

The constants worth knowing

Largest finite single
(2 − 2−23) × 2127 ≈ 3.4 × 1038 = 0x7F7FFFFF
Smallest normal single
2−126 ≈ 1.18 × 10−38 = 0x00800000
Smallest denormal single
2−149 ≈ 1.4 × 10−45 = 0x00000001
Gap at 1 (single)
2−23 ≈ 1.19 × 10−7
Largest finite double
(2 − 2−52) × 21023 ≈ 1.8 × 10308
Exact integers (single)
All integers up to 224; beyond that only even ones, then only multiples of 4, and so on.

Decision procedure

  1. Name the representation and the width. Width fixes the bias, the number of fraction bits and the sign position.
  2. Find the sign, or the sign field. For integers the top bit; for floats the first bit; for fixed point there is none, which is why the format is unsigned unless stated.
  3. Cut the fields, or write the weights. 1 / 8 / 23 for a single; 2k weights for a fixed point.
  4. Apply the bias or the complement. e = E − 127; or invert and add one.
  5. Compute, then check. Decode your own encode. Compare the exponent field with 0 and 255. Compare the sum with the range.

Five quick revision questions

(1) What is 0x3F800000? (2) How many bits does A − B need in two's complement if A and B are n-bit? (3) Is 2−127 a normal single? (4) What is 1111 0000 in 8-bit two's complement? (5) What is the gap between 1.0 and the next single? Answers: 1.0; n + 1; no, it is denormal; −16; 2−23.
