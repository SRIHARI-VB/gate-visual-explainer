Topic and objective

TOPIC: The arithmetic of a two-level cache — average memory access time as a weighted average of three outcomes, its factored form, the two conventions for a level’s stated time, local versus global miss rate, the equation that solving backwards produces, and the conversion of a per-access cost into CPI stall cycles.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a two-level hierarchy, write the three mutually exclusive outcomes of one access with their probabilities and path costs, compute the average both as a weighted sum and as T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3, decide from the wording whether a printed time is a level’s own time or already includes the levels above, tell a local from a global miss rate by its denominator, solve for a miss rate when the average is given, and convert the result into cycles per instruction.

I read the request as the arithmetic of the hierarchy, not a second lesson on mapping, replacement and the three Cs: memory hierarchy and cache owns offset/index/tag, the three mappings, LRU walks and the 3 C’s, and it quotes the single-level average once. This page owns what that lesson only states: the multi-level average, both time conventions, both miss-rate denominators, and the move to CPI. Every number quoted here is computed by the page’s own model in exact rational arithmetic, and 36 checks re-verify it.

Prerequisites

  • Hit time, miss rate, miss penalty and what a block is — memory hierarchy and cache.
  • CPI, CPU time and the idea that a speedup is a ratio of CPIs — CPU performance.
  • Multiplying probabilities for two events in sequence, and weighted averages.
  • The quadratic formula, and why a probability cannot be negative.
  • Clock cycles as the unit of cost; converting between cycles and nanoseconds needs the clock rate.

Five ideas this page establishes

  1. One access has exactly three outcomes; their probabilities sum to one, and the average is their weighted sum.
  2. Regrouping that sum gives T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3 — an identity, not a mnemonic. Using m2 alone gives 26.8 cycles where the truth is 4.3.
  3. A printed time may be a level’s own time or already inclusive; the same GATE 2025 Set 1 data gives 11.85 ns or 12.5 ns depending on that reading.
  4. Local miss rate divides by the accesses that reached the level, global by all accesses; they differ by exactly the factor m1.
  5. A per-access cost becomes a per-instruction cost through the accesses-per-instruction figure, and the extra cost is AMAT minus the hit time, not AMAT.

The one sentence worth remembering

Every two-level cache question is one partition — hit, miss-then-hit, miss twice — and every wrong option is that partition with one probability or one time misread.

Approximate study time

About 55–70 minutes: 15 for the animation, 15 for the two conventions and the two denominators, 20 for the practice set, 10 for revision. The figure is approximate, not measured.

Intuition first

You ask a librarian for a book. Most times the book is on the desk in front of her, and the answer takes a second. Sometimes she has to walk to the shelf in the room behind, which takes longer, and occasionally she has to phone the warehouse across town. Your average waiting time is not the average of one, ten and a hundred seconds: it depends on how often each trip happens. A book on the desk is the common case, so it dominates the average.

That is the entire content of average memory access time. The three trips cannot overlap and one of them always happens, so their frequencies add to one. Once you write the three frequencies down, the average is a weighted sum, and everything else on this page is a way of reading the question that tells you which numbers go into it.

Formal definition: the three outcomes

Let T1 be the L1 access time, T2 the L2 access time, T3 the main-memory access time, m1 the L1 miss rate and m2 the L2 local miss rate. One memory access ends in exactly one of three ways:

  • L1 hit — probability 1 − m1, cost T1.
  • L1 miss, L2 hit — probability m1(1 − m2), cost T1 + T2.
  • Both miss — probability m1 m2, cost T1 + T2 + T3.

The three probabilities add to 1: (1 − m1) + m1(1 − m2) + m1 m2 = 1. The second probability is a product because reaching L2 requires the first miss, and then L2 must also miss; that is the multiplication rule for two events in sequence.

The average, and its factored form

Average memory access time is the weighted sum of the three costs:

AMAT = (1−m1)T1 + m1(1−m2)(T1+T2) + m1m2(T1+T2+T3)

Every term contains T1, and the probabilities sum to 1, so T1 survives once. T2 is paid by the two outcomes that reach L2, whose probabilities sum to m1. T3 is paid only by the third outcome. Regrouping:

AMAT = T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3

Derivation This is algebra on the same three lines, not a new formula, and the page’s model checks the two forms against each other on 500 random configurations.

The two time conventions

Own time: T2 is the cost of looking in L2 and nothing else, so the path cost of reaching main memory is T1 + T2 + T3. Add along the path. GATE CSE 2016 and 2017 Set 2 print times of this kind.

Inclusive time: the printed figure for L2 already contains the L1 lookup and the transfer back, and the printed figure for main memory already contains both. Then the path costs are the printed numbers themselves, or equivalently the increments s1, s2 − s1, s3 − s2. GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 prints times of this kind.

Both readings are exact; the difference is what the number means. Adding an inclusive time to the level above it charges that level twice, which on the 2025 numbers gives 12.5 ns against a key of 11.83–11.87.

Local and global miss rate

Local miss rate of a level = misses in that level divided by the accesses that reached it. Global miss rate = misses in that level divided by all accesses to the cache system. They are one factor apart:

global(L2) = m1 × local(L2)

A third number appears in papers too: misses per instruction, which is a count, not a rate. On GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 the three are 0.05 (local), 0.005 (global) and 0.007 (per instruction), and only one of them answers the question asked.

Hierarchical and simultaneous access

Hierarchical: the levels are consulted in turn, so a main-memory access pays T1 + T2 + T3 and an L1 hit pays only T1. This is the reading the formula above already assumes.

Simultaneous: every level starts at once and the slowest started wins, so an L1 hit pays only T1 while the miss outcomes pay the whole time of the level that finally answers: h1 T1 + (1−h1)T2 + (1−h1)(1−h2)T3.

On the GATE CSE 2016 numbers these give 13.0 ns and 12.8 ns, and both are printed as options. The convention is a property of the hardware the question describes, so it has to be read, not assumed.

From a per-access cost to CPI

A performance question wants cycles per instruction. The bridge is the number of memory accesses per instruction, call it a:

stalls per instruction = a × (AMAT − T1)

CPI = base CPI + stalls per instruction

The subtraction is the assumption: the base CPI already pays one cycle for the access, so only the excess is a stall. Write that assumption next to the formula every time, because charging AMAT in full counts the L1 lookup twice.

Symbols, in words

T1, T2, T3 are the access times of L1, L2 and main memory. m1 is the fraction of accesses that miss in L1. m2 is the fraction of the accesses that reached L2 that miss there — the local rate. m1 m2 is the fraction of all accesses that reach main memory. h1, h2 are hit rates, so h1 = 1 − m1. a is memory accesses per instruction. CPI is clock cycles per instruction. AMAT is clock cycles (or nanoseconds) per memory access.

Worked example 1 — the configuration used throughout, by both routes

T1 = 1, m1 = 0.10, T2 = 8, m2 = 0.25 (local), T3 = 100 cycles

  1. The three outcomes. L1 hit: probability 0.90, cost 1. L1 miss then L2 hit: 0.10 × 0.75 = 0.075, cost 9. Both miss: 0.10 × 0.25 = 0.025, cost 109. — 0.90 + 0.075 + 0.025 = 1, so nothing is missing.
  2. The weighted sum. 0.90(1) + 0.075(9) + 0.025(109) = 0.9 + 0.675 + 2.725 = 4.3 cycles. In exact fractions the terms are 9/10, 27/40 and 109/40, summing to 43/10.
  3. The factored form. 1 + 0.10(8) + 0.10(0.25)(100) = 1 + 0.8 + 2.5 = 4.3 cycles. Same number, fewer lines.
  4. Where the time goes. The main-memory term is 2.725 of the 4.3 cycles, about 63 per cent, although it happens on only 2.5 per cent of accesses.
  5. The wrong form. 1 + 0.10(8) + 0.25(100) = 26.8 cycles. It treats every access as if L1 had already failed.

Worked example 2 — GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, inclusive times

L1 hit rate 0.95 at 10 ns; L2 hit rate 0.85 at 20 ns, stated to include the L1 miss penalty; main memory 200 ns, stated to include both. Average access time in ns?

Probabilities: 0.95, 0.05 × 0.85 = 0.0425, 0.05 × 0.15 = 0.0075. Path costs are the printed times, 10, 20, 200.

0.95(10) + 0.0425(20) + 0.0075(200) = 9.5 + 0.85 + 1.5 = 11.85 ns

The increment route agrees: 10 + 0.05(10) + 0.0075(180) = 11.85.

The published key accepts 11.83 to 11.87. Adding the inclusive times again gives 12.5 ns, which the key rejects.

Worked example 3 — GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, which denominator

1.4 memory accesses per instruction; L1 miss rate 0.1; L2 experiences on average 7 misses per 1,000 instructions. Local miss rate of L2?

1,000 instr → 1,400 accesses → 140 L1 misses → 7 L2 misses

local = 7/140 = 0.05   global = 7/1400 = 0.005

The answer is 0.05. Dividing 7 by 1,000 gives 0.007, a miss count per instruction, which is neither of the two rates.

Worked example 4 — GATE CSE 2017 Set 2, solving backwards

L1 access 1 cycle, L2 access 8 cycles, main-memory miss penalty 18 cycles, L1 miss rate twice the L2 miss rate, average access time 2 cycles. Find both miss rates.

1 + 8x + 18x(x/2) = 2 ⇒ 9x² + 8x − 1 = 0

x = (−8 ± 10)/18 ⇒ x = 1/9 or x = −1

Reject −1 because a probability cannot be negative. Check: 1 + 8/9 + 1/9 = 2.0 exactly.

So m1 = 0.111 and m2 = 0.056. The global miss rate of L2 is 1/162, about 0.0062.

Worked example 5 — the same configuration as CPI, and two upgrades priced

Base CPI 1.0 and 1.4 memory accesses per instruction. The average access costs 4.3 cycles and a hit costs 1, so each access wastes 3.3 cycles. Stalls per instruction = 1.4 × 3.3 = 4.62, so CPI = 1.0 + 4.62 = 5.62. The split by level adds back: 1.4 × 0.10 × 8 = 1.12 for L2 lookups, and 1.4 × 0.025 × 100 = 3.5 for main memory.

Halving m1 to 0.05 gives 1 + 0.4 + 1.25 = 2.65 cycles, stalls 2.31, CPI 3.31, speedup 5.62/3.31 = 1.70. Halving m2 to 0.125 gives 1 + 0.8 + 1.25 = 3.05 cycles, stalls 2.87, CPI 3.87, speedup 1.45.

The inner level wins because m1 multiplies both lower terms while m2 multiplies only the last one. Neither change halves the time: the base CPI of 1.0 is untouched, so halving the L1 miss rate cuts execution time by 41 per cent, not 50.

When a limit collapses the formula

  • m1 = 0. L1 never misses, so AMAT = T1 and the lower levels are irrelevant. This is the only case in which the m2-only form agrees with the truth, which is why a wrong formula can look right on a degenerate example.
  • m2 = 0. L2 never misses, so the third term vanishes and AMAT = T1 + m1 T2. Main memory is never reached.
  • m2 = 1. Every L2 access misses, so the third term becomes m1 T3: the hierarchy is effectively one level deep.
  • One level only. Delete the L2 row and AMAT = T1 + m1 T3, the single-level formula, with T3 playing the part of the miss penalty.
  • T1 = 0. Physically impossible for a real cache, but some questions quote hit times of zero to focus on the misses; then AMAT = m1(T2 + m2 T3) and the “extra cost” used for CPI is AMAT itself.

Three levels, and beyond

The pattern extends without new ideas: the average is the sum over outcomes, and each outcome’s probability is a product of the miss rates above it.

AMAT = T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3 + m1 m2 m3 T4

Each new level adds one term and multiplies the previous probability by one more miss rate. A four-level machine is the same three lines of algebra with a longer sum, and a question that gives three levels is testing exactly this, not a new formula.

Cases that break the simple reading

  • Unified versus split caches. If instruction and data fetches share one L1, the accesses-per-instruction figure is 1 plus the fraction of instructions that load or store, not 1.0. A question that gives separate I and D caches expects you to count fetches and loads separately.
  • Write-back traffic. A dirty block evicted from L2 costs an extra main-memory write. That traffic is not in the read-path AMAT and is usually given as a separate rate; do not fold it into m2 unless the question says so.
  • Inclusive versus exclusive hierarchies. In an inclusive hierarchy a block in L1 is always in L2; in an exclusive one it is not, so a demotion between levels costs a transfer. Neither changes the AMAT algebra, but both change what m1 and m2 mean physically.
  • Cycles versus nanoseconds. Mixing the two is the most common unit error. Convert with the clock rate once, at the end, and never inside the weighted sum.
  • Average over what. AMAT is per memory access. If a question asks for time per instruction, the accesses-per-instruction factor belongs in the line.

A boundary worth computing

The inclusive-time trap has a closed form: the gap between the naive reading and the correct one is m1(s1 + m2 s2), which the model verifies on all 900 swept configurations. On the GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 numbers that is exactly 0.65 ns. With a 10 ns L1, a 20 ns inclusive L2 time and both miss rates at 30 per cent it reaches 4.8 ns.

The gap grows with m1, because m1 is the probability attached to the term that gets counted twice. That is why the trap is worth more marks on a question with a high miss rate than on one with a low one, and why a small gap still fails a two-decimal numerical answer.

Misconception 1 — “the L2 term is m2 times the main-memory time”

This is the single most common error on the topic, and it produces an answer several times too large.

Counterexample. With T1 = 1, m1 = 0.10, T2 = 8, m2 = 0.25, T3 = 100 the m2-only form gives 26.8 cycles; the truth is 4.3. The model sweeps 400 combinations of the two rates and the two forms agree only when m1 = 1 — that is, when L1 never hits at all.

Why it feels right. m2 is called “the L2 miss rate”, and it is tempting to attach it to the L2 miss penalty. But the probability of reaching main memory is the probability of missing twice, m1 m2, and the local rate alone describes only the accesses that already failed in L1.

Misconception 2 — “add the printed times along the path”

True only when the printed times are own times.

Counterexample. GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 prints 20 ns for L2 including the L1 miss penalty. Adding 10 + 0.05(20) + 0.0075(200) gives 12.5 ns; the key is 11.83–11.87 and the correct figure is 11.85. The error is 0.65 ns, which is enough to fail a two-decimal range.

Repair. Write the three path costs before writing any formula. If a printed number already contains the level above, the path cost is that number.

Misconception 3 — “a miss rate is a miss rate”

Local and global rates differ by the factor m1, and questions use both words.

Counterexample. GATE CSE 2017 Set 1: the local rate of L2 is 0.05 and the global rate is 0.005, ten times apart, because m1 = 0.1. Answering with the global figure is a wrong option, not a rounding difference.

Repair. Read the denominator printed in the sentence. “Misses per 1000 instructions” is a count; “the miss rate of the L2 cache” almost always means the local rate, because the global one is not a property of L2 alone.

Misconception 4 — “AMAT is the cycles per instruction”

AMAT is per access; CPI is per instruction. The bridge is the accesses-per-instruction figure.

Counterexample. On this page AMAT is 4.3 cycles per access and the average instruction carries 1.4 accesses, so 6.02 cycles per instruction pass through memory while the stall cost is 4.62 cycles. Quoting 4.3 as a CPI understates the cost by a third.

Repair. Write the unit at the end of every line: cycles per access, cycles per instruction, nanoseconds per access.

Misconception 5 — “halving the miss rate halves the time”

A miss rate is one component of a sum, and the base CPI stays whatever you do to the cache.

Counterexample. Halving m1 from 0.10 to 0.05 takes the CPI from 5.62 to 3.31: a speedup of 1.70 and a time cut of 41 per cent, not 50. Even an infinite cache leaves the CPI at 1.0, so the ceiling is 5.62.

Repair. Compute both CPIs and divide. The ratio of CPIs is the speedup, and it is always smaller than the ratio of the miss rates.

The closely confused pair, side by side

Hierarchical and simultaneous access both describe a two-level cache and both produce a single average. They differ in what an L1 hit costs and what a miss costs:

  • Hierarchical: an L1 hit pays T1, a main-memory access pays T1 + T2 + T3. Formula: T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3. On GATE CSE 2016 data: 13.0 ns.
  • Simultaneous: an L1 hit pays T1, an L2 hit pays T2 only, a main-memory access pays T3 only. Formula: h1 T1 + (1−h1)T2 + (1−h1)(1−h2)T3. On the same data: 12.8 ns.
  • The 0.2 ns gap is smaller than the gap to most wrong options, which is exactly why the convention has to be read from the question and not guessed.

Four verified previous-year shapes

  • GATE CSE 2016 — two hit rates and three times, average access time asked. Shape: hierarchical weighted sum. Answer 13.0 ns; the simultaneous reading gives the printed distractor 12.8.
  • GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 — 1.4 accesses per instruction, an L1 miss rate and an L2 miss count per 1,000 instructions. Shape: denominator arithmetic. Answer 0.05.
  • GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 — three times and an average, both miss rates tied by “twice”. Shape: quadratic from the identity. Answers 0.111 and 0.056.
  • GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 — inclusive times and two hit rates, a numerical answer in nanoseconds. Shape: outcome-weighted sum with the printed times as path costs. Answer 11.85, key 11.83–11.87.

The procedure that works on all four

  1. List the three outcomes and write each probability as a product of the miss rates above it.
  2. Check the three probabilities sum to 1 before computing anything.
  3. Decide whether each printed time is own or inclusive; write the three path costs in the question’s unit.
  4. Multiply and add, exactly, and only then round to the precision asked.
  5. Name the substitution behind each distractor you can see in the options.

What each distractor usually is

  • Far too large — the m2-only form, or a local rate used as a global one.
  • Slightly too large — inclusive times added along the path (12.5 against 11.85).
  • Slightly too small — the simultaneous reading where hierarchical was meant (12.8 against 13.0).
  • Off by the accesses-per-instruction factor — per-access used as per-instruction.
  • Exactly one of two symmetric values — the two miss rates swapped, which happens when m1 and m2 are confused.

MCQ Original practice

In the factored form of average memory access time for a two-level cache, which quantity multiplies T3, the main-memory access time?

  1. m2, the local L2 miss rate.
  2. m1, the L1 miss rate.
  3. The product m1 m2.
  4. The sum m1 + m2.

Answer: (C). Main memory is reached only when both levels miss, and the two misses happen in sequence, so the probability is m1 m2.

(A) is the classic error and gives 26.8 instead of 4.3 on the page’s configuration. (B) is the probability of reaching L2, which multiplies T2. (D) can exceed 1 and is not a probability.

NAT Original practice

A two-level cache has T1 = 2 cycles, m1 = 0.08, T2 = 12 cycles, m2 = 0.30 (local), and a main-memory access time of 150 cycles. All times are own times. Find the average memory access time in cycles.

Answer to two decimal places.

Answer: 6.56 cycles. 2 + 0.08(12) + 0.08(0.30)(150) = 2 + 0.96 + 3.6 = 6.56.

The three outcome probabilities are 0.92, 0.08 × 0.70 = 0.056 and 0.024, and 0.92(2) + 0.056(14) + 0.024(164) = 1.84 + 0.784 + 3.936 = 6.56, which agrees. Using m2 alone in the last term would give 47.

MCQ Original practice

A cache system has an L1 miss rate of 0.05 and an L2 local miss rate of 0.20. What is the fraction of all memory accesses that reach main memory?

  1. 0.20
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.01
  4. 0.15

Answer: (C). 0.05 × 0.20 = 0.01, the global miss rate of L2.

(A) is the local rate, which is what fraction of L2 accesses miss. (B) adds the rates, which is meaningless because they have different denominators. (D) subtracts them, the same error in the other direction.

NAT Original practice

L1 hit rate 0.90 at 1 ns. L2 hit rate 0.60 at 5 ns, stated to include the L1 miss penalty. Main memory 100 ns, stated to include both penalties. Find the average access time in nanoseconds.

Answer to one decimal place.

Answer: 5.2 ns. Probabilities 0.90, 0.10 × 0.60 = 0.06, 0.10 × 0.40 = 0.04. 0.90(1) + 0.06(5) + 0.04(100) = 0.9 + 0.3 + 4.0 = 5.2.

The increment route agrees: 1 + 0.10(4) + 0.04(95) = 1 + 0.4 + 3.8 = 5.2. Adding the inclusive times again gives 1 + 0.10(5) + 0.04(100) = 5.5, which is wrong.

MCQ Original practice

A processor has base CPI 2.0, 1.2 memory accesses per instruction, and an average memory access time of 3.5 cycles with an L1 hit time of 1 cycle. What is its CPI?

  1. 4.2
  2. 5.0
  3. 6.2
  4. 3.5

Answer: (B). Extra cost per access = 3.5 − 1 = 2.5; stalls = 1.2 × 2.5 = 3.0; CPI = 2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0.

(A) forgets the accesses-per-instruction factor (2.0 + 2.5 − 0.3). (C) charges the full 3.5 per access instead of the extra 2.5. (D) is the average access time used as a CPI, ignoring the base.

MSQ Original practice

Which of the following are true of a two-level cache with own access times?

  1. The three outcome probabilities sum to 1.
  2. The global miss rate of L2 is m1 times its local miss rate.
  3. Halving m1 always halves the average access time.
  4. If m1 = 0, the average equals T1 whatever m2 is.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

(A) holds because the outcomes are exhaustive and disjoint. (B) is the definition of the two denominators. (D) holds because every other term carries a factor m1.

(C) is false: the average is T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3, so halving m1 halves the two lower terms but not T1. On the page’s configuration it takes 4.3 to 2.65, not to 2.15.

NAT GATE CSE 2017 Set 1

Consider a two-level cache hierarchy with L1 and L2 caches. An application incurs 1.4 memory accesses per instruction on average. For this application, the miss rate of L1 cache is 0.1; the L2 cache experiences, on average, 7 misses per 1,000 instructions. What is the miss rate of L2, correct to two decimal places?

Answer: 0.05. 1,000 instructions carry 1,400 accesses; 10 per cent of them miss in L1, which is 140 misses; 7 of those 140 reach main memory, so the local L2 miss rate is 7/140 = 0.05.

The published answer is 0.05. The trap is dividing 7 by 1,000, which gives 0.007 — a count per instruction, not a rate over L2 accesses. The global miss rate, 0.005, is a third quantity and is not what the question asks for.

MCQ GATE CSE 2017 Set 2

In a two-level cache system, the access times of L1 and L2 caches are 1 and 8 clock cycles respectively. The miss penalty from the L2 cache to main memory is 18 clock cycles. The miss rate of L1 cache is twice that of L2. The average memory access time of this cache system is 2 cycles. The miss rates of L1 and L2 respectively are:

  1. 0.111 and 0.056
  2. 0.056 and 0.111
  3. 0.0892 and 0.1784
  4. 0.1784 and 0.0892

Answer: (A). With x = m1 and m2 = x/2: 1 + 8x + 18x(x/2) = 2, so 9x² + 8x − 1 = 0, whose roots are 1/9 and −1. A rate cannot be negative, so x = 0.111 and m2 = 0.056.

(B) swaps the two rates, which the factor of two forbids. (C) and (D) come from misreading the 18 as an own L2 time rather than a main-memory penalty, which changes which term carries the square.

NAT GATE CSE 2016

Consider a system with a two-level cache and main memory. The L1 cache has an access time of 1 ns and a hit rate of 0.8; the L2 cache has an access time of 10 ns and a hit rate of 0.9; main memory has an access time of 500 ns. What is the average access time of the system in ns, ignoring the search time within the cache?

Treat the levels as hierarchical, with each time being that level’s own time.

Answer: 13.0 ns. 1 + 0.2(10) + 0.2(0.1)(500) = 1 + 2 + 10 = 13.0.

The simultaneous reading gives 0.8(1) + 0.2(10) + 0.02(500) = 12.8 ns, which is a printed distractor on that paper. The accepted answer is 13.0, so the intended hardware is hierarchical.

NAT GATE CSE 2025 Set 1

A computer has a memory hierarchy with a two-level cache (L1 and L2) and a main memory. An access looks in L1, then L2, then main memory. The L1 hit rate is 0.95 with an access time of 10 ns. The L2 hit rate is 0.85 with an access time of 20 ns, stated to include the L1 miss penalty. Main memory takes 200 ns, stated to include both cache miss penalties. Find the average memory access time in ns.

Answer to two decimal places.

Answer: 11.85 ns. The probabilities are 0.95, 0.05 × 0.85 = 0.0425 and 0.05 × 0.15 = 0.0075, and the path costs are the printed inclusive times 10, 20 and 200: 9.5 + 0.85 + 1.5 = 11.85.

The published key accepts 11.83 to 11.87. Adding the inclusive times along the path gives 12.5, which is outside the range; the difference is that the L1 lookup gets charged twice.

NAT Original practice

Take the page’s configuration (T1 = 1, m1 = 0.10, T2 = 8, m2 = 0.25, T3 = 100 cycles) with base CPI 1.0 and 1.4 memory accesses per instruction. An engineer halves the L1 miss rate. What is the resulting speedup, correct to two decimal places?

Answer: 1.70. New average = 1 + 0.05(8) + 0.05(0.25)(100) = 2.65 cycles; stalls = 1.4 × 1.65 = 2.31; CPI = 3.31. Speedup = 5.62 / 3.31 = 1.70.

Halving the L2 local miss rate instead gives CPI 3.87 and a speedup of 1.45, so the inner level is the better investment here. Neither change gives 2.00, because the base CPI of 1.0 is unaffected by any cache improvement.

Definitions in one line each

  • AMAT: average cycles (or time) per memory access.
  • Own time: a level’s access time with nothing else in it.
  • Inclusive time: a level’s stated time that already contains the levels above.
  • Local miss rate: misses over the accesses that reached that level.
  • Global miss rate: misses over all accesses to the cache system.
  • Hierarchical access: levels consulted in turn, times add along the path.
  • Simultaneous access: levels started together, slowest started wins.
  • Stall cycles per instruction: accesses per instruction times the excess cost per access.

Formulas and their conditions

  • Probabilities: 1 − m1, m1(1 − m2), m1 m2; they sum to 1 (two levels, sequential).
  • Weighted average: Σ p × path cost (always true, any convention).
  • Factored form: T1 + m1 T2 + m1 m2 T3 (own times, hierarchical).
  • Increment form: s1 + m1(s2 − s1) + m1 m2(s3 − s2) (inclusive times, hierarchical).
  • Simultaneous: h1 T1 + (1−h1)T2 + (1−h1)(1−h2)T3.
  • Three levels: add the term m1 m2 m3 T4.
  • CPI: base + a(AMAT − T1); speedup = old CPI / new CPI.

Decision procedure

  1. Write the three outcomes with a probability and a path cost each.
  2. Check the probabilities sum to 1.
  3. Fix the convention: own or inclusive; hierarchical or simultaneous.
  4. Fix the unit: cycles or nanoseconds, per access or per instruction.
  5. Weight, add, and only then round.
  6. If a rate is unknown, solve; keep the root in [0, 1].

Traps, each with its number

  • “m2 times T3” → 26.8 against the true 4.3 cycles.
  • “add inclusive times” → 12.5 against 11.85 ns.
  • “global as local” → 0.005 against 0.05.
  • “per access as per instruction” → 4.3 against 6.02 cycles.
  • “both roots are answers” → the −1 root is not a rate.
  • “halving the miss rate halves the time” → 1.70, not 2.00.

Six quick revision questions, answers inverted

1. What multiplies T3 in a two-level average? — m1 m2, the probability of reaching main memory.

2. What is the relation between the two miss-rate denominators? — global = m1 × local.

3. When may you add the printed times along the path? — only when each is a level’s own time.

4. What does GATE CSE 2016 distinguish by 13.0 against 12.8? — hierarchical against simultaneous access.

5. Why is the extra cost AMAT − T1 and not AMAT? — the base CPI already pays for one access.

6. Which level is worth improving first? — the inner one: its miss rate multiplies every level below it.
