Topic and objective

TOPIC: Integration — the definite integral as a limit of sums, the fundamental theorem, the three techniques that reduce an unfamiliar integrand to a standard row (substitution, by parts, partial fractions), the mean value theorem for integrals, and improper integrals. This is the word Integration in the Calculus clause of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, together with the word Mean value theorem in the same clause.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Evaluate a definite integral by choosing the right technique and converting the limits correctly; decide convergence of an improper integral from the p-test; find the point the mean value theorem promises; and read a density, a cumulative distribution and an expectation as three integrals.

I read the topic as one definition and four jobs. The definition is the limit of rectangle sums; the four jobs are evaluate, locate a promised point, decide convergence, and connect to probability. Every number on the page is computed by the model and checked against a closed form.

The five ideas

  1. The integral is a limit of sums, and the limit is what the theorem evaluates.
  2. An antiderivative turns an area into one subtraction, F(b) minus F(a).
  3. Three techniques exist to reach five rows of a table, and nothing else.
  4. An improper integral is a limit, and the p-test settles the whole family.
  5. A density integrates to 1; probability, the distribution and the mean are all integrals of it.

Intuition first: adding up thin slices

A rectangle is easy: width times height. A curve is not, because its height changes. So cut the region into thin vertical strips, pretend each strip is a rectangle, add the rectangles, and then let the strips get thinner. The number the sum approaches is the integral. That is the whole idea, and it is the definition, not an analogy for one.

Educational interpretation The strip picture carries the definition exactly. What it does not carry is the sign rule: a strip below the axis contributes a negative amount, which is why the integral is signed area while the word "area" is not.

Formal definition

Riemann sum. Split the interval from a to b into n panels of width h equal to (b minus a) over n, choose a sample point xi in each panel, and form the sum of h times f(xi).

Definite integral. The integral from a to b of f is the limit of that sum as n grows without bound, when the limit exists independently of the sample points.

Antiderivative. A function F with derivative f. The integral is written with limits and is a number; the antiderivative is written without limits and is a family of functions differing by a constant.

Improper integral. An integral whose interval is unbounded, or whose integrand is unbounded at an endpoint, defined as a limit of proper integrals. It converges when that limit is finite.

Symbols, in words

  • the integral sign with a and b — add up from a to b; the two numbers are the limits of integration, and they are not the limits of a sequence.
  • dx — the width of one strip, in the variable x. It is what makes the sum an area rather than a sum of heights.
  • F(b) minus F(a) — the antiderivative at the upper limit minus the same at the lower; often written with a vertical bar and the two limits.
  • plus C — the arbitrary constant in an indefinite integral. It cancels in a definite one and is not written there.
  • converges — the limit defining an improper integral is a finite number.
  • density f — a non-negative function with total integral 1; probability of an interval is the integral over it.

The fundamental theorem, both halves

First half. If f is continuous at x, then the accumulation function F of x, defined as the integral from a to x of f, has derivative f at x. The area accumulated so far grows at the current height.

Second half. If F is any antiderivative of f on the interval, the integral from a to b of f equals F(b) minus F(a). Any antiderivative works, because two of them differ by a constant and the constant cancels.

Verified fact Both halves are stated this way in OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, section 1.3, with the same continuity hypotheses.

The five properties

  • Additivity — a middle point splits the integral into two that add back.
  • Reversal — swapping the limits multiplies the answer by minus one.
  • Linearity — constants pull out and sums split; products and quotients do not.
  • Comparison — a larger integrand gives a larger integral over the same interval.
  • Symmetry — an odd integrand cancels over an interval centred on zero; an even one doubles.

Each is checked in the model on an actual function rather than asserted: additivity on x squared plus 3x minus 2 giving 16.5 both ways, reversal on 4x squared minus 7x giving plus and minus 8 over 3, symmetry on x cubed giving 0 and x squared giving 16 over 3.

Going to 100 panels divides the left error by 9.7, the trapezoid error by 100 and the midpoint error by 100. The trapezoid error is positive and the midpoint error is negative, and the midpoint error is exactly half the trapezoid error here — the two are not the same rule.

The trapezoid error bound is of order one over n squared and the midpoint error bound likewise, with constants (b minus a) cubed times M over 12 and over 24; Simpson's bound is of order one over n to the fourth. (Source: OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, key equations for the error bounds.)

2. Three standard integrals by subtraction

The integral of 1 over x from 1 to e is ln e minus ln 1, which is 1. The integral of sine from 0 to pi is minus cos pi plus cos 0, which is 2. The integral of 1 over 1 plus x squared from 0 to 1 is arctan 1 minus arctan 0, which is pi over 4.

Quadrature gives 1.000000000, 2.000000000 and 0.785398163 — the same three numbers to nine places. The third is worth noticing: a function with no trigonometry in it produces pi.

3. Substitution, and the limits moving

Take the integral from 0 to 2 of 2x times e to the x squared. Put u equal to x squared, so du equals 2x dx — the factor 2x is exactly what is present. The limits move: x equal to 0 gives u equal to 0, and x equal to 2 gives u equal to 4.

The integral becomes e to the u from 0 to 4, which is e to the fourth minus 1, that is 53.598150.

The error this example is built to catch. Evaluating e to the u at the old limits 0 and 2 gives e squared minus 1, which is 6.389056. That is a factor of eight away, and it is offered as an option.

4. By parts, where the boundary term is the answer

For x times e to the x on the interval from 0 to 1, LIATE puts the algebraic factor first, so u is x and dv is e to the x dx. Then the boundary term u v is x e to the x evaluated from 0 to 1, which is e minus 0; the remaining integral is e to the x from 0 to 1, which is e minus 1; and the difference is exactly 1.

Written as an antiderivative, the answer is (x minus 1) e to the x, evaluated at 1 giving 0 and at 0 giving minus 1, so 0 minus minus 1 is 1. Both routes agree, and quadrature gives 1.000000000.

5. Repeated by parts, tabulated

For x squared times e to the x on the interval from 0 to 1, differentiate the polynomial twice: x squared, then 2x, then 2, then 0. Integrate the exponential alongside, and alternate the signs. The result is e minus 2, that is 0.718282, and quadrature agrees to nine places.

The table terminates because the derivative column reaches zero. Against a logarithm it never does, and the tabular method produces an infinite series instead of a closed form.

6. The logarithm rule

The derivative of 1 plus x is 1, which is the numerator, so the integral of 1 over 1 plus x is ln of the absolute value of 1 plus x. From 0 to 1 that is ln 2 minus ln 1, which is 0.693147.

The same rule gives the integral of 1 over x from 1 to 4 as ln 4, that is 1.386294, and it is checked against quadrature. The rule needs the numerator to be the derivative up to a constant, and that constant becomes a factor outside the logarithm, not inside it.

7. The mean value theorem, computed

For x squared on the interval from 0 to 3, the integral is 9, so the average value is 9 over 3, that is 3. Solving x squared equals 3 gives x equal to plus or minus the square root of 3; only the positive root, 1.732051, lies in the interval, and the function there is 3.

Two neighbouring numbers are not the answer: the midpoint value is 2.25 and the average of the two endpoint values is 4.5. Both are plausible options and both are wrong.

8. Improper integrals, both kinds

Infinite limit: the integral of x to the minus 2 from 1 to R is 1 minus 1 over R, which tends to 1. The integral of 1 over x from 1 to R is ln R, which reaches 13.815511 at a million and never settles. The integral of x to the minus 0.5 from 1 to a million is 1998, still growing.

Infinite integrand: the integral of 1 over x from epsilon to 1 is minus ln epsilon, which is 6.907755 at a thousandth, 13.815511 at a millionth and 20.723266 at a billionth, so it diverges. Yet the integral of ln x from 0 to 1 is exactly minus 1: the integrand is unbounded there and the area is still finite.

9. The three probability integrals

For the exponential density with rate 2: the total is 1, the mean is 0.5, the second moment is 0.5, the variance is 0.25, and the probability below 0.5 is 1 minus e to the minus 1, that is 0.632121.

For the uniform density on the interval from 0 to 4 the total is 1 and the mean is 2. For the standard normal the total is 1, the mean is 0 and the variance is 1; the probability between minus 1 and 1 is 0.682689 and the upper tail beyond 1 is 0.158655. Each of these is one quadrature in the model, checked against its closed form.

Where the rules stop applying

Every property and technique here has a hypothesis. These are the ones an exam can step outside of.

Boundaries and counterexamples

Discontinuity in the interval. The second half of the fundamental theorem needs an antiderivative on the whole interval. For 1 over x squared from minus 1 to 1, the naive subtraction gives minus 2, but the integrand is unbounded at 0 and the integral diverges. The subtraction is invalid, not merely inaccurate.

Continuity is not enough for the first half. At a jump the accumulation function is continuous but not differentiable, so the derivative statement fails exactly at that point.

A step function defeats the mean value theorem. The function that is 0 on the left half and 2 on the right half has average 1 and never equals 1. Continuity is the hypothesis doing the work.

Odd symmetry needs a symmetric interval. x cubed integrates to 0 from minus 2 to 2, and to 4 from 0 to 2. The function did not change; the interval did.

Unbounded is not divergent

The integrand ln x tends to minus infinity as x tends to 0, and its integral from 0 to 1 is exactly minus 1. The integrand 1 over the square root of x tends to infinity at 0, and its integral from 0 to 1 is exactly 2.

Conversely, a bounded integrand over an infinite interval can diverge: 1 over x is bounded on every finite piece of the positive line and its integral to infinity diverges. So neither word implies the other, and the p-test is what decides.

The two halves of the p-test point opposite ways

To infinity, x to the minus p converges when p exceeds 1. To zero, it converges when p is below 1. The same exponent gives opposite answers, because in one case the tail is long and in the other the singularity is sharp.

At p equal to 1 both diverge, and only logarithmically: the values 6.907755, 13.815511 and 20.723266 at epsilon equal to a thousandth, a millionth and a billionth look almost stable and are not. (Source: the p-test as stated in the SFU Math 158 course notes, theorems 2.63 and 2.64.)

Degrees, factors and the constant

Partial fractions require the numerator degree to be strictly smaller. If it is not, divide first; the quotient is a polynomial term the answer needs and the split applies only to the remainder.

A repeated linear factor needs a term over the factor and a term over its square. A quadratic factor that does not factor over the reals needs a linear numerator, not a constant.

A definite integral has no plus C. The constant belongs to the indefinite integral, and adding it to a number is not a rounding difference — it is a different question.

A density value is not a probability

The exponential density with rate 2 equals 2 at the origin. No probability is 2, and there is no contradiction: the density is a rate per unit length, and only its integral over an interval is a probability.

A narrow interval can therefore carry a probability larger than the density value times a careless reading of the width, and a single point always carries probability 0 however large the density is there.

Six beliefs that produce a specific wrong answer

Each entry names the belief, the counterexample that kills it, and the option it usually turns into.

"The integral of a product is the product of the integrals"

For f and g both equal to x on the interval from 0 to 1: the integral of x squared is one third, and the product of the two integrals is one quarter. They differ by one twelfth, and the gap grows with the interval.

There is no product rule for integrals. By parts is the closest thing, and it produces a boundary term and a different integral, not a product.

"A definite integral needs plus C"

The constant cancels in the subtraction: (F(b) plus C) minus (F(a) plus C) is F(b) minus F(a). Writing it is harmless in working and wrong in an answer.

The exam version of this is an option that differs from the correct value by a constant. It is the indefinite-integral answer offered to a definite-integral question.

"After substituting, the old limits still apply"

The limits are values of the variable of integration. Once the variable is u, the limits must be u values. For the worked example that is 0 and 4, not 0 and 2, and the wrong choice gives 6.389056 instead of 53.598150.

Either convert the limits with the substitution, or convert the antiderivative back to x before substituting. Doing neither is the error, and doing half of each is the same error.

"Area and integral are the same word"

The integral of x cubed from minus 2 to 2 is 0 and the area enclosed is 8. The integral of sine from 0 to 2 pi is 0 and the area is 4.

A question that says "area" wants each piece counted positively, which means splitting at the zeros and taking absolute values. A question that says "integral" wants the signed sum.

"Divergent means the function is unbounded"

The integral of ln x from 0 to 1 is minus 1 although the integrand is unbounded at 0. The integral of 1 over x from 1 to infinity diverges although the integrand is bounded on every finite piece.

Divergence is a statement about a limit, not about the size of the integrand. The p-test is the tool that separates the two.

"The mean value theorem tells you where"

It asserts that some point exists and says nothing about which. For x squared on the interval from 0 to 3 there is exactly one such point, but a function can have many, and the theorem is equally true in both cases.

A question asking for the number of points is asking something the theorem does not answer; a question asking for one point is asking you to solve f(c) equals the average and keep the roots inside the interval.

How the clause gets tested

Four question types, each with a different first move. Naming the type before computing is what stops the wasted work.

The four types

  1. Evaluate. A definite integral with a technique in mind. Classify the integrand: product, inner function with its derivative, or rational.
  2. Locate. A point guaranteed by the mean value theorem. Compute the average, solve f(c) equals it, keep the roots inside the stated interval.
  3. Decide convergence. An improper integral. Apply the p-test, or compare against a known integral.
  4. Connect to probability. A density, a distribution or an expectation. Write the integral, then use the closed form.

The arithmetic patterns

Average value — the integral divided by the width. For x squared on the interval from 0 to 3 that is 9 over 3, that is 3.

Density check — the total is 1. If your density integrates to something else, the constant is wrong before anything else is.

Exponential moments — with rate lambda, the mean is 1 over lambda, the second moment is 2 over lambda squared, and the variance is 1 over lambda squared.

p-test — to infinity converges for p above 1; to zero converges for p below 1; both fail at exactly 1.

Three checks before you commit

A positive integrand over an interval gives a positive integral. A region below the axis gives a negative one, with no reversal involved. And the integral never exceeds the maximum height times the width.

The last is an inequality, not an estimate: it rules out impossible answers and says nothing about whether a plausible one is right. It is still the fastest check available under time pressure.

NATOriginal practice question

The trapezoidal rule with 2 subintervals is used to approximate the integral of x squared from 0 to 1. The approximation is _______.

Answer: 0.375

With two subintervals the width is 0.5. The trapezoid rule is half the width times the sum of the end values plus twice the interior values: 0.25 times (0 + 2 times 0.25 + 1) = 0.25 times 1.5 = 0.375.

Why 0.25 appears. That is the midpoint rule with one panel, which is a different rule. Why 0.5 appears. That is the left rule with one panel. Why 0.333333 appears. That is the exact value, and the question asked for the approximation.

The lesson computes all four rules at n equal to 10 on the same integral; the trapezoid error there is 0.001667 and the midpoint error 0.000833, exactly half with the opposite sign.

MCQOriginal practice question

The value of the integral of 2x e to the x squared, from 0 to 2, is

  1. e squared minus 1
  2. e to the fourth minus 1
  3. e to the fourth
  4. 2 e to the fourth minus 2

Answer: B

Substitute u equal to x squared, so du equals 2x dx. The limits become 0 and 4. The integral is e to the u from 0 to 4, that is e to the fourth minus 1, about 53.598150.

Why A is wrong. It keeps the old limits, evaluating at 0 and 2: e squared minus 1 is 6.389056. Why C is wrong. It forgets the lower evaluation. Why D is wrong. It doubles the answer, which is what treating the factor 2 as surviving the substitution would give.

The lesson checks the direct quadrature and the substituted integral against each other and against e to the fourth minus 1 to eight places.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following improper integrals converge?

  1. The integral of x to the minus 1.5 from 1 to infinity
  2. The integral of 1 over x from 1 to infinity
  3. The integral of x to the minus 0.5 from 0 to 1
  4. The integral of 1 over x from 0 to 1

Answer: A and C

To infinity, x to the minus p converges exactly when p exceeds 1, so 1.5 converges and 1 does not. To zero, it converges exactly when p is below 1, so 0.5 converges and 1 does not. The value in C is exactly 2.

Why B is wrong. It grows like the logarithm: 13.815511 at a bound of a million, and no bound stops it. Why D is wrong. It equals minus ln epsilon, which is 20.723266 at epsilon equal to a billionth and still rising.

This is the closely confused pair in the topic: the two halves of the p-test have opposite inequalities, and an option set that mixes them is testing exactly that.

NATOriginal practice question

The average value of x squared over the interval from 0 to 3 is _______.

Answer: 3

The integral of x squared from 0 to 3 is 9, and the width is 3, so the average value is 3. The point the mean value theorem promises is then the square root of 3, about 1.732051, because x squared equals 3 there.

Why 2.25 appears. That is the value at the midpoint, 1.5 squared. Why 4.5 appears. That is the average of the two endpoint values, 0 and 9. Why 1.732051 appears. That is the point c, not the average value — the two quantities are different and the question asked for the first.

The lesson solves for c by bisection and confirms that f at c equals the average to twelve places.

MCQOriginal practice question

In the integral of x e to the x from 0 to 1, taking u equal to x and dv equal to e to the x dx, the boundary term evaluated between the limits equals

  1. 1
  2. e
  3. e minus 1
  4. 0

Answer: B

The boundary term is u v, that is x e to the x, evaluated at 1 and at 0. At 1 it is e; at 0 it is 0. So the boundary term is e, and the remaining integral is e minus 1, and the difference is 1 — which is the value of the integral, not the boundary term.

Why A is wrong. That is the value of the whole integral, which is the commonest confusion in this question. Why C is wrong. That is the remaining integral alone. Why D is wrong. That is the lower evaluation only.

The lesson prints the boundary term e, the remainder e minus 1 and their difference 1 side by side on the canvas for this reason.

MCQOriginal practice question

The mean value theorem for integrals guarantees the existence of a point c with f(c) equal to the average value when

  1. f is bounded on the open interval from a to b
  2. f is continuous on the closed interval from a to b
  3. f is differentiable on the open interval from a to b
  4. f is monotone on the closed interval from a to b

Answer: B

Continuity on the closed interval is the stated hypothesis. The proof traps the average between the minimum and the maximum, which needs the extreme value theorem on a closed bounded interval, and then applies the intermediate value theorem.

Why A is wrong. Bounded is not enough: the step function that is 0 then 2 has average 1 and never equals 1. Why C is wrong. Differentiability implies continuity, so it is stronger than needed, and it is not the stated hypothesis. Why D is wrong. Monotonicity is irrelevant; the theorem holds for functions that rise and fall.

The step function is the counterexample the lesson computes: its average is 1 and the set of values it takes is exactly the two points 0 and 2.

NATOriginal practice question

A random variable has the exponential distribution with rate 3. Its expected value is _______.

Answer: 0.333 (that is 1/3)

The density is 3 e to the minus 3x, and the mean is the integral of x times that density from 0 to infinity, which is 1 over the rate, that is one third. The second moment is 2 over 9, so the variance is 1 over 9.

Why 3 appears. That is the rate, not the mean. Why 0.111 appears. That is the variance, 1 over 9. Why 0.667 appears. That is the probability below one third, 1 minus e to the minus 1, which is a different quantity.

The lesson checks the rate-2 case numerically: total 1, mean 0.5, second moment 0.5, variance 0.25, and the probability below 0.5 equal to 0.632121.

Decision procedure

  1. Name the type: evaluate, locate, convergence, or probability.
  2. For evaluation, classify the integrand: product, inner function with its derivative, or rational.
  3. Product: by parts, with LIATE for u. Inner function: substitute and move the limits. Rational: divide if needed, then split.
  4. For convergence, apply the p-test, and check which end is improper.
  5. For probability, write the integral, then use the closed form; check the total is 1 first.

Traps, each with its counterexample

"The integral of a product is the product of the integrals" — x times x on 0 to 1 gives one third against one quarter.

"Old limits after a substitution" — 6.389056 against 53.598150.

"Area equals integral" — x cubed on minus 2 to 2 gives integral 0, area 8.

"Divergent means unbounded" — ln x on 0 to 1 is unbounded and integrates to minus 1.

"A density value is a probability" — the rate-2 exponential density is 2 at the origin.

"The mean value theorem gives the point" — it gives existence, never a formula.

Where this connects

Limits and derivatives are the prerequisite and the other half of the same theorem — that is limits, continuity and differentiation. The probability third of this lesson is probability and random variables, and each named distribution has its own lesson: uniform, exponential and Poisson, normal and binomial. The average value is the continuous form of the mean, and the expectation written as an integral is the same object as the sum in linearity of expectation. In algorithms, the same integral appears whenever a sum is bounded by an area, which is how harmonic sums are estimated in asymptotic complexity and divide and conquer.
