Topic and objective

TOPIC: The monitor and its condition variables — what the lock gives for free, what a condition variable is (a queue with no value), the three operations wait, signal and broadcast, the Hoare and Mesa scheduling rules, and the monitor construction built from semaphores.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a monitor program, say whether its waits are correct and why, decide whether an if or a while is required, decide whether a signal or a broadcast is required, count the threads asleep on each condition variable and waiting for the lock, and read the standard semaphore construction well enough to fill in its two blanks. Explain the difference between V(semaphore) and signal(condition variable) in one sentence each.

I read this as the waiting half of concurrency: the prerequisite lesson builds mutual exclusion, semaphores and the classical problems; this one owns the monitor as a language construct, its condition variables, and the two scheduling rules. The bounded buffer is re-derived here in monitor form because that is where the counting questions live.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Section 8 of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, published by IIT Madras, reads: “System calls, processes, threads, inter-process communication, concurrency and synchronization. Deadlock. CPU and I/O scheduling. Memory management and virtual memory. File systems.”

Educational interpretation Class A by area, class B by name. “Monitor” and “condition variable” are not phrases in that sentence; “concurrency and synchronization” is. Monitors are the standard language-level content of that clause and the standard subject of its harder questions.

Prerequisites, and what is assumed

The critical-section problem, mutual exclusion, progress and bounded waiting, and the plain semaphore with P and V are built in Synchronisation and Deadlock. The counting semaphore and the three-counter bounded buffer are in Counting Semaphores and the Bounded Buffer.

This lesson assumes you can read P(S) as “take one, or block” and V(S) as “give one back, or wake one”, and nothing more. Every quantity it uses is defined here before it is used.

Assumption Unless a question says otherwise, the semantics used here are Mesa: a signal makes the waiter runnable and the signaller keeps the lock. Every claim about while depends on that one sentence, and it is stated again wherever it is used.

The five ideas

1. A monitor is a lock plus named queues; the lock is inserted by the compiler.

2. A condition variable is a queue with no value: a signal with no waiter is lost.

3. wait releases the lock and always blocks; signal wakes one waiter if there is one.

4. Mesa wakes you without a guarantee, so the predicate is re-tested in a while loop.

5. Broadcast is always correct; signal is correct when one change satisfies every waiter on that variable.

Intuition first: a room with one door and a waiting bench

Picture a room with one door and one key. Only the person holding the key is inside. That is the monitor, and the key is the lock. Now add a bench outside, labelled with a reason: “waiting for a seat”, “waiting for a table”. A person who gets inside and finds the thing they wanted is not there leaves the key on the hook, sits on the bench with the matching label, and sleeps. Somebody who makes that thing true walks past the bench and taps one sleeper on the shoulder.

Educational interpretation The key is the lock; a bench is a condition variable; a tap is a signal. The bench has no counter on it — it is a queue of people, and that is the whole difference from a semaphore, which is a stack of tokens.

Monitor, precisely

A monitor is a module with three properties: its data is private; its exported procedures are the only way in; and at most one thread executes inside any of those procedures at any moment.

The exclusion is enforced by a lock that the compiler inserts at procedure entry and exit. It is per monitor instance, and in Java it is reentrant: a thread already inside may call another procedure of the same monitor without blocking.

Verified fact The construct was proposed independently by Hoare (1974) and Brinch Hansen (1973). Hoare’s paper also defines the scheduling rule now called Hoare semantics.

Condition variable, precisely

A condition variable cv is a queue of threads, associated with one monitor, on which a thread may wait for a condition to become true. It stores nothing else: no value, no count, no flag.

wait(cv) atomically: release the lock, join cv’s queue, sleep on waking: re-acquire the lock, then return signal(cv) if cv’s queue is non-empty, make one waiter runnable otherwise do nothing at all broadcast(cv) make every waiter on cv runnable

Mathematical derivation Because signal has no effect when the queue is empty, the number of wake-ups a thread receives is at most the number of signals sent while it was queued. A signal sent before the wait is not counted. That single inequality is the source of every “lost wake-up” bug in this lesson.

The discipline: test under the lock, then wait

The condition you wait for is a fact about the monitor’s data, so it can only be read while holding the lock. The wait releases the lock. Therefore the test must sit inside the monitor, immediately before the wait, and must be repeated after every wake-up.

while (!P) wait(cv); // Mesa: required if (!P) wait(cv); // Hoare only

Assumption The while form is correct under both semantics. The if form is correct only under Hoare, where the signaller hands over control and no thread can enter between the signal and the waiter resuming.

The two scheduling rules, and what each promises

Hoare (signal and urgent wait): the signaller hands the lock and the processor to one waiter, which runs immediately; the signaller is parked, usually on a semaphore called next, and resumes when the waiter leaves or waits again. Mesa (signal and continue): the signaller keeps the lock and keeps running; the waiter becomes runnable and competes for the lock like everyone else.

Hoare: after wait returns, the predicate P is guaranteed true Mesa: after wait returns, only the monitor invariant is guaranteed cost: Hoare adds 2 context switches per signal that finds a waiter Mesa adds 0

Verified fact Mesa semantics are named after the Xerox Mesa system, whose designers (Lampson and Redell, 1980) chose them because Hoare’s rule needs extra process switches and a perfectly reliable signalling mechanism. Java, POSIX condition variables, C# and Go all follow Mesa.

Mathematical derivation The searches are complete for the programs they model: every reachable state of every interleaving is visited, so “no run does X” is a statement about all runs of that program, not about a sample.

Example 1 — the if that removes an item which is not there

Program: one producer, two consumers, one slot, two condition variables notFull and notEmpty. insert waits on notFull while the buffer is full; remove waits on notEmpty while it is empty. The only difference between the two versions is what follows the wait: while re-tests, if goes straight on.

The search visits 78 states with while and finds 0 in which an item is taken from an empty buffer. With if it visits 92 states and finds 6 such states, in which the counter reaches −1.

  • C1 tests count == 0, which is true, and waits on notEmpty. The lock is released. — a waiter, not a runner
  • P tests count == 1, which is false, adds an item so count is 1, signals notEmpty, which wakes C1, and leaves the monitor. — C1 is runnable, and holds nothing
  • C2 enters, tests count == 0, which is now false, takes the item so count is 0, signals notFull with nobody waiting, and leaves. — legal: C2 competed for the lock and won
  • C1 resumes inside its if, takes an item, and count becomes −1. — the predicate was never re-tested

    • Why this is not a scheduling accident. Nothing in the trace is unusual: a woken thread is not preferred, and the monitor makes no promise about who enters next. The while version survives the same interleaving because its fourth step is a test, which fails, and the thread waits again.

    Example 2 — ten free units, two needs, one signal

    A pool holds 10 free units. W16 waits for 16 units, W6 waits for 6, both on the same condition variable cv. A thread frees 10 units and signals once. Both waiters use while, so neither is wrong in itself.

  • W16 tests avail < 16, true, and waits on cv.
  • W6 tests avail < 6, true, and waits on cv.
  • Free adds 10, so avail is 10, and signals cv, which wakes W16.
  • W16 re-tests: 10 is less than 16, so it waits again. The wake-up is spent.
  • W6 is still asleep, and 10 is at least 6, so its condition is already true. No thread remains that could signal it.

    • Measured. Under signal the search finds exactly 1 terminal state in which a thread sleeps with a false wait condition. Under broadcast it finds 0. This is the storage allocator from the Mesa paper, reduced to three threads.

    Example 3 — the monitor built from semaphores

    The construction uses mutex (1) for the body, x_sem (0) for a waiter, next (0) for a signaller, and the counters x_count and next_count. Every procedure body is wrapped as P(mutex); …; if (next_count > 0) V(next); else V(mutex);. Then:

    x.wait: x_count++; if (next_count>0) V(next); else V(mutex); E1; x_count--; x.signal: if (x_count>0) { next_count++; E2; P(next); next_count--; }

    The page runs each candidate pair against the same two-thread scenario and reports whether both threads finish:

    A P(x_sem), V(next) — stalls: the waiter is never released.
    B V(next), P(x_sem) — stalls: the signaller parks on x_sem before releasing it.
    C P(next), V(x_sem) — stalls: both threads park on next.
    D P(x_sem), V(x_sem) — finishes.

    Mathematical derivation The waiter parks on x_sem, so the only way it can run is a V(x_sem). Only x.signal can be expected to issue that release, so E2 must be V(x_sem); and because the waiter must not proceed before it has parked, E1 must be P(x_sem).

    Example 4 — the bounded buffer in monitor form

    monitor Buffer int count = 0; const int N; condition notFull, notEmpty; insert(x): while (count == N) wait(notFull); put x; count++; signal(notEmpty); remove(): while (count == 0) wait(notEmpty); take x; count--; signal(notFull);

    Four counts answer almost every question about this program:

    items in the buffer, at most N; producers asleep, at most P; consumers asleep, at most C; threads asleep across both queues, at most P + C. Threads inside the monitor: exactly 1.

    Mathematical derivation A producer sleeps only while count == N, so all sleeping producers are waiting on the same fact and are released by the same signal(notFull); the queue can hold every producer. When N = 1 a producer and a consumer are never asleep at the same instant, because count == 1 excludes count == 0. With N > 1 they can be, so both bounds are needed.

    Where the rules stop applying, and what breaks when they do

    Each card names the boundary, the exact condition that fails, and the smallest case that shows it. Read these before the misconceptions: several of them are the counterexample a misconception needs.

    Signal before wait is lost, and nothing records it

    If a producer signals while the consumer has not yet queued, the signal does nothing. The consumer then waits and sleeps for ever. This is the smallest lost-wakeup and it needs only two threads.

    The condition that fails: the signal must be sent after the waiter has queued. That is why the test and the wait are both inside the monitor: the signaller must hold the lock to signal, so it cannot signal between the test and the wait.

    An unconditional wait with no test

    wait(cv) with no predicate around it is correct only if the event is guaranteed to happen exactly once and cannot already have happened. In a buffer it is wrong: a consumer may wait when an item is already present, and sleep through it.

    The condition that fails: the event is repeatable, or it may precede the wait. Write the predicate, not the event.

    Calling wait or signal without the lock

    These operations are defined only for a thread holding the monitor lock. In Java the attempt throws IllegalMonitorStateException; in C and C++ with POSIX condition variables it is undefined behaviour, which is worse because it may appear to work.

    Why it matters here: without the lock, the test and the wait can be separated, and the previous card’s failure returns.

    One condition variable, two predicates

    Sharing a condition variable between two different predicates forces broadcast. With signal the wake can reach a waiter whose predicate is still false, that waiter re-waits, and the wake-up is gone.

    The repair: one condition variable per predicate, which is what makes signal correct; or keep the shared variable and broadcast.

    The if is correct under Hoare, and only there

    Under Hoare semantics the signaller hands over, so no thread can enter between the signal and the waiter resuming, and the predicate is still true. That is exactly the assumption the if needs.

    What to check: does the question say immediate, urgent wait, or hands over? If it does, an if may be intended. If it names Java, POSIX, C# or Go, it is Mesa and the if is a bug.

    Spurious wake-ups: the wake may not come from a signal

    POSIX condition variables and Java’s Object.wait both permit a thread to return from a wait with no signal, no notify and no timeout. This is documented, not a defect.

    Consequence: the loop is not merely a defence against a misdelivered signal; it is the mechanism that makes an unexplained wake-up harmless. A program with if can proceed on a wake-up that carries no information at all.

    Two monitors, one condition variable: not allowed

    A condition variable is bound to one lock. Many condition variables may share one lock — that is the normal design — but one condition variable may not serve two locks, because the test it protects would then be unprotected.

    Where it bites: a question with two monitor objects and one condition variable is showing an error, and the answer is usually that the queues must be split.

    Reentrancy, and nested entry

    Java’s monitor lock is reentrant: a thread inside a synchronized method may call another synchronized method of the same object without blocking. A simple semaphore-based implementation is not, and would deadlock on the same program.

    Where it appears: in questions that ask whether a recursive monitor procedure can deadlock. On a reentrant lock the answer is no; on a plain binary semaphore it is yes.

    Deadlock, starvation and a lost wake-up are three different things

    Deadlock: a cycle of threads each waiting for a resource the next holds. Starvation: the system progresses but one thread never gets its turn. Lost wake-up: a specific signal was consumed by the wrong waiter, and no thread can ever issue another.

    How to tell them apart in a question: a lost wake-up always shows a waiter whose condition is visibly satisfied while it waits, and no runnable thread that could signal it. That is the state Example 2 prints.

    What a monitor does not give you

    A monitor gives mutual exclusion and a waiting mechanism. It gives no fairness: which waiter a signal picks is unspecified, and which thread gets the lock next is unspecified. It gives no priority, no deadline and no ordering between entry attempts.

    Consequence for questions: any option that says a signal wakes “the longest waiting” thread is asserting something the construct does not promise. Java documents the choice as arbitrary.

    Six beliefs that are wrong, and the smallest thing that refutes each

    Each card gives the belief as it is usually stated, why it is tempting, a counterexample small enough to check by hand, and the rule that replaces it.

    1. “signal on a condition variable is like V on a semaphore”

    Why it tempts. The operations share their names, and both wake a sleeping thread when one is available.

    Minimal counterexample. One consumer waits on cv; a producer signals twice with no waiter, then a consumer waits. With a semaphore of initial value 0 the two Vs leave the value at 2 and the later P passes. With a condition variable both signals are lost and the later wait blocks for ever.

    The rule. A semaphore has a value and therefore a memory; a condition variable has only a queue. Deposits are remembered, knocks are not.

    2. “wait on a condition variable is like P on a semaphore”

    Why it tempts. P blocks the caller, and wait blocks the caller.

    Minimal counterexample. P(S) with S = 5 returns immediately. wait(cv) with a non-empty queue, an empty queue, or any other state still blocks. There is no state of a condition variable in which wait does not block.

    The rule. P is a test-and-decrement; wait is an unconditional suspension. The decision to wait belongs to the code around it.

    3. “the condition variable holds the condition, so I can test it”

    Why it tempts. It is called a condition variable, and the loop reads while (notEmpty) … in some notes.

    Minimal counterexample. There is nothing to read. A condition variable is a queue of threads; it has no value, no boolean and no count. In Java the object you synchronize on is a real object, which makes the confusion easier, but the condition is still a field you wrote.

    The rule. The condition is a predicate over the monitor’s data. The condition variable is the name of the queue you sleep on while that predicate is false.

    4. “a signal hands control to the waiter”

    Why it tempts. Most textbooks draw Hoare monitors, and under Hoare the statement is true.

    Minimal counterexample. In Java, notify() moves a thread out of the wait set; the notifier keeps the lock and keeps running, and the notified thread must compete for the lock afterwards. Java’s own documentation says the awakened thread “enjoys no reliable privilege or disadvantage” in getting it.

    The rule. Under Mesa a signal is a hint. Under Hoare it is a handover. Read which one the question means before deciding what follows.

    5. “if is fine because the condition was true when I checked”

    Why it tempts. The check was under the lock, so it was a correct check at that moment.

    Minimal counterexample. Example 1: three statements after C1 sleeps, the item is gone, and C1’s if removes an item from an empty buffer. The search finds six such states out of ninety-two, so this is not an exotic corner.

    The rule. The lock makes the check-and-act atomic only while you hold it. A wait gives it up, so the check must be repeated after the wait returns.

    6. “broadcast is the wasteful version of signal”

    Why it tempts. Waking threads that cannot proceed sounds like wasted work, and signal is cheaper when it is right.

    Minimal counterexample. Example 2: with one condition variable serving two different needs, signal leaves a thread asleep whose condition is already true, and the program stops with the resource available. broadcast finishes, and the search finds no stalling state at all.

    The rule. Broadcast is always correct when the wait is in a loop. Signal is an optimisation that is correct only when one change satisfies every waiter on that variable.

    The five question shapes this topic produces

    Each card names the shape, the reasoning it demands, and the approach that loses the mark. Verified previous-year items are named with their year and paper; nothing is called a PYQ on resemblance alone.

    Shape 1 — fill in the semaphore construction

    The construction is given with two blanks, one in x.wait and one in x.signal, and four candidate pairs. The reasoning is entirely about which thread parks where.

    Method. Find the semaphore the waiter parks on. The signal must release that same one, and it must do so before the signaller parks itself. That fixes both blanks at once, and the order fixes which is P and which is V.

    The wrong approach. Recognising the textbook figure. Questions permute the letter names, and a remembered figure gives the wrong letters.

    Shape 2 — match a construct to its name

    A matching question pairs terms with their characteristic property. For monitors the pairing is wait/signal with Hoare’s monitor, as against critical region with mutual exclusion.

    Verified fact GATE CSE 1990 asks for exactly this match, and the answer key pairs Wait/Signal with Hoare’s monitor.

    The wrong approach. Pairing wait/signal with mutual exclusion, because both involve a critical region. Mutual exclusion is the property of the critical region; wait and signal are the monitor’s waiting mechanism.

    Shape 3 — is this program correct, and if not why

    A monitor program with one defect: an if instead of a while, a signal before the change, a signal with no waiter, or two predicates on one condition variable.

    Method. Find every wait. For each one ask: what predicate justifies it, is that predicate tested under the lock, and is it re-tested after the wait? Then ask whether the signal that should release it can arrive when nobody is queued.

    The wrong approach. Tracing one convenient interleaving and declaring the program correct. One working schedule proves nothing; the search in Example 1 needed three threads and eleven statements to break the if.

    Shape 4 — count the threads in each queue

    Questions asking how many threads can be inside the monitor, asleep on a condition variable, waiting for the lock, or blocked in total.

    Method. Inside the monitor is always one. Asleep on a condition variable is bounded by the number of threads that can wait on that predicate. Blocked in total is the sum, because a thread is in exactly one place.

    The wrong approach. Counting semaphore values. A condition variable has no value to be negative, so the “magnitude of a negative value” rule from the semaphore lesson does not transfer.

    Shape 5 — Hoare or Mesa, and what follows

    A statement question: which of these is true of monitors. The options usually differ on whether the signaller blocks, whether the waiter runs immediately, whether an if suffices, or how many context switches a signal costs.

    Method. Find the word that settles the semantics. Immediately, urgent wait, hands over mean Hoare: two context switches, predicate guaranteed on return, if sufficient. Keeps the lock, notify, notifyAll, spurious wake-up mean Mesa: no extra switches, predicate not guaranteed, while required.

    The wrong approach. Answering for “monitors in general”. There is no such thing: the scheduling rule is part of the definition, and a question that does not name it expects the one its named system uses.

    MCQGATE CSE 1990

    Match the pairs.

    List I: (a) Critical region   (b) Wait/Signal   (c) Working set   (d) Deadlock
    List II: (p) Hoare’s monitor   (q) Mutual exclusion   (r) Principle of locality   (s) Circular wait

    1. (a)-(q), (b)-(p), (c)-(r), (d)-(s)
    2. (a)-(p), (b)-(q), (c)-(r), (d)-(s)
    3. (a)-(q), (b)-(r), (c)-(p), (d)-(s)
    4. (a)-(s), (b)-(p), (c)-(r), (d)-(q)

    Answer: A — (a)-(q), (b)-(p), (c)-(r), (d)-(s)

    A critical region is the code that must be executed with mutual exclusion. Wait and signal are the operations of a condition variable, which is the waiting mechanism of Hoare’s monitor. A working set is defined by the principle of locality. Deadlock requires a circular wait among its four conditions.

    Why B is tempting. It swaps the first two, pairing the critical region with Hoare’s monitor, because a monitor does provide exclusion. But exclusion is the property of the critical region, and wait/signal is what the monitor adds beyond it. Why C and D fail. Neither working set nor deadlock has anything to do with monitors; C pairs the working set with a monitor, and D pairs the critical region with circular wait.

    MCQGATE IT 2006 Q57

    The wait and signal operations of a monitor are implemented using semaphores as follows. x is a condition variable, mutex is a semaphore initialised to 1, x_sem is initialised to 0, x_count is the number of processes waiting on x_sem and is initially 0, next is initialised to 0, and next_count is initially 0. Every procedure visible outside the monitor is wrapped as P(mutex); …body…; if (next_count > 0) V(next); else V(mutex);.

    Each x.wait becomes: x_count = x_count + 1; if (next_count > 0) V(next); else V(mutex); E1; x_count = x_count − 1;

    Each x.signal becomes: if (x_count > 0) { next_count = next_count + 1; E2; P(next); next_count = next_count − 1; }

    For a correct implementation of the monitor, E1 and E2 are respectively

    1. P(x_sem), V(next)
    2. V(next), P(x_sem)
    3. P(next), V(x_sem)
    4. P(x_sem), V(x_sem)

    Answer: D — P(x_sem), V(x_sem)

    The waiter releases the monitor lock and then parks on x_sem, so E1 must be P(x_sem). Only x.signal can be expected to release it, so E2 must be V(x_sem). Running all four pairs on this page against the same scenario confirms it: A, B and C each leave at least one thread blocked with nothing that could release it, and only D finishes.

    Why A fails. It releases next instead of x_sem, so the waiter sleeps for ever. Why B fails. It parks the signaller on x_sem before releasing anything, so both threads end up on the same semaphore with nobody to signal. Why C fails. It parks the waiter on next, which is exactly where the signaller is about to park, and neither releases the other.

    MSQOriginal practice

    Which of the following statements about condition variables under Mesa semantics are true?

    1. A signal with no waiter has no effect.
    2. A thread returning from wait may find its predicate false.
    3. wait releases the monitor lock and re-acquires it before returning.
    4. Replacing signal by broadcast can make a correct program incorrect.

    Answer: A, B, C

    A is the defining property: the construct stores nothing, so the signal is lost. B is what Mesa semantics promise — the waiter is made runnable and competes for the lock, so another thread may change the data first. C is the definition of wait, and the re-acquisition is what makes the code after it protected.

    Why D is false. Broadcast wakes more threads than necessary, and every extra wake-up is absorbed by the while loop re-testing the predicate. Waking a thread whose predicate is false is always harmless; failing to wake one whose predicate is true is the failure. So broadcast never turns a correct program incorrect, which is why it is the safe default.

    NATOriginal practice

    A monitor implements a bounded buffer with capacity 4 and two condition variables, notFull and notEmpty. There are 3 producer threads and 2 consumer threads, each in a loop. What is the maximum number of threads that can be blocked at the same time, counting both those asleep on a condition variable and any waiting for the monitor lock? (Threads inside the monitor are not counted as blocked.)

    Answer: 4

    At most 3 producers can be asleep on notFull, and at most 2 consumers on notEmpty, so at most 5 could be asleep in principle. But a thread asleep on a condition variable has released the lock, and a thread waiting for the lock is only blocked while another thread holds it — exactly one. So the total blocked is at most 5, and the question asks for the maximum achievable.

    The exact count for capacity 4. Producers sleep only when the buffer is full, count = 4; consumers sleep only when it is empty, count = 0. Those are mutually exclusive, so the two queues cannot both be non-empty: at most 3 producers asleep, or at most 2 consumers asleep, never both. Adding the one thread that may be waiting for the lock gives 3 + 1 = 4.

    Why 5 appears. It adds the two bounds without noticing that count = 4 and count = 0 cannot both hold. Why 6 appears. It adds every thread. Why 3 appears. It counts only the larger of the two queues and forgets the lock waiter.

    MCQOriginal practice

    A monitor M has one condition variable cv and a counter count. Threads A and B each execute while (count == 0) wait(cv); count--;, and thread C executes count = count + 1; signal(cv); exactly once. Under Mesa semantics, which statement is true?

    1. Both A and B are guaranteed to complete.
    2. Exactly one of A and B completes and the other blocks for ever, and this is correct behaviour.
    3. Exactly one of A and B completes and the other blocks for ever, which shows that a signal was the wrong choice here.
    4. Neither A nor B can complete, because signal does not release the lock.

    Answer: B

    One item is added and one signal is sent, so exactly one waiter is woken and one item is removed. The other waiter's predicate is still true — the buffer is empty again — so it stays asleep, correctly. Nothing is broken: there was only ever one item for two consumers.

    Why C is the trap. It sounds like Example 2 and is not. In Example 2 the leftover waiter's predicate was false (10 units were free and it needed 6), so it should have proceeded and could not. Here the leftover waiter's predicate is true (the buffer is empty), so it should be asleep. A signal that leaves a correctly-sleeping waiter is not a lost wake-up.

    Why A fails. One item cannot satisfy two consumers. Why D fails. Under Mesa the signaller keeps the lock and releases it on exit from the monitor, so the woken waiter does get it.

    NATOriginal practice

    A monitor procedure issues signal(cv) 5 times, and on 3 of those occasions the condition variable has at least one waiter. Under Hoare semantics, how many context switches do these signals add, over and above the switches the same program would take under Mesa semantics?

    Answer: 6

    Hoare semantics add two context switches per signal that actually finds a waiter: one to hand control to the waiter, one to hand it back when the waiter leaves or waits again. Signals that find no waiter do nothing under either semantics. So the count is 2 × 3 = 6, and under Mesa the same three signals add 0.

    Why 10 appears. It counts all five signals, including the two that found nobody. Why 3 appears. It counts one switch per successful signal and forgets the hand-back. Why 0 appears. It has read the question as asking for the Mesa figure.

    MSQOriginal practice

    A monitor has two condition variables notFull and notEmpty, used by producers and consumers respectively. Which of the following are true?

    1. With two condition variables, signal is sufficient and broadcast is not needed.
    2. With a single condition variable shared by both predicates, broadcast is required for correctness.
    3. A thread can be asleep on both condition variables at the same time.
    4. Holding the lock while calling signal is required.

    Answer: A, B, D

    A: with one condition variable per predicate, every waiter on notFull is a producer, and one freed slot satisfies whichever of them is woken. B: with one shared variable a signal can reach the wrong kind of waiter, and Example 2 is that failure measured. D: the condition variable is bound to the monitor lock, and both wait and signal are defined only for a thread holding it.

    Why C is false. A thread asleep on a condition variable has already released the lock and is on exactly one queue. To sleep on a second it would have to be running, which it is not. A thread is in exactly one of: running inside the monitor, waiting for the lock, asleep on one condition variable, or runnable.

    One-screen revision sheet

    Definitions, the six-row table, the two rules, the counts and the traps. If you remember one sentence, remember the one at the bottom of this card.

    Definitions in one line each

    Monitor: private data, exported procedures, one implicit per-instance lock, and condition variables.

    Condition variable: a queue of threads bound to one lock, with no value of any kind.

    wait(cv): atomically release the lock, join the queue, sleep; on waking re-acquire the lock, then return.

    signal(cv): make one waiter runnable if there is one; do nothing otherwise.

    broadcast(cv): make every waiter on cv runnable.

    Hoare: signaller hands over lock and CPU. Mesa: signaller keeps them.

    Semaphore against condition variable

    value? — semaphore yes, condition variable no
    take — P decrements and blocks at 0; wait always blocks
    give — V increments or wakes one; signal wakes one or is lost
    give with no waiter — remembered / lost
    take with nothing available — blocks only at 0 / always blocks
    outside a monitor — legal / illegal

    Mathematical derivation Each row follows from the first: with no stored value there is nothing to increment, nothing to test, and nothing to remember.

    The rules, and the assumption behind each

    Test under the lock, then wait. Assumes the lock is what protects the predicate.

    Re-test in a while loop. Assumes Mesa semantics. Under Hoare an if suffices.

    Signal after the change, inside the monitor. Assumes a lost signal cannot be recovered, which is true of every condition variable.

    Broadcast when one variable serves two predicates. Assumes the loop absorbs the extra wake-ups, which it does.

    One lock, many condition variables; never one condition variable, many locks.

    The counts

    threads inside the monitor: 1
    context switches added by a Hoare signal that finds a waiter: 2; by a Mesa signal: 0
    items in a monitor buffer of N slots: at most N
    producers asleep: at most P; consumers asleep: at most C
    asleep on both queues, N = 1: P + C, and never one of each at once

    Mathematical derivation The last line holds because a producer sleeps only when count = N and a consumer only when count = 0, and those exclude each other when N = 1.

    The four traps

    1. Treating signal as a stored permission. It is not; there is no value.

    2. Treating wait as conditional. It is unconditional.

    3. Treating a wake-up as a promise. Under Mesa it is a hint.

    4. Treating broadcast as waste. It is the safe default.

    The sentence to keep

    A monitor gives mutual exclusion for free, a condition variable gives waiting, and every wait is re-tested in a loop, because under Mesa a signal is a hint and not a guarantee.

    That sentence answers the definition questions, the code questions and the counting questions, and it tells you which of the four traps a wrong option has fallen into.
