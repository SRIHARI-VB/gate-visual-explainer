Topic and objective

TOPIC: The bitwise operators of C — AND, OR, exclusive OR, complement, and the two shifts — and the manipulation they make possible: masks, fields, the two bit tricks, the counting loop, and the four places the language standard refuses to define the answer.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given any expression built from &, |, ^, ~, << and >>, decide its value bit by bit, or state that the standard defines none. Build the mask for a single bit and the mask for a field. Extract a field with a shift and a mask in that order. Use x & (x-1) to clear the lowest set bit and to test for a power of two, with its one false positive. Count the set bits in exactly as many steps as there are ones. And say, for each of the five undefined or implementation-defined forms, that no value is the answer.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class A — Section 4 of the syllabus names “Programming in C”, and these are operators of that language, not an extension of it. Two previous-year papers ask this material directly: GATE CS 2022, Q44 (three bitwise operators on two character codes, printed with %c) and GATE CSE 2017, Set 1, Q55 (a counting loop built from v & 1 and v >>= 1). The words “bitwise operator” are not printed in the syllabus; the language they belong to is.

PREREQUISITES: integer types and their widths, the operators of C, and integer promotion (C fundamentals); two’s complement as a way of writing negative numbers (Number representation); and, for the tables in scene two, the four-row truth tables of Boolean algebra. No pointers, no memory, no recursion.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A byte is eight bits, and a bit is worth a power of two: the leftmost cell of the drawing is worth 128, the rightmost 1.
  • An int is at least 16 bits and is 32 on every platform this lesson mentions; unsigned types have no sign bit, so all their bits are value.
  • Integer promotion: a char operand is converted to int before an arithmetic or bitwise operator sees it, which is why a bit operation on a byte produces an int.
  • The four-row truth tables of AND, OR and XOR, from the Boolean algebra lesson.
  • Two’s complement: a negative value is written as its positive form complemented and then incremented.

Helpful, not required: having met hexadecimal, since the lesson writes bytes as 0xB6 and 0x3C because two hex digits are exactly eight bits.

Approximate learning time: 40–55 minutes with the animation, or 12 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. A bitwise operator is eight decisions, not one. Column k of the result depends on column k of the inputs and on nothing else, which is why no carry ever appears and why every Boolean law holds bit for bit.
  2. A mask is one one in a known place. OR sets, AND with the complement clears, XOR toggles, AND reads. The complement in the second one is the step people leave out.
  3. Fields are shift-then-mask, in that order. Masking first throws away the bits the shift was going to keep, and the wrong answer is plausible enough to survive.
  4. Subtracting one borrows from the lowest set bit. That single fact gives x & (x-1) to clear it, x & -x to isolate it, and the counting loop that runs once per one.
  5. Four forms have no portable answer. Shift count out of range, left shift of a negative or into the sign bit, right shift of a negative, and the negation of the most negative value. A question that offers a number for any of them is testing that you know there is none.

Intuition: a row of eight switches, and a stencil

A byte is eight switches in a row. Ordinary arithmetic on a byte is a conversation between the switches: adding one can carry into the next switch, and every switch cares what the ones below it did. A bitwise operator is the opposite. It reads switch 3 of one byte and switch 3 of the other, decides that one answer, and moves on. Eight independent decisions, made in parallel, with no carrying anywhere.

The second picture is a stencil. Cut one hole in a piece of card, hold it over the switches, and you can read that switch, or paint through it, or scrape it clean. That card is a mask, and the number with a single one in it is the mask for that position.

Educational interpretation The stencil is exact about position and silent about width: a mask has to match the width of the value it is applied to, and a mask built for a byte applied to an int clears the top 24 bits. That is the mistake the Edge cases tab opens with.

Formal definition

The bitwise operators take operands of integer type. Each operand undergoes the integer promotions first — a char or short becomes int — and then the result is computed on the promoted operands.

a & b, a | b, a ^ b — bit k of the result is f(bit k of a, bit k of b)

Read: the three binary operators apply the two-input Boolean function f to each column separately. AND keeps a one where both are one; OR keeps a one where either is; XOR keeps a one where they differ.

~a — bit k of the result is the opposite of bit k of a

Read: the only unary bitwise operator. It flips every bit, including the sign bit, which is why its value is negative for a positive operand.

a << n, a >> n — the pattern moves n places; the result has the type of the promoted left operand

Read: the count n must satisfy 0 ≤ n < width, and the width is the width of the promoted left operand — 8 for a byte after promotion it is 32 for an int, 32 for an int. Left shift fills with zeros; right shift fills with zeros for an unsigned operand.

Verified fact The promotion rule, the result type, the count range and the undefined cases are the text of clause 6.5.7 of the C standard, confirmed against three independent reproductions of that clause (see Sources). The bit-pattern reading of a signed negative operand depends on two’s complement, which C23 mandates and every mainstream compiler has used for decades.

Derivation 1 — the columns are independent, so the Boolean laws are bitwise laws

The definition says bit k of the result depends only on bit k of each operand. Nothing else is consulted: not the neighbouring bits, not the sign, not the position. So any identity that holds for a two-input Boolean function holds for the whole number, in every column at once.

~(a & b) = ~a | ~b and ~(a | b) = ~a & ~b

Read: De Morgan, applied to all eight columns simultaneously. The left form is the one that gets used: to clear a set of bits, complement the OR of their masks.

The same argument gives the count that makes the topic finite: a two-input Boolean function is fixed by four output bits, so there are exactly 16 of them, and C provides three as binary operators plus the unary complement. Everything else in this lesson is built from those four.

Derivation Checked in code on all three operators over all 65,536 pairs of bytes, column by column against the single-bit function, and both De Morgan laws over the same 65,536 pairs. Zero disagreements; the counts are reported in the model-checks panel on the Animation tab.

Derivation 2 — one mask, four operations

Let m be the mask for bit k, which is 1 shifted left by k, so m has value 2k. Then, in column k: OR with 1 forces a 1; AND with 0 forces a 0; XOR with 1 flips; AND with 1 preserves. Every other column is untouched, because m has a 0 there and OR with 0 and XOR with 0 preserve, while the complement of m has a 1 there and AND with 1 preserves.

set: x | m clear: x & ~m toggle: x ^ m test: (x >> k) & 1 or (x & m) != 0

Read: four operations, one mask. Clearing needs the complement of the mask, because the column that must become zero needs a zero in the operand, and only ~m supplies one.

The test is the one that gets written wrong, because x & m is already zero-or-nonzero and looks like a truth value. It is a number: either 0 or m. Comparing with zero is what makes it a test, and it is what the 2017 item’s loop relies on when it writes count += v & 1.

Derivation Checked in code for every byte and every position: the target bit ends where it should, and all seven other bits are unchanged, for set, clear and toggle. 2,048 cases, no disagreement.

Derivation 3 — a field is extracted by shifting, then masking

A field is a run of w bits beginning at position k. Two steps put it where it can be read. First shift right by k, which moves the field down to start at position 0 and brings the bits below it off the end. Then AND with a mask of w ones, which removes everything that was above the field.

field = (x >> k) & ((1 << w) - 1)

Read: shift first, mask second. The mask of w ones is one shifted left by w, minus one: for w = 4 that is 16 - 1 = 15, which is 1111.

Why the other order fails: masking first keeps the low w bits, which are the bits below the field, and the shift that follows then discards them. For the byte 182 with k = 2 and w = 4 the correct field is 13; masking first gives 6, and shifting that gives 1. A different question, answered correctly.

Insertion runs the same two steps backwards: clear the field with a mask and its complement, shift the new value up by k, and OR it in. The order is the algorithm, not a style.

Derivation Checked in code for every byte, every start position and every width that fits: the extracted value equals the bits read directly out of the pattern, in all 1,296 cases. The wrong order is computed too, and disagrees on the drawn example.

Derivation 4 — why the complement is minus x minus one

Write x in binary with a sign bit worth -2w-1 and the rest worth +2j. Complementing every bit replaces each +2j by (1 - that bit)·2j, so the sum of the place values becomes the sum of all the places minus the original sum. The sum of all the places, in a w-bit two’s complement word, is -1 — one more than the largest negative value, and exactly the all-ones pattern.

~x = (-1) - x = -x - 1

Read: the complement of x is minus x minus one, for every signed value. So ~5 is -6, and ~0 is -1, which is the all-ones pattern.

Two consequences carry the rest of the topic. A negative operand has leading ones, so a mask must be applied after any bit work on it, or the ones above the field survive. And widening a negative value copies the sign bit into every new bit, so the byte 0xF8 is -8 as a signed char and 248 as an unsigned char: the same eight bits, two numbers.

Derivation Checked in code on all 256 signed byte values, and the sign-extension rule on all 256 as well. Both hold with no exception; the model-checks panel reports the counts.

Derivation 5 — the shift rules, and the four cases the standard leaves open

A left shift by n multiplies by 2n and fills the vacated low bits with zeros. A right shift by n divides by 2n and discards the remainder, filling the vacated high bits with zeros when the operand is unsigned or non-negative. Those two sentences are the whole defined behaviour. Everything else is one of the following.

count < 0 or count ≥ width undefined left shift of a negative value undefined left shift that leaves the signed type undefined right shift of a negative value implementation-defined

Read: the first three have no value at all. The fourth has a value chosen by the compiler and documented by it, which is why arithmetic (-8 >> 1 = -4) and logical (-8 >> 1 = 124 in a byte) both occur in real code.

The fifth case belongs to the complement rather than the shift: negating the most negative value has no representable result, so x & -x has nothing to say at INT_MIN. And note that an arithmetic right shift floors: -7 >> 1 is -4, while -7 / 2 is -3.

Verified fact Clause 6.5.7 of the C standard states the count range, the unsigned result, the signed left-shift condition and the implementation-defined right shift; the four-row summary above is that clause restated. Derivation The two conventions are shown to differ on all 128 negative byte patterns in code, which is why neither can be relied on.

Derivation 6 — the two tricks, and the counting loop

Subtracting one from a number borrows from the first place that has something to lend, which is the lowest set bit. That bit becomes zero, every bit below it — all zeros before — become ones, and nothing above it changes. Now AND the original with that: the new ones below cannot survive against the original’s zeros, the lowest set bit is zero in the subtrahend, and everything above is identical.

x & (x-1) removes exactly the lowest set bit x & -x keeps exactly the lowest set bit x & (x-1) == 0 <=> x has one bit set, for x > 0

Read: two consequences of one borrow. The first drains a number one one at a time, which is the counting loop; the second isolates the bit, and fails at x = 0, where the expression is 0 but no bit is set.

The counting loop therefore runs exactly as many times as there are ones, where a shift loop always runs the full width. And the same information folds down to one bit for parity: XOR the value with itself shifted by half the width, then a quarter, then an eighth, and the lowest bit is the parity of the number of ones.

Derivation All four claims checked in code over all 256 bytes: the clearing identity and the popcount drop of one, the isolation identity against the position of the lowest set bit, the loop’s iteration count against the popcount, and the parity fold. The single false positive of the power-of-two test is found by enumeration and reported as {0}.

clear bit 3

toggle bit 3

test bit 3

clear bit 2
182 & ~4 = 178 = 10110010
test bit 2

Example 5 — counting the ones three ways, on the same byte

182 is 10110110, which has five ones.

shift loop
eight iterations, whatever the value
clearing loop
182 → 180 → 176 → 160 → 128 → 0: five iterations
parity fold
one operation per level, three levels, one bit out

The clearing loop always runs as many times as there are ones, so its cost is the answer rather than the width. The fold answers a different question — is the count odd — and for 182 it gives 1, which agrees with five being odd.

Derivation The loop’s iteration count is checked against the popcount for all 256 bytes, and the fold against the parity of the popcount for all 256. No exceptions.

1. A mask built for a byte, applied to an int

A mask of 0xFF clears the top 24 bits of an int as well as reading the low byte. If the value was negative, the result is a different number, not the low byte of the same number. Build the mask at the width of the value you are masking, and say which width that is.

Derivation Integer promotion happens before the operator, so a char operand is already an int by the time the mask meets it.

2. A shift count equal to the width

For a 32-bit int, 1 << 32 is undefined, and so is 0xFF >> 32. It is not zero and it is not one; it has no value. On common hardware the count is taken modulo the width, so the expression quietly produces the unshifted value, which is exactly how the bug survives testing.

Verified fact The count range is clause 6.5.7 of the C standard; the modulo behaviour of the hardware is documented in the CERT rule that cites it (see Sources).

3. A negative shift count

x >> -1 is undefined for the same reason as the count being too large: the standard gives the count a range and this is outside it. A count computed at run time needs a guard, because the compiler is entitled to assume it is in range.

Derivation The same clause covers both ends of the range, so this is one rule with two failure modes rather than two rules.

4. Left-shifting a negative value, or into the sign bit

-1 << 2 is undefined, and so is 1 << 31 on a 32-bit int, because the result does not fit in the signed type. The unsigned forms are defined: 1u << 31 is a value, and -1u << 2 is a value, because an unsigned result is reduced modulo one more than the maximum.

Verified fact The standard defines the unsigned result by reduction and the signed result only when the product is representable; the two undefined cases above are the ones outside it.

5. Right-shifting a negative value

This one is implementation-defined, not undefined: the compiler must pick a convention and document it. Most pick arithmetic, which copies the sign bit. The consequence is that no program that must be portable can depend on the answer, and a question that prints it is asking about a specific implementation.

Verified fact The standard names this exact case as its example of implementation-defined behaviour (see Sources).

6. x & -x at the most negative value

The isolation trick needs the negation of x. For the most negative value of a signed type, the negation has no representable result, so the expression has nothing to say there. The trick is safe for positive x, which is the only domain the power-of-two test needs.

Derivation The identity x & -x = 1 << ctz(x) is verified in code on the positive bytes; the boundary is stated rather than computed, because computing it would itself be the undefined operation.

7. x & (x-1) == 0 accepts zero

The power-of-two test as usually written has exactly one false positive, and the page finds it by enumeration rather than by assertion: 0 & -1 is 0, so zero passes, and zero is not a power of two. The guarded form is x != 0 && (x & (x-1)) == 0.

Derivation Scanning all 256 bytes reports the exception set as {0}; that set is a computed result, not a claim about all integers.

8. Two operations that look interchangeable and are not

Complement and negation are the pair most often confused: ~x is -x-1, so for five they are -6 and -5. The second pair is x & ~m and x & m: the first clears bit k, the second keeps bit k and clears everything else. Both are one character apart and they answer opposite questions.

Derivation The complement identity is checked on all 256 signed bytes; the two masks are shown side by side in scene three.

Misconception 1 — “& is a faster &&”

Why it is wrong: && produces 0 or 1 and stops evaluating as soon as the answer is known; & produces a number with bits in it and always evaluates both operands. 6 & 3 is 2; 6 && 3 is 1.

What to hold instead: & is eight Boolean ANDs in parallel. If the answer is going to be printed or used as a value, the number is the answer.

Derivation Computed in code on the drawn pair: 182 & 60 = 52, while 182 && 60 = 1.

Misconception 2 — “x & (x-1) == 0 means x is a power of two”

Why it is wrong: it means x has at most one bit set. Zero has no bits set, so it passes. The page enumerates all 256 bytes and reports exactly one false positive, which is 0.

What to hold instead: the equivalence holds for x > 0. The guarded form is x && !(x & (x-1)).

Derivation Exception set computed by enumeration: {0}.

Misconception 3 — “x & -x gives the lowest set bit, always”

Why it is wrong: it gives the lowest set bit for every non-zero x, and 0 for x = 0. At the most negative value of a signed type the negation itself has no representable result, so the expression has no defined answer there.

What to hold instead: the identity is x & -x = 1 << ctz(x) on the positive values, which is the whole domain of the power-of-two test.

Derivation Checked for every non-zero byte against the position of the lowest set bit: no disagreement.

Misconception 4 — “>> divides by two”

Why it is wrong: for a negative value it is implementation-defined, so it may be an arithmetic shift (-8 >> 1 = -4) or a logical one (-8 >> 1 = 124 in a byte). And even arithmetic shift floors, so -7 >> 1 is -4 while -7 / 2 is -3.

What to hold instead: >> is floor division by a power of two for unsigned and non-negative values, and is not division at all for negative ones.

Derivation The two conventions differ on all 128 negative byte patterns, computed in code.

Misconception 5 — “~x is -x”

Why it is wrong: ~x is -x-1. For five, the complement is -6 and the negation is -5. They differ by exactly one, which is small enough to slip past a check that only looks at the sign.

What to hold instead: the all-ones pattern is -1, so ~0 is -1, and the complement of a positive number is always negative.

Derivation The identity is verified on all 256 signed byte values.

Misconception 6 — “left shift is multiplication, so it cannot go wrong”

Why it is wrong: << is multiplication only while the result fits. 1 << 31 on a 32-bit int is undefined, and shifting a negative value left is undefined however small the count. Neither is an overflow that wraps.

What to hold instead: do bit work in an unsigned type, where the result is defined by reduction modulo one more than the maximum.

Verified fact Clause 6.5.7 defines the unsigned result by reduction and the signed result only when the product is representable.

Misconception 7 — “shifting by the width is fine, it just gives zero”

Why it is wrong: it is undefined, and on x86 the count is masked to five bits, so shifting a 32-bit value by 32 shifts it by 0. A program that relied on getting zero appears to work and then changes behaviour when the compiler optimises the assumption.

What to hold instead: the legal counts are 0 to width-1, and a computed count needs a guard, not a hope.

Verified fact The count range and the hardware’s modulo behaviour are both documented in the CERT rule cited on the Sources tab.

Misconception 8 — “the XOR swap is a safe swap”

Why it is wrong: when both names refer to the same object the first step zeroes it, and the value is destroyed with no error. Modelled as one object, all 256 aliased cases end at zero.

What to hold instead: guard with if (x != y), or use a temporary, which has no aliasing case at all. A compiler already swaps through a register.

Derivation Both the 65,280 distinct pairs and the 256 aliased cases are enumerated in code.

Pattern A — evaluate an expression and print it

The 2022 item: two character constants, three bitwise operators, arithmetic on the results, printed with %c. The work is four steps: convert the characters to their codes, write the codes in binary, apply the operator column by column, then apply the arithmetic and convert back to a character.

Where marks are lost: printing the number instead of the character (option B in that paper), or forgetting that the bit work happens before the addition because of the parentheses.

Pattern B — trace a loop that walks the bits

The 2017 item: a function that adds the low bit and shifts, called in a loop, with a static counter. The bit part is the easy half — it counts ones. The half that decides the mark is the storage class: static means the counter accumulates across calls.

Where marks are lost: resetting the counter on every call, which turns 23 into 7, or reading v & 1 as a truth value and adding 1 instead of the bit — which happens to give the same answer here, and will not on a question where the bit can be 0 with a non-zero v.

Pattern C — which of these is undefined

Four or five expressions, one of which has no defined value. The five forms to check, in order: a shift count that is negative or at least the width; a left shift of a negative; a left shift that leaves the signed type; a right shift of a negative (implementation-defined, so not portable, and usually the distractor rather than the answer); and the negation of the most negative value.

Shortcut, with its assumption: “bit work belongs in unsigned types” answers most of these at a glance. The assumption is that the expression is written with a u suffix; without it, the operand is int and the signed rules apply.

Pattern D — the value of a mask or a field

Either “what is x & 0x0F”, which is one mask, or “what does this extraction return”, which is shift-then-mask. Write the byte in binary first; everything else is reading columns. For a field, the width of the mask is the number of bits in the run, and the shift is the position of its lowest bit.

Shortcut, with its assumption: a mask of w ones is 2w - 1, which is why 15 appears so often. The assumption is that w is smaller than the width of the type.

Pattern E — a condition that is always false

A loop or test whose condition can never be true because of precedence: x & 1 == 0, or a shift inside a comparison. The tell is that the comparison sits to the right of a bitwise operator with no parentheses. Evaluate the comparison first, then the bit operator, and the condition usually collapses to a constant.

Where marks are lost: evaluating the expression as if the parentheses were there, which gives the answer the question setter is counting on.

The decision procedure for any bitwise question

  1. Write every operand in binary at its actual width, with the sign bit visible if the type is signed.
  2. Apply the operator column by column, right to left if you like; carries do not exist.
  3. If a shift is involved, check the count is at least 0 and less than the width before computing anything.
  4. If a negative operand meets a shift, stop: left shift is undefined, right shift is implementation-defined, and the answer to “what is printed” is that no value is defined.
  5. If the result is used as a character, convert at the end, not before.
  6. Check one column by hand against the definition; a single column catches most slips.

This procedure is the procedure scene twelve animates. It is a checklist for reading, not a proof of anything.

Practice set — ten original questions

Every question here is written for this lesson and labelled as original practice. None of them is a previous-year question, and none is presented as one: the two real previous-year items are recomputed on the GATE patterns tab, where their years and papers are stated.

Answers are hidden by default in mock mode and revealed one at a time otherwise. Every solution says why each wrong option is wrong, and the two shortcuts carry their assumptions.

MCQOriginal practice

What is 0xB6 & 0x3C?

(A) 0x34   (B) 0xB4   (C) 0xFE   (D) 0x8A

Answer: (A) 0x34, which is 52. 10110110 & 00111100 keeps a one only where both hold one, giving 00110100.

(B) is the AND with a one in a column where the second byte has a zero. (C) is the OR, 190. (D) is the XOR, 138. Each wrong option is a different operator, which is the commonest way this is asked.

MCQOriginal practice

For an int holding 5, what is ~5?

(A) -5   (B) -6   (C) 250   (D) 5

Answer: (B) -6. The complement is -x-1, so ~5 = -6.

(A) is negation, the closest confused pair in the topic. (C) is the value of the low byte read as unsigned, which ignores the width of an int. (D) is the identity operation. The identity is checked on all 256 signed byte values in the model.

NATOriginal practice

How many bits are set in the byte 0xB6?

Answer: 5. 0xB6 is 10110110, which has ones at positions 7, 5, 4, 2 and 1.

The clearing loop reaches zero in exactly five steps: 182 → 180 → 176 → 160 → 128 → 0. That trace is the check, and it is computed in the page rather than counted by hand.

NATOriginal practice

The variable x holds 42. What is x & (x-1)?

Answer: 40. 42 is 101010, whose lowest set bit is the 2s bit. Clearing it gives 101000, which is 40.

The shortcut is that x & (x-1) subtracts the lowest set bit, so the answer is x minus the largest power of two dividing x — here 42 - 2. The assumption is that x is non-zero; for x = 0 the expression gives 0, which is also what the shortcut gives, so the two agree there by accident.

MCQOriginal practice

Which condition is false for every value of x?

(A) (x & 1) == 0   (B) x & 1 == 0   (C) (x & 1) != 0   (D) x ^ 1 == 0

Answer: (B). It parses as x & (1 == 0), which is x & 0, which is 0 for every x.

(A) is true for every even x. (C) is true for every odd x. (D) parses as x ^ (1 == 0), which is x ^ 0, equal to x, so it is true for x = 0 and false otherwise. The page computes (B) over all 256 bytes and finds it is 0 in every case.

MSQOriginal practice

On a platform where int is 32 bits, which of these are undefined behaviour?

(A) 1 << 32   (B) 1u << 31   (C) -1 << 2   (D) (-8) >> 1

Answer: (A) and (C). A shift count equal to the width is undefined, and shifting a negative value left is undefined.

(B) is defined: an unsigned operand reduces the result modulo 232, giving 231. (D) is implementation-defined, not undefined — the compiler must choose and document a convention, so it is not portable but it is not nothing. That distinction is the point of the question.

NATOriginal practice

What is the number of set bits in 0xF0 ^ 0x3C?

Answer: 4. 11110000 ^ 00111100 = 11001100, which has four ones.

The XOR keeps a one where the bytes differ, so the count is the number of positions at which they disagree. Counting disagreements is often faster than writing the result out.

MCQOriginal practice

A function receives int *p and int *q and swaps through them with the three-XOR trick. Which input destroys a value?

(A) p and q point to different objects   (B) p and q point to the same object   (C) one of them is NULL   (D) the values are equal but the objects differ

Answer: (B). The first XOR of the object with itself gives 0, and the remaining two keep it 0.

(A) is the case the trick works on: 65,280 distinct pairs are checked and all exchange. (D) is safe, because the objects are distinct even though the values are equal. (C) is a different failure — a null dereference, not an aliasing one. Modelled as one object, all 256 aliased cases end at zero, which the model checks explicitly.

NATOriginal practice

The byte 0xB6 is stored in x. What is (x >> 2) & 0x0F?

Answer: 13. 182 >> 2 = 45 = 00101101, and 45 & 15 = 1101 = 13.

Reading the bits directly gives the same answer: positions 5 down to 2 of 10110110 are 1, 1, 0, 1. The shortcut is shift-then-mask, and its assumption is that the field fits: with a start of 6 and a width of 4 the mask would run off the top of the byte, which is why the question fixes both.

MCQOriginal practice

For the byte 182, which expression has the value 1?

(A) 182 & 4   (B) (182 >> 2) & 1   (C) (182 >> 3) & 1   (D) ~182 & 1

Answer: (B). Bit 2 of 182 is set, so shifting it down and masking gives 1.

(A) is 4, not 1: the mask test returns the mask itself, which is why comparing with zero is the honest form. (C) tests bit 3, which is clear. (D) is 0, because the complement’s lowest bit is the opposite of the original’s.

Definitions, one line each

  • Bitwise operator: applies a two-input Boolean function to each column of the operands independently.
  • Promotion: a char or short operand becomes int before the operator; the result has the promoted left operand’s type.
  • Mask: a value with exactly one one, at the position of the bit it addresses; the mask for bit k is 2k.
  • Field: a run of w bits starting at position k; extracted by (x >> k) & ((1 << w) - 1).
  • Lowest set bit: the smallest power of two that divides x; isolated by x & -x, removed by x & (x-1).
  • Popcount: the number of ones in the pattern; computed in popcount steps by clearing the lowest one each time.
  • Undefined behaviour: the standard imposes no requirements, so no value is the answer.
  • Implementation-defined behaviour: the compiler must choose and document a convention; the answer exists but is not portable.

Formulas, each with its condition

x | m sets bit k; x & ~m clears it; x ^ m toggles it

m is 1 << k, and the complement in the clear form is the whole point. All three act on bit k only.

(x >> k) & ((1 << w) - 1) extracts w bits from k

Requires k + w ≤ width. Shift first, mask second; the reverse order answers a different question.

~x = -x - 1

For every signed value. So ~0 is -1, and the complement is never the negation.

x & (x-1) clears the lowest set bit; x & -x keeps it

Both need x > 0 for the clean statement. The power-of-two test is x != 0 && (x & (x-1)) == 0.

legal shift counts: 0 ≤ n < width

The width is that of the promoted left operand. Outside the range: undefined. Negative left operand on a left shift: undefined. Negative operand on a right shift: implementation-defined.

The decision procedure

  1. Write the operands in binary at their real width; show the sign bit when the type is signed.
  2. Apply the operator column by column. There is no carry, ever.
  3. For a shift, check the count is in range before computing.
  4. For a negative operand meeting a shift, stop and say which case it is.
  5. Convert to a character or a number only at the end.
  6. Verify one column by hand against the definition.

This is a reading procedure for exam questions. It is not a proof, and it does not replace knowing why the identities hold.

Traps, each with its defusing example

  • & for &&: 6 & 3 is 2, not 1.
  • x & m as a test: gives 4, not 1, when bit 2 is set.
  • Masking before shifting a field: gives 1 where the field is 13.
  • ~x for -x: gives -6 where negation gives -5.
  • Shifting by the width: undefined, and silently shifts by 0 on x86.
  • >> as division on negatives: -7 >> 1 is -4, while -7 / 2 is -3.
  • The power-of-two test without a guard: accepts 0.
  • XOR swap with aliasing: the value becomes 0, silently.

Every one of these is refuted on the Misconceptions or Edge cases tab with the smallest example that breaks it, computed rather than chosen.

Six questions to test yourself, answers below

1. What is ~0 for a signed type?
2. What is 0xB6 & (0xB6 - 1)?
3. Is 1u << 31 defined?
4. Which mask clears bit 5, and which keeps it?

5. How many iterations does the clearing loop take for 0xF0?
6. A test written x & 2 == 0: what does it actually compute?

Answers. 1. -1, the all-ones pattern. 2. 180: the lowest set bit of 182 is the 2s bit. 3. Yes — an unsigned operand reduces the result, giving 231. 4. x & ~32 clears it and x & 32 keeps only it. 5. Four, because 0xF0 has four ones. 6. x & (2 == 0), which is x & 0, which is 0: always false.
