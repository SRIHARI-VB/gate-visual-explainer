Topic and objective

TOPIC: Counting and combinatorics — the sum rule and the product rule as the only two primitives; the four-box table of arrangements (order matters or not, repetition allowed or not); permutations, combinations and the divide-by-r-factorial argument; over-counting by symmetry (round tables, repeated letters); stars and bars for identical items in distinct bins; inclusion and exclusion; the pigeonhole principle and its generalised ceiling form; counting functions between finite sets (all, injective, surjective); and derangements.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a counting problem in words, classify it in the four-box table and produce the correct closed form; justify every division by naming the symmetry that caused the over-count; count solutions of a bounded linear equation with stars and bars; repair a double-counted union with inclusion and exclusion; and answer a "minimum number that guarantees" question with the generalised pigeonhole bound.

I read this as the syllabus item "Combinatorics: counting, recurrence relations, generating functions" cut at the word counting. Recurrence relations and generating functions are separate lessons in the tracker and are not taught here, though this lesson is their prerequisite. It builds on Sets and Set Operations (power set, cartesian product, the subset count) and reuses the counting habits from Functions and Their Properties (the four counts of functions between finite sets).

Prerequisites

  • Sets and set operations: a set, a subset, the power set with its 2-to-the-n elements, the cartesian product, and union and intersection. Every count in this lesson is a count of sets or of tuples.
  • Functions and their properties: what a function is, and the words injective, surjective and bijective. Scene 8 is those four counts revisited with their reasons.
  • Factorials and the binomial coefficient, including the convention that 0 factorial is 1. If that convention feels arbitrary, the reason is in the Build-up tab: it is the only value that keeps the formulas consistent at the edges.
  • Nothing from calculus, probability or algorithms is assumed. Probability is the natural sequel to this lesson, not its prerequisite.

The five ideas

  1. There are only two primitives: add disjoint alternatives, multiply independent stages. Every formula in the unit is built from these.
  2. Two yes/no questions — does order matter, may items repeat — sort almost every problem into one of four boxes, and the box gives the formula.
  3. Every division is an apology for over-counting. If you cannot name what you counted too many times, you cannot name the divisor.
  4. Identical objects are counted by where the separators go, not by which object went where. That is the whole of stars and bars.
  5. Conditions of the form "at least one" or "not any" are handled by counting the complement and repairing the overlap, which is inclusion and exclusion.

Conventions used throughout

Verified fact Symbols: n is the number of things to choose from and r the number chosen; n! ("n factorial") is the product of all whole numbers from 1 to n, with 0! = 1 by convention; C(n, r), also written as a binomial coefficient, is "n choose r", the number of r-element subsets of an n-element set; P(n, r) is the number of r-element sequences drawn without repetition from n things. "Distinct" objects can be told apart; "identical" objects cannot. Every numeric claim on this page is computed by brute-force enumeration of the small case as well as by the formula, and the two are compared — the live count is on the Animation tab.

Intuition first: a menu, not a mathematics

A café offers three sandwiches and two drinks. How many different lunches? You take one of each, so the sandwich choice and the drink choice happen together, and the answer is six. Now suppose the café offers three sandwiches or two drinks and you may have only one item. That is five. The arithmetic is trivial; the whole difficulty of combinatorics is deciding which of those two situations you are in, and then dealing with the two complications that break both of them: things that cannot be told apart, and conditions of the form "at least one".

So the lesson has one small skeleton — add disjoint alternatives, multiply independent stages — and four repairs: divide when you have counted the same object more than once, choose positions instead of objects when the objects are identical, subtract the overlap when the alternatives are not disjoint, and find the largest arrangement that avoids the condition when the question asks what is guaranteed.

Level 1 — the two primitives

Verified fact Sum rule. If a set of outcomes is the union of disjoint parts with sizes a and b, its size is a + b. The precondition is disjointness: no outcome belongs to both parts.

Product rule. If an outcome is determined by a first choice from a set of size a and a second choice from a set of size b, independently, the number of outcomes is a × b. The precondition is that the second choice set does not depend on which first choice was made.

Both rules are proved by putting the outcomes in a list and counting the list: disjoint parts concatenate, independent stages form a grid. A grid with a rows and b columns has a × b cells, which is the product rule drawn.

Level 2 — the four boxes

Choosing r things from n, with the two questions answered both ways:

repetition allowedrepetition banned
order mattersnrP(n, r) = n!/(n−r)!
order ignoredC(n+r−1, r)C(n, r) = n!/(r!(n−r)!)

Derivation Top-left: r independent stages of n choices each. Top-right: the same product, but each stage loses one option. Bottom-right: top-right divided by r! because the r chosen items can be reordered r! ways without changing the set. Bottom-left: identical items into distinct bins, which is stars and bars (scene 5).

Educational interpretation With n = 3 and r = 2 the four boxes give 9, 6, 3 and 6. The last two are the pair students mix up: choosing two of three things gives 3, but choosing two of three kinds, repeats allowed, gives 6, because the pair (kind 1, kind 1) is now legal.

Level 3 — why the divisions are legitimate

Derivation Suppose a counting procedure counts each object exactly k times, with the same k for every object. Then the number of objects is the count divided by k. The proof is one line: the procedure's output is a list in which every object appears k times, so the list has k times as many entries as there are distinct objects.

This single lemma covers P(n, r)/r! (each set has r! orders), n!/n for a round table (each seating has n rotations), and n!/(a! b! …) for a word with repeated letters (each distinct word is produced by a! b! … relabellings of the identical letters). If k is not constant, the lemma does not apply and the answer needs cases — that is the trap in the Edge cases tab.

Level 4 — stars and bars

Derivation The number of solutions in non-negative integers of x1 + … + xr = n equals C(n+r−1, r−1). Draw n stars for the total, and r−1 bars to separate the r bins. A pattern of stars and bars is a string of length n+r−1 with exactly r−1 bars, and choosing the bar positions determines the pattern. Distinct patterns give distinct solutions and every solution gives a pattern, so the counts are equal.

Variants. Every xi ≥ 1: put one star in each bin first, leaving n−r to place freely, giving C(n−1, r−1). Upper bounds xi ≤ c: inclusion and exclusion over the bins that exceed c. Bins distinguishable but items identical is exactly this model; if the items are distinct as well, multiply by the multinomial factor n!/(x1! … xr!) — but then it is easier to count directly.

Level 5 — inclusion and exclusion

Derivation For three sets, |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A|+|B|+|C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|. The signs alternate because each element's contribution must end at 1. An element in exactly one set is added once. An element in exactly two is added twice and removed once — net 1. An element in all three is added three times, removed three times, net 0, so the triple must be added back.

The general form runs over every non-empty subset of the conditions, with the sign given by the subset's size. This is the tool behind counting surjections (scene 8) and derangements (scene 9), and it is the repair for a sum rule whose alternatives overlap.

Level 6 — the pigeonhole principle

Verified fact Basic form. If N objects are placed in k boxes and N > k, some box holds at least two objects.

Generalised form. If N objects are placed in k boxes, some box holds at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects, where ⌈x⌉ is x rounded up to the next whole number.

Derivation Suppose every box held at most ⌈N/k⌉ − 1 objects. Then the total is at most k(⌈N/k⌉ − 1) < k · (N/k) = N, a contradiction. The bound is sharp: with N = km, placing m in every box shows that no box need hold m+1.

Level 7 — the four counts of a function

From a domain of size m to a codomain of size n:

  • All functions: nm — m independent choices.
  • Injections: P(n, m) = n(n−1)…(n−m+1), and 0 when m > n by the pigeonhole principle.
  • Surjections: Σj=0..n (−1)j C(n, j) (n−j)m — inclusion and exclusion over the images that are missed.
  • Bijections: m! when m = n, and 0 otherwise.

Six worked examples, each one step at a time

Every answer below was also obtained by listing the objects and counting them, so the formula and the enumeration are known to agree. The counts are reproduced by the page's own checks.

Example 1 — a three-course lunch

A café has 4 starters, 5 mains and 3 desserts. How many three-course meals?

  1. One starter and one main and one dessert: stages.
  2. The stage sizes do not depend on earlier choices: the product rule applies.
  3. 4 × 5 × 3 = 60.

Derivation A meal is a triple (starter, main, dessert), and the set of meals is the cartesian product of the three menus, whose size is the product of the three sizes.

Example 2 — a committee with a fixed size

From 10 students choose a committee of 3. Then choose a president and a secretary from the whole 10.

  1. A committee is a set: order is invisible, repetition impossible. Box bottom-right.
  2. C(10, 3) = 10!/(3! 7!) = (10·9·8)/6 = 120.
  3. President and secretary are distinct roles: order matters. P(10, 2) = 10·9 = 90.

The contrast is the whole point: the same 10 people, the same 3 positions, two different boxes because of one word.

Example 3 — five identical sweets, three children

Distribute 5 identical sweets among 3 distinct children, then with every child getting at least one.

  1. Identical items, distinct bins, empty bins allowed: stars and bars with 5 stars and 2 bars.
  2. 7 positions, choose 2 for the bars: C(7, 2) = 21.
  3. At least one each: give one to each child, then distribute the remaining 2 freely: C(4, 2) = 6.

Derivation Both numbers are also obtained by listing every triple (x1, x2, x3) of non-negative integers summing to 5 — 21 of them — and keeping the 6 with no zero entry. The page does exactly this.

Example 4 — the divisibility count

How many integers from 1 to 500 are divisible by 3 or 5 or 7?

  1. Singles: ⌊500/3⌋ = 166, ⌊500/5⌋ = 100, ⌊500/7⌋ = 71. Sum 337.
  2. Pairs: multiples of 15, 21, 35 give 33 + 23 + 14 = 70. Now 267.
  3. Triple: multiples of 105, of which there are 4. Now 271.

Derivation The sets overlap, so the sum rule needs the repair. The page also tests all 500 integers one at a time and gets 271, which is the check that the alternating signs were applied correctly.

Example 5 — onto functions from four elements to three

Count all functions, the injections and the surjections from a 4-element set to a 3-element set.

  1. All: 34 = 81.
  2. Injections: 4 > 3, so by the pigeonhole principle none exist — 0. The falling product P(3, 4) has no meaning.
  3. Surjections: 81 − 3·24 + 3·14 = 81 − 48 + 3 = 36.

Derivation The 3·24 term removes the functions that miss a named image; the 3·14 term adds back the three constant functions, each of which misses two images and was removed twice. The page enumerates all 81 functions and counts the 36 that hit all three images.

Example 6 — a word with a repeated letter, deranged

How many distinct arrangements of LILAC leave no letter in its original position?

  1. First collapse the repetition: 5!/2! = 60 distinct arrangements.
  2. Impose "no letter in its own place" on those 60.
  3. Count by inclusion and exclusion over the four distinct letters, giving 12.

Derivation The page lists all 60 arrangements and tests each one against the condition, and the count is 12 — the same value the official key gives for that question. Doing the derangement on the 120 labelled arrangements first and halving afterwards gives 6, which is the wrong procedure and the wrong answer.

Where the formulas stop working

Each item below is a place where a correct-looking formula gives a wrong answer because one of its preconditions has quietly failed. These are the boundaries that exam questions are built on.

Zero and one as arguments

C(n, 0) = 1 and C(n, n) = 1: there is exactly one way to choose nothing, and one way to choose everything. 0! = 1 is not a convention chosen for elegance — it is forced. The recurrence C(n, r) = C(n−1, r−1) + C(n−1, r) at r = 0 reads C(n, 0) = C(n−1, −1) + C(n−1, 0), so the left side stays 1 only if the empty choice is 1.

Stars and bars at r = 0 bins with n = 0 items gives C(−1, −1), which the formula cannot express; the answer is 1 (the empty distribution), and the honest statement is that the formula requires r ≥ 1.

When the divisor is not constant

The divide-by-k lemma needs the same k for every object. Counting necklaces of 4 beads in 2 colours: there are 24 = 16 strings, but dividing by 4 is wrong, because the all-red string has 4 rotations that coincide (it is counted once, not four times) while a two-red two-blue string has 4 distinct rotations. The counts per object are 1, 2 or 4, so no single divisor exists.

The general tool there is Burnside's lemma, which averages the number of strings fixed by each rotation. That is beyond this syllabus item, and it is mentioned only so you know why the naive division fails rather than that it must be forced.

Identical versus distinct items

"Distribute 5 sweets among 3 children" is 21. "Distribute 5 different toys among 3 children" is 35 = 243, because each toy independently picks a child. The words identical and distinct change the answer by an order of magnitude, and GATE questions rely on your noticing.

A useful test: can two objects be swapped without producing a different outcome? If yes, they are identical for counting purposes, and stars and bars applies.

The empty case in inclusion and exclusion

For surjections the sum runs j = 0 to n, and the j = 0 term is (n−0)m = nm, the total. Omitting it gives a negative answer. For derangements the j = 0 term is n!, the total, and the sum must be truncated at j = n because no arrangement can fix more than n positions.

A quick sanity check: the alternating sum must come out non-negative and no larger than the total. If it does not, a term is missing.

Guarantee questions and their direction

"Minimum number that guarantees three of a kind" is not the number of holes. The largest arrangement that avoids the condition is what you compute, and the answer is one more. Four suits, two cards each is 8, so 9 guarantees three of one suit.

The mirror error: a question asks for the minimum number that guarantees two with the same remainder mod 7. The holes are the 7 remainders, so the answer is 8 — and 7 is the tempting wrong answer.

Repetition that is allowed but not required

"Combinations with repetition" counts multisets: choosing 2 from {a, b, c} with repetition gives aa, ab, ac, bb, bc, cc — six. It does not mean each element must appear twice. The formula C(n+r−1, r) already includes the cases with no repetition, so do not add them again.

The count C(3+2−1, 2) = 6 splits as 3 without repetition plus 3 with a repeat, which is a useful way to check the formula by hand.

Counterexample: the sum rule with overlapping alternatives

Claim to refute: "the number of integers from 1 to 100 divisible by 2 or by 3 is 50 + 33 = 83."

Refutation by a single witness: 6 is divisible by both, so it is counted in both parts, and the true count is 50 + 33 − 16 = 67, since there are 16 multiples of 6 up to 100. The claim over-counts by exactly the overlap.

This is why the sum rule carries the word disjoint. Adding overlapping sets is not a small error that averages out; it is always too large, by exactly the size of the intersection.

Counterexample: dividing when k is not constant

Claim to refute: "the number of distinct necklaces of 4 beads in 2 colours is 16 ÷ 4 = 4."

There are 6: RRRR, RRRB, RRBB, RBRB, RRBR is a rotation of RRRB, and so on — listing them gives RRRR, BBBB, RRRB, RRBB, RBRB, RBBR, which is 6 distinct necklaces. The division by 4 assumed every necklace is counted 4 times; RRRR is counted once. The count per object is not constant, so the lemma does not apply.

Eight wrong beliefs, each with the smallest case that kills it

Each item states the belief, gives a minimal counterexample or a precise reason, and then states the repair. The minimal case is the part worth memorising: it is small enough to check in an exam.

1. "Order never matters in a committee, so C(n, r) always applies"

Why it fails. A committee of 3 with a named chair is not a set. From {a, b, c} the committees are {a,b,c} — one — but the chaired committees are three, because the chair can be any of the three.

Repair. Ask whether two outcomes that differ only by a swap are the same outcome. If a role distinguishes the members, order matters and the box is P(n, r), or C(n, r) × r if exactly one role is named.

2. "C(n, r) counts arrangements"

Why it fails. C(3, 2) = 3, but the 2-element arrangements of {a, b, c} number 6: ab, ac, ba, bc, ca, cb. The binomial coefficient counts sets, never sequences.

Repair. The relation P(n, r) = C(n, r) · r! is the whole distinction. Whenever an answer feels too small by a factor, check whether the question wanted sequences.

3. "Stars and bars gives C(n, r)"

Why it fails. With n = 5 items and r = 3 bins, C(5, 3) = 10, but the true count is 21. The choice is over the n + r − 1 positions, not over the items.

Repair. Count the objects in the pattern: n stars plus r − 1 bars, so C(n+r−1, r−1). Note also that C(n+r−1, r−1) = C(n+r−1, n), which is the form most often written.

4. "The pigeonhole principle tells you which box is full"

Why it fails. Five pigeons in four holes force some hole to hold two, but nothing says which. The principle is an existence statement and gives no information about the placement.

Repair. Read every pigeonhole conclusion as "there exists". If a question asks for a specific hole, the pigeonhole principle is not the tool.

5. "Adding is safe when the cases look different"

Why it fails. Counting the 3-digit numbers with a 7 in the hundreds place (100) plus those with a 7 in the tens place (100) gives 200, but 7 in both places is counted twice; the true count of "a 7 in at least one of those two places" is 190.

Repair. Name the intersection before adding. If the cases can co-occur, the sum rule needs inclusion and exclusion, or the cases must be made disjoint by construction.

6. "Dividing by r! is always the fix for over-counting"

Why it fails. It fixes exactly one kind of over-count: a group of r items whose internal order is invisible. A round table of 4 is divided by 4, not by 4!; a bracelet is divided by 8, not by 4.

Repair. Name the symmetry and count how many times one object is produced. The divisor is that number, and it must be the same for every object.

7. "Every function can be made injective by shrinking the domain"

Why it fails. From 4 elements into 3, no injection exists at all — the pigeonhole principle forbids it. Shrinking the domain changes the question rather than answering it.

Repair. Check the sizes first. If the domain is larger than the codomain, the injection count is zero, and the honest answer is zero rather than a formula evaluated outside its range.

8. "A derangement is just n! minus n"

Why it fails. Subtracting the arrangements with one fixed point subtracts the same arrangement several times. For n = 3, 6 − 3 = 3, but the true derangement count is 2.

Repair. Use inclusion and exclusion over the events "position i is fixed": n! − C(n,1)(n−1)! + C(n,2)(n−2)! − …, which for n = 3 gives 6 − 6 + 3 − 1 = 2.

Two pairs that are confused with each other

Permutation against combination. A permutation is a sequence; a combination is a set. P(n, r) and C(n, r) differ by exactly r! for every n and r. The test is not the size of the answer but the question "would swapping two chosen items give a different outcome?"

Counting against guaranteeing. A count answers "how many objects have this property". The pigeonhole principle answers "must some object have this property". The first is exact and the second is a bound, and a question that asks for the minimum number to guarantee is the second kind even when it looks like the first.

Pattern 1 — non-decreasing sequences

PYQ · GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q5. "The number of 4-digit numbers having their digits in non-decreasing order (from left to right) constructed by using the digits belonging to the set {1, 2, 3} is ______."

Answer: 15. A non-decreasing 4-digit string over 3 symbols is a multiset of size 4 from 3 kinds, so stars and bars: C(3+4−1, 4) = C(6, 4) = 15. Listing all 34 = 81 strings and keeping those that never decrease gives the same 15.

Educational interpretation The phrase "non-decreasing" is the signal that the order of the chosen digits is already forced, so only the counts per digit matter — which is exactly the multiset model.

Pattern 2 — derangement with a repeated letter

PYQ · GATE CSE 2020, Q42. "The number of permutations of the characters in LILAC so that no character appears in its original position, if the two L's are indistinguishable, is ______."

Answer: 12. Collapse the repetition first: 5!/2! = 60 distinct arrangements. Then impose the no-fixed-position condition on those 60 by inclusion and exclusion over the four distinguishable letters. The page lists all 60 and tests each, and the count is 12.

Exam trap Deranging the 120 labelled arrangements first and halving gives 6, not 12. The repetition must be removed before the condition is applied, because the condition is stated about positions in the collapsed word.

Pattern 3 — inclusion and exclusion on a range

PYQ · GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, Q47. "The number of integers between 1 and 500 (both inclusive) that are divisible by 3 or 5 or 7 is ______."

Answer: 271. 166 + 100 + 71 = 337; subtract 33 + 23 + 14 = 70; add 4. The three pair counts are the multiples of 15, 21 and 35; the triple count is the multiples of 105.

Derivation This page tests all 500 integers individually and also applies the alternating sum; both give 271. That is the check worth doing in an exam, on a smaller range, if you have a minute.

Pattern 4 — onto functions

PYQ · GATE CSE 2012, Q37. "How many onto (or surjective) functions are there from an n-element (n ≥ 2) set to a 2-element set?" Options include 2n, 2n − 1 and 2n − 2.

Answer: 2n − 2. All functions number 2n; the two constant functions miss one image each, and there is no overlap to add back because a function cannot miss both images and still be a function. So 2n − 2.

The same inclusion–exclusion machinery gives 36 for 4 elements onto 3 (this page's check), and 150 for 5 onto 3.

Pattern 5 — pigeonhole on pairs

PYQ · GATE CSE 2005, Q44. "What is the minimum number of ordered pairs of non-negative numbers that should be chosen to ensure that there are two pairs (a, b) and (c, d) in the chosen set such that a ≡ c mod 3 and b ≡ d mod 5?"

Answer: 16. A pair is classified by its two remainders: 3 possibilities for the first, 5 for the second, so 15 holes. With 15 pairs you can fill every hole exactly once with no collision; the 16th must collide.

Exam trap The answer is holes + 1, not holes. The question asks for the minimum that guarantees, which is always one more than the largest collision-free selection.

Pattern 6 — a product rule with a reserved assignment

PYQ · GATE CSE 2021 Set 1, Q19. "There are 6 jobs with distinct difficulty levels, and 3 computers with distinct processing speeds. Each job is assigned to a computer such that the fastest computer gets the toughest job and the slowest computer gets the easiest job, and every computer gets at least one job. The number of ways in which this can be done is ______."

Answer: 65. Two assignments are forced, leaving 4 jobs to place. All 34 = 81 placements, minus the 24 = 16 that leave the middle computer empty, gives 65.

Educational interpretation "Every computer gets at least one job" looks like a stars-and-bars condition, but the jobs are distinct, so it is a complement count on the product rule instead. Noticing distinctness changes the tool.

Three more patterns worth recognising

Pigeonhole on suits. PYQ · GATE CSE 2000, Q1.1. "The minimum number of cards to be dealt from an arbitrarily shuffled deck to guarantee that three cards are from the same suit." Four suits, two cards each is 8 with no three of a kind, so 9 guarantees it. Community observation The published key for this question gives 9.

Double counting. PYQ · GATE CSE 2019, Q5. With U = {1, …, n} and A = {(x, X) : x ∈ X ⊆ U}, both |A| = n·2n−1 and |A| = Σk k·C(n, k) are true: count pairs (element, subset containing it) by fixing the element (2n−1 subsets each) or by fixing the subset size. The page verifies both expressions agree for n up to 10.

Stars and bars in disguise. PYQ · GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q26. "The coefficient of x12 in (x3 + x4 + x5 + ⋯)3 is ______." Each factor contributes at least 3, so write each exponent as 3 + yi and solve y1 + y2 + y3 = 3 with yi ≥ 0: C(5, 2) = 10. Generating functions are the next lesson; this is the same stars-and-bars count behind them.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q5 1 mark

The number of 4-digit numbers having their digits in non-decreasing order (from left to right) constructed by using the digits belonging to the set {1, 2, 3} is ______.

Answer: 15. A non-decreasing string is completely determined by how many 1s, how many 2s and how many 3s it uses, because the order is then forced. So count solutions of x1 + x2 + x3 = 4 in non-negative integers: stars and bars gives C(4+3−1, 3−1) = C(6, 2) = 15.

Check by listing. All 81 strings of length 4 over {1,2,3}; keep those that never decrease: 1111, 1112, 1113, 1122, 1123, 1133, 1222, 1223, 1233, 1333, 2222, 2223, 2233, 2333, 3333. That is 15.

Exam trap The tempting move is 34 = 81, which counts every string, not only the sorted ones. The word "non-decreasing" is the instruction to use the multiset model. Derivation The page enumerates all 81 strings and keeps 15, and separately evaluates C(6, 4).

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2020, Q42 2 marks

The number of permutations of the characters in LILAC so that no character appears in its original position, if the two L's are indistinguishable, is ______.

Answer: 12. First count the distinct arrangements: five letters with two identical Ls gives 5!/2! = 60. Then impose the condition: no L in position 1 or 3, no I in position 2, no A in position 4, no C in position 5.

How to reach 12 without listing. Treat the four distinguishable letters I, A, C and the block of Ls as five slots with the Ls occupying two positions. Inclusion and exclusion over the four named letters, applied to the 60 collapsed arrangements, gives 12. The page instead lists all 60 arrangements and tests each against the four position conditions, and the count is 12 — which agrees with the official key.

Exam trap Deranging the 120 labelled arrangements and then dividing by 2 gives 6. That is wrong because "no character in its original position" is a condition on the collapsed word: if either L sits in position 1 or 3, a letter is in its original place. Collapse first, then derange.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, Q47 2 marks

The number of integers between 1 and 500 (both inclusive) that are divisible by 3 or 5 or 7 is ______.

Answer: 271. Singles: 166 + 100 + 71 = 337. Pairs: multiples of 15 (33), 21 (23) and 35 (14) total 70, leaving 267. Triple: multiples of 105, which are 105, 210, 315, 420 — four of them. 267 + 4 = 271.

Why the signs alternate. A number divisible by 6 but not by 5 or 7 is counted in the multiples of 3 and of 5? No — it is counted once by 3 and once by 15, so subtracting the pair restores it to 1. A multiple of 105 is counted 3 times by the singles and removed 3 times by the pairs, reaching 0, so the triple must be added back.

Exam trap Stopping at 267 loses exactly the four multiples of 105. The other classic slip is using 500/15 = 33.3 and rounding to 34 instead of flooring to 33. Derivation The page tests all 500 integers one by one and also evaluates the alternating sum; both give 271.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2012, Q37 2 marks

How many onto (or surjective) functions are there from an n-element set (n ≥ 2) to a 2-element set?

(A) 2n    (B) 2n − 1    (C) 2n − 2    (D) 2

Answer: (C) 2n − 2. All functions from an n-set to a 2-set number 2n, since each of the n elements independently picks one of two images. A function fails to be onto only if it misses an image, which means it is constant. There are exactly two constant functions.

Why no correction term is needed. Inclusion and exclusion would normally add back the functions missing both images, but a function from a non-empty set must have an image, so there are none. Hence 2n − 2 with no further term.

Exam trap (B) is the answer to "not constant", which is the same count — so if you reasoned "all minus the constant ones" and then picked 2n − 1 you have forgotten that there are two constant functions, not one. Derivation For n = 4 the page enumerates all 16 functions into a 2-set and finds 14 onto, matching 24 − 2.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2005, Q44 2 marks

What is the minimum number of ordered pairs of non-negative numbers that should be chosen to ensure that there are two pairs (a, b) and (c, d) in the chosen set such that a ≡ c mod 3 and b ≡ d mod 5?

Answer: 16. Classify each pair by the pair of remainders it produces. The first coordinate has 3 possible remainders mod 3 and the second has 5 mod 5, so there are 3 × 5 = 15 classes — fifteen holes.

Sharpness. Choosing (0,0), (1,1), (2,2), (0,3), (1,4), (2,0), … one representative per class fills all 15 holes with no collision. The 16th pair must land in an occupied class, which gives the required a ≡ c and b ≡ d.

Exam trap 15 is the number of holes, not the answer. Every "minimum to guarantee" question is holes + 1. Derivation The page builds the 15 residue classes explicitly and confirms that 15 representatives are collision-free while any 16 pairs contain a collision.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2021 Set 1, Q19 1 mark

There are 6 jobs with distinct difficulty levels, and 3 computers with distinct processing speeds. Each job is assigned to a computer such that the fastest computer gets the toughest job and the slowest computer gets the easiest job, and every computer gets at least one job. The number of ways in which this can be done is ______.

Answer: 65. The two stated assignments are forced, so 4 jobs remain, each free to go to any of the 3 computers: 34 = 81 placements.

The condition. "Every computer gets at least one job" is already satisfied for the fastest and slowest computers, which hold the toughest and easiest jobs. Only the middle computer can be empty, and it is empty in exactly 24 = 16 placements. So 81 − 16 = 65.

Exam trap Stars and bars does not apply: the jobs are distinct, so "which job went where" matters. The correct repair for a distinctness condition of the form "at least one" is a complement count. Derivation The page enumerates all 34 = 81 assignments of the four free jobs and counts the 65 in which the middle computer receives at least one.

MSQ Original practice question no official marks

Which of the following statements are TRUE? Select all that apply.

(A) The number of solutions in non-negative integers of x1 + x2 + x3 + x4 = 7 is C(10, 3).
(B) The number of solutions in positive integers of the same equation is C(6, 3).
(C) The number of ways to give 7 identical sweets to 4 children so that each child gets at most one is C(7, 4).
(D) The number of ways to give 7 distinct sweets to 4 children is 47.

Answer: (A), (B), (D).

(A) true. Stars and bars: 7 stars, 3 bars, C(10, 3) = 120.

(B) true. Give one to each child first, leaving 3 to distribute freely among 4: C(3+4−1, 3) = C(6, 3) = 20, which is also C(n−1, r−1) = C(6, 3).

(C) false. "At most one each" with 7 sweets and 4 children is impossible: four children can hold at most 4 sweets. The expression C(7, 4) = 35 is the answer to a different question — giving 4 of the 7 identical sweets, at most one each, which is C(4, 4) = 1 in that reading. Either way, the stated count is wrong.

(D) true. Each of the 7 distinct sweets independently picks one of 4 children: 47 = 16384. Exam trap (C) is the distractor that tests whether you check feasibility before applying a formula; a count of zero is a legitimate answer and the binomial expression hides it.

NAT Original practice question no official marks

Five people sit around a circular table. Two of them, A and B, refuse to sit next to each other. The number of distinct seatings, counting two seatings the same when one is a rotation of the other, is ______.

Answer: 12. Total circular seatings of 5 people: (5−1)! = 24. Now count the seatings where A and B are adjacent: treat the pair AB as one block, giving 4 objects around a circle, which is (4−1)! = 6, and the block has 2 internal orders, so 12. Hence 24 − 12 = 12.

Check by fixing a reference. Seat A at a fixed place, leaving 4! = 24 linear arrangements of the other four, and remove those with B in either neighbour seat: B in seat 2 gives 3! = 6, B in seat 5 gives 6, and those two sets are disjoint, so 24 − 12 = 12.

Exam trap Using 5! = 120 forgets the rotation. Using 5!/5 = 24 and then subtracting 4! = 24 gives 0, which forgets that the adjacent block has two internal orders. Derivation The page enumerates all 120 linear orders, reduces them modulo rotation to 24 distinct seatings, and counts the 12 in which A and B are not neighbours.

NAT Original practice question no official marks

A bag contains 10 red, 10 blue and 10 green balls. Balls are drawn without looking. The minimum number that must be drawn to guarantee that at least four balls of the same colour have been drawn is ______.

Answer: 10. The holes are the colours: 3. The largest draw with no colour reaching 4 is three of each colour, which is 9. One more ball forces a fourth of some colour, so the answer is 9 + 1 = 10.

By the generalised form. We need ⌈N/3⌉ ≥ 4, which first holds at N = 10, since ⌈9/3⌉ = 3 and ⌈10/3⌉ = 4.

Exam trap 12 is the answer to "guarantee four of a specified colour", not four of any colour, and 13 is the answer if you forget that three of each is already 9. Derivation The page searches all draw sizes and reports the smallest N for which every multiset of N balls has a colour with at least 4.

NAT Original practice question no official marks

The number of derangements of 5 distinct items — arrangements with no item in its original position — is ______.

Answer: 44. Inclusion and exclusion over the five events "item i is in place": 120 − 5·24 + 10·6 − 10·2 + 5·1 − 1 = 120 − 120 + 60 − 20 + 5 − 1 = 44.

Cross-check. The nearest integer to 5!/e = 120/2.71828 = 44.145 is 44, and the recurrence D(n) = (n−1)(D(n−1) + D(n−2)) with D(3) = 2, D(4) = 9 gives D(5) = 4 × 11 = 44.

Exam trap 120 − 24 = 96 subtracts the arrangements with a fixed point only once, but an arrangement with two fixed points is subtracted twice. The alternating sum is what repairs it. Derivation The page enumerates all 120 permutations of 5 items and counts the 44 with no fixed point.

Revision sheet

Everything in the lesson on one screen: the formulas, the decision procedure, the traps and the small numbers worth knowing by heart.

Formulas

Sum rule: disjoint alternatives add.
Product rule: independent stages multiply.
All functions m → n: nm
Injections m → n: P(n, m) = n!/(n−m)!, and 0 if m > n
Surjections m → n: Σj (−1)j C(n, j)(n−j)m
C(n, r) = P(n, r)/r! = n!/(r!(n−r)!)
C(n, r) = C(n, n−r); Σr C(n, r) = 2n
Multiset count: C(n+r−1, r)
At least one in each of r bins: C(n−1, r−1)
Circular arrangements of n: (n−1)!
Word with repeats: n!/(a! b! …)
Derangements: n! Σj=0..n (−1)j/j!, the nearest integer to n!/e
Pigeonhole: ⌈N/k⌉ in some box

The decision procedure

  1. Are the objects distinct or identical?
  2. Does order matter — would a swap give a different outcome?
  3. May an item be used more than once?
  4. Is there a condition of the form "at least one", "none", "at most"? If so, count the complement and repair with inclusion and exclusion.
  5. Did you count anything more than once? Name the symmetry and divide by its size.
  6. Check on the smallest case you can list by hand.

Traps in one line each

  • "Or" with overlapping sets: the sum rule needs inclusion and exclusion.
  • Committees with named roles: not a set, so not C(n, r).
  • Round tables: divide by n, not by n!.
  • Bracelets: reflections count, so the divisor is larger.
  • Stars and bars chooses bar positions, never items.
  • "Minimum to guarantee" is holes + 1, not holes.
  • Injections from a larger domain: the answer is 0, not a formula.
  • Derangements: subtract with alternating signs, not once.

Numbers worth knowing

3! = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120, 6! = 720. Binomial row 5: 1 5 10 10 5 1. Derangements: D(1) = 0, D(2) = 1, D(3) = 2, D(4) = 9, D(5) = 44, D(6) = 265. Surjections: 4 → 3 is 36, 5 → 3 is 150. Multisets: C(n+r−1, r) with n = 3, r = 2 is 6. All of these are reproduced by the page's checks.

Where this lesson leads

The next topic in the tracker is recurrence relations, which counts the same objects when they are built in stages: a bit string that avoids two consecutive ones is counted by a recurrence, not by a binomial. After that, generating functions package these counts as coefficients of a power series, and the coefficient-of-x-to-the-12 question in the GATE patterns tab is exactly that. Probability, later in the syllabus, divides these counts by a total, so every error in this lesson becomes an error there. Within Discrete Mathematics, the counts of relations and functions you met in Relations and Functions are the same binomial and falling-product arguments, and the subset count 2n from Sets is the sum of a binomial row.
