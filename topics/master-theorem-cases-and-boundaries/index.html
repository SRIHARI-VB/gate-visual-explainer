Topic and objective

TOPIC: The master theorem for divide-and-conquer recurrences, and the three boundaries where it stops answering: the regularity condition of case 3, the gap between case 1 and case 2 where no case applies, and the recurrences that are not of the form $$aT(n/b) + f(n)$$ at all.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a recurrence, identify a, b and f(n), compute the watershed n raised to log base b of a, decide whether f is polynomially below it, equal to it, or polynomially above it, and then state the class — while saying which of the case's own conditions has been checked. Recognise the three shapes the theorem refuses, and solve those by unrolling or substitution instead. State why the regularity condition never needs a separate check when f is a polynomial.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class A — Section 5 of the syllabus names “Asymptotic worst case time and space complexity” and “Algorithm design techniques: greedy, dynamic programming and divide-and-conquer”. Solving the recurrence of a divide-and-conquer algorithm is the arithmetic of that second clause. Three previous-year CSE items are recomputed on the GATE patterns tab, and two of them are recurrences this theorem refuses.

PREREQUISITES: reading T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n) as a recipe (Recurrence relations); the recursion tree as a sum of level weights (Divide and conquer); the three notations Θ, O and Ω and what “up to a constant factor” means (Asymptotic complexity). No probability, no data structures, no calculus.

VISUALIZATION PLAN: eleven scenes. First the three ingredients, because the theorem is a comparison and the comparison needs names. I read this topic as the theorem and its boundaries, not as a first course in recurrences. The neighbouring lessons already solve recurrences by unrolling and by substitution; this one owns the comparison, the three cases, and the places where the comparison fails.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • Reading $$aT(n/b) + f(n)$$: a copies of a problem of size n over b, plus work f(n) at this level.
  • Exponents and logarithms changing base: $$\log_b n = \log_2 n / \log_2 b$$, and $$a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$$.
  • That a geometric series with ratio below 1 is bounded by its first term divided by one minus the ratio.
  • That Θ hides a constant factor but not a factor of &log; n.

Helpful, not required: having drawn one recursion tree by hand, since the level weight $$a^i f(n/b^i)$$ is the only object the theorem reasons about.

Approximate learning time: 40–55 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. The theorem compares two quantities. The work at the top, f(n), against the number of leaves, n^(log base b of a). There is nothing else in it.
  2. The comparison is by a polynomial factor. “Smaller” is not enough; f must be smaller by a factor that grows like a power of n.
  3. Equal levels give one log. Case 2's logarithm is the height of the tree, and it appears only when every level costs the same.
  4. Case 3's regularity condition is free for polynomials. For f(n) = n^p the ratio is exactly a/b^p, so c < 1 is the same statement as p > log base b of a. That is why papers never test it on its own.
  5. The gap is real. n/log n is smaller than n and yet no case applies; the answer is Θ(n log log n), and it comes from unrolling.

Intuition: three questions about a business that keeps splitting

A firm takes one order. To fill it, the firm splits the order into four identical smaller orders, each half the size, and spends some fixed effort on the splitting and on putting the results back together. Each of those four is split the same way, and so on until the pieces are too small to split.

Ask where the firm's total effort goes. Either most of it is spent at the very top, splitting the one big order; or most of it is spent at the bottom, on the enormous number of tiny pieces; or every layer of the firm costs the same, and then the effort is one layer's cost multiplied by the number of layers.

Those three answers are the three cases of the master theorem. The theorem's only job is to tell you which of the three you are in, and it does that by comparing the cost of one layer with the number of pieces at the bottom.

Educational interpretation The firm is an analogy for the recursion tree. It is exact about the arithmetic of splitting and silent about everything else; in particular it says nothing about the cost of the base case, which is why the theorem carries a Θ and not an exact count.

Formal statement

Let a ≥ 1 and b > 1 be constants, let f(n) be a function, and let T(n) be defined on the non-negative integers by

$$T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$$

with floors or ceilings around n/b; they never change the answer. Define the watershed $$W(n) = n^{\log_b a}$$.

Case 1. If $$f(n) = O(W(n)/n^{\varepsilon})$$ for some constant ε > 0, then $$T(n) = \Theta(W(n))$$.

Case 2. If $$f(n) = \Theta(W(n))$$, then $$T(n) = \Theta(W(n)\log n)$$. More generally, if $$f(n) = \Theta(W(n)\log^{k} n)$$ for a constant k ≥ 0, then $$T(n) = \Theta(W(n)\log^{k+1} n)$$.

Case 3. If $$f(n) = \Omega(W(n)\,n^{\varepsilon})$$ for some ε > 0 and if $$a f(n/b) \le c f(n)$$ for some constant c < 1 and all sufficiently large n, then $$T(n) = \Theta(f(n))$$.

Verified fact This is the standard textbook statement, with the regularity condition in case 3 and the extended form of case 2. Two independent online references consulted this run state the same three cases; the page then checks every consequence of them numerically, so no number below depends on a source being read correctly.

Derivation 1 — where the watershed comes from

In the recursion tree, depth i holds $$a^i$$ subproblems, each of size $$n/b^i$$. Subproblems stop when the size reaches 1, which takes $$k = \log_b n$$ levels of splitting.

So the bottom level holds $$a^k$$ subproblems. Substituting $$k = \log_b n$$ and using $$a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$$ gives the leaf count.

$$a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a} = W(n)$$

At a = 4, b = 2, n = 1024: $$4^{10} = 1048576 = 1024^2$$. Both sides are the same count written two ways.

Derivation Checked numerically for five pairs of (a, b) and every k from 1 to 12: $$a^k$$ and $$n^{\log_b a}$$ agree to six decimals at every point.

Derivation 2 — the three cases as one geometric series

The whole tree costs the sum of its level weights, plus the leaves' own base-case work:

$$T(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} a^i f(n/b^i) \;+\; a^k\,T(1)$$

The first term is the interior levels, the second is the bottom level. Everything below is a statement about this one sum.

When $$f(n) = n^p$$, the ratio between consecutive level weights is the constant $$a/b^p$$. That single number decides the case:

  • Ratio above 1 — weights grow downwards, the last term dominates, the answer is the leaf count. Case 1, and $$p < \log_b a$$.
  • Ratio exactly 1 — every weight equals the first, there are k + 1 of them, the answer is one weight times the number of levels. Case 2, and $$p = \log_b a$$.
  • Ratio below 1 — weights shrink, the first term dominates and bounds the rest, the answer is f(n). Case 3, and $$p > \log_b a$$.

Derivation The three drawn recurrences are exactly these three ratios: 2, then 1, then 1/2, with a = 4 and b = 2 and p = 1, 2, 3. Their level weights and sums are computed in the page and checked against closed forms.

Derivation 3 — the gap, and why it exists

Take a = 2, b = 2, so $$W(n) = n$$, and take $$f(n) = n/\log_2 n$$. The level weight at depth i is

$$2^i \cdot \frac{n/2^i}{\log_2 (n/2^i)} = \frac{n}{k-i}$$

So the weights are n/k, n/(k−1), …, n/1: they grow slowly downwards instead of geometrically.

A slowly growing sequence of weights is summed by the harmonic number, not by a geometric formula. With the leaves' own n added:

$$T(n) = n\left(1 + H_k\right), \quad H_k = 1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac1k$$

Since $$\ln k \le H_k \le \ln k + 1$$ and $$k = \log_2 n$$, the answer is $$\Theta(n \log\log n)$$.

Derivation The page computes $$n(1 + H_k)$$ and compares it with the unrolled sum for k from 0 to 20; they agree to a relative error below 1e-6. At n = 1024 the sum is 4023.26.

Derivation 4 — why regularity is free for polynomials

Case 3 asks for a constant c < 1 with $$a f(n/b) \le c f(n)$$. Put $$f(n) = n^p$$:

$$\frac{a f(n/b)}{f(n)} = \frac{a\,(n/b)^p}{n^p} = \frac{a}{b^p}$$

The n cancels. The ratio is a constant that depends only on a, b and p.

So for a polynomial, a suitable c exists exactly when $$a/b^p < 1$$, which is the same inequality as $$p > \log_b a$$ — and that is precisely case 3's first hypothesis. The two conditions are the same condition.

For a non-polynomial f they separate, and the condition has to be checked. The page computes one such f, namely $$n^2$$ multiplied by 2 ± 1 according to the parity of the level, whose ratio reaches 1.5, so no c < 1 exists.

Derivation The ratio and the closed form a/b^p agree to twelve decimal places on four (a, b, p) triples, and the case-3 entries of the 72-entry grid are exactly the entries with a/b^p < 1.

Worked examples, page 1 of 2 — the same tree three times

All three recurrences use a = 4, b = 2 and n = 1024, so the watershed is n² = 1048576 in all three. Only f changes. Every level weight and every sum below is computed by the page from the recurrence, then compared with a closed form.

Example 1 — 4T(n/2) + n, the leaves win

Level i weighs $$4^i \cdot n/2^i = n\cdot 2^i$$: 1024, 2048, 4096, …, 1048576. The ratio is 2 at every step, so the last term dominates.

The ten interior levels sum to 1047552 and the leaves add 1048576, giving 2096128, which equals 2n² − n exactly.

Since f = n and the watershed is n², the gap is a factor of n — a polynomial gap — so case 1 applies and the answer is Θ(n²).

Verified fact The weight ratio is checked to be exactly 2 at levels 1 to 9, and the sum is checked against 2n² − n.

Example 2 — 4T(n/2) + n², the levels tie

Level i weighs $$4^i \cdot n^2/4^i = n^2$$: every level is 1048576, leaves included. There are k + 1 = 11 of them.

The total is 11534336 = n²(k + 1), and the answer is Θ(n² log n).

The exact ratio $$T/(n^2\log_2 n) = (k+1)/k$$ is 1.167 at k = 6 and 1.063 at k = 16: it settles on the constant 1, which is why the textbook can write an equality rather than only a bound.

Verified fact All eleven weights are checked to equal n², and the sum against n²(k + 1).

Example 3 — 4T(n/2) + n³, the root wins

Level i weighs $$4^i \cdot n^3/8^i = n^3/2^i$$: 1073741824, then 536870912, then half again, for eleven levels.

A series with ratio 1/2 is at most twice its first term, so the total lies between n³ and 2n³. The exact value is 2146435072, and the closed form is $$n^3(2 - 2^{-(k-1)}) + n^2$$.

The answer is Θ(n³) = Θ(f(n)), which is case 3.

Verified fact Each ratio is checked to be exactly 1/2, and the sum is checked to lie strictly between n³ and 2n³.

Example 4 — the gap at n = 1024

For $$2T(n/2) + n/\log_2 n$$ the weights are n/10, n/9, …, n/1, then the leaves. Numerically: 102.4, 113.8, 128, 146.3, 170.7, 204.8, 256, 341.3, 512, 1024, and 1024 for the leaves.

The sum is 4023.26, which equals n(1 + H₁₀) exactly. Since H₁₀ = 2.929 and Hk grows like ln k, the answer is Θ(n log log n).

No case of the theorem produces this. It is the gap between case 1 and case 2.

Derivation Each weight is checked against n/(k − i), and the sum against n(1 + Hk).

Example 7 — the 72-entry grid

The page takes a ∈ {2, 3, 4, 8}, b ∈ {2, 3, 4} and p ∈ {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3} with f(n) = n^p, classifies each of the 72 combinations by the rule, unrolls each one to sixteen levels, and checks that T divided by the predicted class stays inside a bounded factor.

The same grid confirms the regularity claim: the case-3 entries are precisely those with a/b^p < 1, the case-1 entries those with it above 1, and the case-2 entries those where it equals 1.

Page 1 of 2

a = 1, and the tree with one branch

With a = 1 the recurrence is $$T(n) = T(n/b) + f(n)$$, a single chain rather than a tree. The watershed is $$n^0 = 1$$, so case 3 applies whenever f is polynomially above 1, which is almost always, and the answer is Θ(f(n)).

Binary search is the standard instance: $$T(n) = T(n/2) + 1$$ gives Θ(log n), and the page's check for $$2T(n/2) + 1$$ is its two-branch cousin.

Derivation The ratio here is $$a/b^p = 1/b^p < 1$$ for every p > 0, so case 3's hypotheses hold and the sum is bounded by f(n)/(1 − 1/bp).

Floors and ceilings

The textbook states the theorem with $$\lfloor n/b \rfloor$$ or $$\lceil n/b \rceil$$ in place of n/b, and the answer is unchanged. The reason is that the rounding can only change a level weight by a constant factor, and the theorem is a statement about factors that are bounded.

This page works on exact powers of b so that every arithmetic step is exact. That is a convenience for the reader, not a restriction on the theorem.

Educational interpretation In an exam, do not spend time worrying about floors unless the question is specifically about them; the class never changes.

When f(n) is exactly on the boundary

The boundary is $$p = \log_b a$$, and there case 2 applies with an extra log. The page computes one entry that sits exactly on it: a = 8, b = 2, p = 3, where a/b^p = 1 exactly and log base 2 of 8 = 3.

That entry is case 2, not a case 3 whose regularity condition fails. The distinction matters: a failing hypothesis in case 3 would leave the theorem silent, whereas this entry is answered by case 2.

Derivation The grid check asserts that every case-2 entry has a/b^p = 1 to twelve decimals, and this entry is one of them.

f(n) = 0, and the base case

If f is identically zero the tree costs only its leaves, so $$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a})$$ with no additive term: case 1 with the widest possible gap.

The value of T(1) never changes the class, only the constant, because the base-case work is multiplied by the leaf count and is therefore the same order as the leaves. That is why the theorem is stated with a Θ and why this page sets T(1) = 1 without loss.

Educational interpretation A question that asks for an exact count is a different question, and the master theorem is the wrong tool for it.

b need not be an integer

Nothing in the statement requires b to be an integer; only b > 1 is needed, because the tree's height is $$\log_b n$$ and the argument never counts whole pieces.

Similarly a need not be an integer in the statement, though in practice it counts subproblems and so it is one. What must hold is that a and b are constants — independent of n.

Derivation The grid uses non-integer p throughout (0.5, 1.5) and reproduces the classification at every entry, which is the same freedom applied to the exponent.

The theorem is sufficient, not necessary

If a hypothesis fails, the theorem says nothing. It does not say the conclusion is false. The page demonstrates this on its own computed counterexample: for the oscillating f the regularity ratio reaches 1.5, so case 3 does not apply, and yet T/f stays between 1.55 and 3.34, so the conclusion happens still to be true.

The honest statement is therefore “the theorem does not license this answer”, not “this answer is wrong”. In an exam, a case that does not apply means you need a different method, not a different answer.

Derivation Both numbers are computed over fourteen levels of that recurrence and are reported as a model check.

1. “Case 1 applies because f is smaller than the watershed”

Wrong. Case 1 needs f smaller by a polynomial factor. Counterexample: $$f(n) = n/\log_2 n$$ against a watershed of n is smaller at every n > 1, and no ε makes $$n^{\varepsilon}/\log_2 n$$ bounded: at n = 240 and ε = 0.5 it is 26214.

Derivation The correct answer there is Θ(n log log n), obtained by unrolling, not by any case.

2. “A log factor in f means the extended case 2”

Wrong. The extended case 2 needs f to be the watershed times a power of log. Counterexample: $$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \log_2 n$$ has f = log n and watershed n; here f is polynomially below the watershed, so case 1 applies and the answer is Θ(n).

Verified fact This is the 2014 Set 2 item; the page computes its exact solution, 3n − log₂n − 2.

3. “The log in case 2 comes from a binary search inside the algorithm”

Wrong. It comes from the height of the tree. Counterexample: merge sort has no binary search and still costs Θ(n log n); the log is the k + 1 levels of its tree.

Derivation With f = n² and a = 4, b = 2 the answer is Θ(n² log n) for the same reason, with no search anywhere in the algorithm.

4. “The regularity condition has to be checked in every case-3 question”

Unnecessary for polynomials. For f(n) = n^p the ratio $$a f(n/b)/f(n)$$ equals a/b^p exactly, so c < 1 exists precisely when p > log base b of a — which is already case 3's first hypothesis.

It is a separate condition only for a non-polynomial f, such as the oscillating example the page computes.

Derivation Checked on four triples: the ratio and a/b^p agree to twelve decimals.

5. “Theta(n) and Theta(n log log n) are the same answer”

Wrong. Θ hides constants, not logarithms. Counterexample: at n = 2512 the two differ by the factor log log n, which is about 9. So an option saying Θ(n) for the gap recurrence is wrong even though it is a valid upper bound.

Educational interpretation A GATE option is a class, and a class that is looser than the truth is a wrong option when a tighter one is offered.

6. “A recurrence that is not the theorem's shape is a hard recurrence”

Wrong. The three refused shapes are each solved in two lines. Counterexample: $$T(n) = 2T(n-1) + n2^n$$ unrolls to a sum over n levels, giving Θ(n²2n); $$T(n) = \sqrt{n}T(\sqrt{n}) + n$$ becomes Θ(n log log n) after substituting n = 22m.

Verified fact Both are computed in the page, the first to n = 40 and the second to n = 2512.

How this unit is actually asked

Three shapes, and the first step is the same in all of them: decide whether the recurrence has the theorem's form at all. The three verified items below include two that do not, which is the pattern the papers seem to prefer.

Pattern A — the recurrence that does fit

GATE CSE 2014, Set 2. “Which one of the following correctly determines the solution of the recurrence relation with T(1) = 1? $$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \log n$$” Options Θ(n), Θ(n log n), Θ(n²), Θ(log n).

Step 1. a = 2, b = 2, f = log n, so the watershed is n. Step 2. Is log n polynomially below n? Yes: log n ≤ n0.5 for every n ≥ 16, so ε = 0.5 works. Step 3. Case 1, answer Θ(n).

Answer Θ(n). The page's exact solution is 3n − log₂n − 2, which is Θ(n) and rules out the other three options directly.

Verified fact Wording and answer from two independent past-paper archives that agree; the official paper was not opened this run. The solution is recomputed here.

Pattern B — the recurrence that does not fit

GATE CSE 2024, Set 1. “Consider the following recurrence relation: $$T(n) = \sqrt{n}\,T(\sqrt{n}) + n$$ for n ≥ 1, and 1 for n = 1. Which one of the following options is CORRECT?” Options Θ(n log log n), Θ(n log n), Θ(n² log n), Θ(n² log log n).

Step 1. The coefficient in front of the recursive call is √n, not a constant, so there is no a and the theorem does not apply. Step 2. Substitute n = 22m: the recurrence becomes one that adds 1 per step, so T = n · log log n.

Answer Θ(n log log n). The page computes T/(n log log n) as 1.43 at n = 216 falling to 1.19 at n = 2512, and T/(n log n) falling to 0.02, which excludes the second option.

Verified fact Wording and answer from two independent past-paper archives that agree; both ratios are computed in the page.

Pattern C — subtract and conquer

GATE CSE 2025, Set 1. “Consider the following recurrence relation: $$T(n) = 2T(n-1) + n2^n$$ for n > 0, T(0) = 1. Which ONE of the following options is CORRECT?” Options Θ(n²2n), Θ(n2n), Θ((log n)²2n), Θ(4n).

Step 1. The size falls by 1, not by a factor, so b does not exist. Step 2. Unroll: each level contributes n2n reduced by successive powers of 2, so the sum is dominated by the first terms and totals about ½n²2n.

Answer Θ(n²2n). The page computes the recurrence exactly to n = 40 and reports T/(n²2n) falling from 0.560 to 0.513.

Verified fact Wording and answer from a past-paper archive; the ratio is computed here, not quoted.

The four-step procedure

  1. Identify a, b and f from the recurrence — and check first that they are constants and that the argument is n/b. If not, stop: the theorem does not apply.
  2. Compute the watershed $$n^{\log_b a}$$. Write the exponent down before looking at f; it is the number you will compare against.
  3. Place f: polynomially below, equal (up to a power of log), or polynomially above. Say which ε you are using, or say that none exists.
  4. Check the case's own condition — the polynomial gap for cases 1 and 3, the regularity condition for case 3 — then write the answer.

The fourth step is where marks are lost, and it is the step a candidate is most likely to skip because the first three feel like the whole method.

Q1 — classify by the watershed

Which is the solution of $$T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n^2$$?

(a) Θ(n²)   (b) Θ(n² log n)   (c) Θ(n³)   (d) Θ(n log n)

Answer: (b)

a = 4, b = 2 gives a watershed of $$n^{\log_2 4} = n^2$$, and f = n² equals it exactly. Case 2, so the answer is the watershed times a log.

The page's exact solution is n²(k + 1): at n = 1024 that is 11534336, and T/(n² log₂n) = (k+1)/k settles on 1.

(a) drops the log, which case 2 always adds. (c) uses the exponent of f as if it were the answer, which is case 3's answer and needs f strictly above the watershed. (d) uses log base a instead of log base b of a.

MCQ original

Q2 — the gap

Which is the solution of $$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n/\log_2 n$$?

(a) Θ(n)   (b) Θ(n log n)   (c) Θ(n log log n)   (d) Θ(log n)

Answer: (c)

The watershed is n and f is just below it, but no ε makes $$n^{\varepsilon}/\log_2 n$$ bounded, so case 1 does not apply. Unrolling gives the harmonic weights n/(k−i) and the total n(1 + Hk), which is Θ(n log log n).

(a) is the answer case 1 would give, and it is wrong because the gap is not polynomial. (b) overstates it. (d) is the answer for f = 1, a different recurrence.

Q3 — the watershed itself

For $$T(n) = 8T(n/2) + n^2$$, the exponent of n in the watershed is ______.

Answer: 3

$$\log_2 8 = 3$$, so the watershed is n³. Since f = n² is polynomially below it (a factor of n), case 1 applies and the answer is Θ(n³).

Answering 2 takes the exponent from f, which is the commonest slip in this topic. The ratio a/b^p here is 8/4 = 2 > 1, which is the case-1 signature.

Q4 — which statements are true

For recurrences of the form $$T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n)$$ with a, b constants and b > 1, which are TRUE?

(a) if f(n) is a constant, case 1 applies whenever a > 1   (b) the answer in case 2 always contains a log   (c) case 3 needs f polynomially above the watershed   (d) if no case applies, the recurrence has no solution

Answer: (a), (b) and (c)

(a) holds because a constant is polynomially below any positive power of n, and a > 1 makes the watershed at least nsomething positive. (b) holds by the statement of case 2. (c) is the first hypothesis of case 3.

(d) is false: the theorem being silent says nothing about the recurrence. The gap recurrence has the solution Θ(n log log n).

(d) is the misconception the Edge-cases tab is built around: a failed hypothesis is not a false conclusion.

Q5 — the regularity ratio

For f(n) = n2.5, a = 4 and b = 2, the value of $$a f(n/b)/f(n)$$ is:

(a) 0.5   (b) 4/22.5 ≈ 0.707   (c) 1   (d) it depends on n

Answer: (b)

The ratio is a/b^p = 4/22.5 = 4/5.657 ≈ 0.707, which is below 1, so case 3's regularity condition holds with c = 0.707.

Since 2.5 > log₂4 = 2, this is a case-3 entry: the answer is Θ(n2.5).

(a) is the ratio for p = 2, which is the boundary and not this question. (c) is the boundary case again. (d) is the misconception: for a polynomial the n cancels and the ratio is constant.

Q6 — the shape test

For which of these does the master theorem apply directly?

(a) $$T(n) = 2T(n-1) + n$$   (b) $$T(n) = T(\sqrt{n}) + 1$$   (c) $$T(n) = 9T(n/3) + n$$   (d) $$T(n) = n\,T(n/2) + n$$

Answer: (c)

a = 9, b = 3, f = n: the watershed is $$n^{\log_3 9} = n^2$$, f is polynomially below it, so case 1 gives Θ(n²).

(a) subtracts, so there is no b. (b) transforms the argument and has no constant a. (d) has a coefficient n that depends on n, so there is no constant a either.

Q7 — one log more

$$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n\log_2^3 n$$. The exponent of log n in the answer is ______.

Answer: 4

The watershed is n and f = n log³ n is the watershed times log³ n, so the extended case 2 applies with k = 3 and gives Θ(n log⁴ n).

Answering 3 forgets that the extension adds one to the exponent. The page checks the ladder for k = 0, 1, 2 and finds the ratio settling in each case.

Q8 — the false statement

Which statement is FALSE?

(a) case 1 requires f to be polynomially below the watershed   (b) case 2's log is the height of the tree   (c) for a polynomial f, the regularity condition is implied by the polynomial gap   (d) if case 1 and case 2 both fail, case 3 must apply

Answer: (d)

If f is neither polynomially below nor equal to the watershed, the theorem is silent, and it may be silent because f sits in the gap between them — as $$n/\log_2 n$$ does. Case 3 needs f polynomially above, which is a different condition again.

(a), (b) and (c) are the three statements the page derives and checks. (d) is the error of treating the three cases as a partition that must cover everything.

The theorem in four lines

Shape
T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n), a ≥ 1, b > 1, both constant
Watershed
W(n) = nlogb a
Case 1
f ≤ W/nε → Θ(W)
Case 2
f = Θ(W logk n) → Θ(W logk+1 n)
Case 3
f ≥ Wnε and af(n/b) ≤ cf(n) → Θ(f)
Ratio test
f = np → the level ratio is a/bp

The ratio test is the fast route: above 1 is case 1, exactly 1 is case 2, below 1 is case 3, and it is the same number as the regularity constant.

Three shapes that are not the theorem's

  • Subtract and conquer: T(n) = aT(n−1) + f(n). No b. Unroll over n levels, so the answer gains a factor n where the theorem's would gain a log.
  • Domain transform: T(√n) or T(n−√n). No constant a or no constant shrink. Substitute to turn it into a constant-shape recurrence, or unroll.
  • Non-polynomial gap: f between W/nε and W for every ε. No case applies; unroll.

All three are solved exactly on this page, so “the theorem does not apply” is never the end of an answer.

Fast tests that are safe

Sanity check
the answer is never below Θ(nlogb a) nor above Θ(f) when f dominates
Case 2 signature
f and the watershed have the same exponent
Case 1 signature
the leaf count is the biggest term: exponent of f below logb a
Case 3 signature
the root is the biggest term: exponent of f above logb a
Regularity
free for np; check it only for a non-polynomial f

These five cover every classification the papers ask for; the unsafe shortcut is reading “f is smaller” as case 1 without asking whether the gap is polynomial.

What to say in one sentence

The master theorem compares the work at the top of the recursion tree with the number of leaves, and answers with whichever of the two dominates — multiplying by one log when neither does — provided the comparison is by a polynomial factor and, in the root-dominant case, the levels shrink by a fixed fraction.

If you remember one thing: the three cases are one geometric series read at ratio above 1, equal to 1, and below 1.

The four-step procedure, again

  1. Shape first. Is it aT(n/b) + f(n) with constant a, b? If not, stop.
  2. Watershed. Compute the exponent logb a before reading f.
  3. Place f. Below, equal (up to log powers), or above — and name the ε or say none exists.
  4. Condition. Polynomial gap for cases 1 and 3, regularity for case 3, then write the class.

The first step is the one the 2024 and 2025 items were testing.
