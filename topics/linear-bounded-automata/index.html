Topic and objective

TOPIC: The linear bounded automaton — a Turing machine whose tape is exactly the input plus two endmarkers — and the class of languages it accepts, the context-sensitive languages, which sit between the context-free languages and the recursive ones.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State the machine and its two endmarker conditions; simulate one that decides anbncn; count its configurations as states × head positions × tape contents; explain why that count being finite makes membership decidable while emptiness stays undecidable; generate the same language from a non-contracting grammar; and name the two problems Kuroda posed about the machine, one of which is still open.

I read the request as the rung itself, not another tour of the hierarchy: the Chomsky hierarchy owns the four grammar types and prints the configuration bound in one line, and Turing machines and undecidability owns the halting problem, reductions and Rice’s theorem. This page owns what neither does: a working bounded machine, the count computed six times and enumerated twice, the grammar checked against an independent test, the closure table, and the two LBA problems.

GATE relevance: class B

Section 6 of the verified GATE 2027 Computer Science syllabus prints, verbatim: “Regular expressions and finite automata. Context-free grammars and push-down automata. Regular and context-free languages, pumping lemma. Turing machines and undecidability.”

The words linear bounded automaton and context-sensitive are not in that clause. This page is classified B because the bounded tape is the mechanism behind “undecidability”: it is what makes the decidable side of the line decidable, and it is the class named in the statement questions that ask where a language sits. 1 — Intuition: a Turing machine with a fixed desk

A Turing machine is a person at an endless desk. A linear bounded automaton is the same person at a desk cut to the size of the sheet: the sheet, plus a paperweight at each end. They can write anywhere on the sheet, cross it as often as they like, and read what they wrote last time — but they can never put anything down outside it. Everything on this page follows from that one cut. EDUCATIONAL INTERPRETATION

The analogy is offered for the bounded tape only. It is not a proof of anything, and the proof is in section 4.

2 — Formal definition, every symbol in words

A linear bounded automaton is a seven-tuple (Q, Σ, Γ, δ, q0, qacc, qrej): Q is the finite set of states; Σ is the input alphabet; Γ is the tape alphabet, which contains Σ and two endmarkers; δ is the transition function, from a state and a tape symbol to a symbol written, a direction, and a new state; q0 is the start state; and the last two are the accepting and rejecting states.

The machine is bounded by three conditions, and all three are needed: the tape alphabet holds a left and a right endmarker; no transition may print a symbol over an endmarker; and no transition may move left of the left marker or right of the right one. VERIFIED FACT

Consequence, in one line: on a word of n letters the tape has exactly n + 2 cells, and the machine can never obtain a third. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

3 — The class it names

The languages accepted by nondeterministic linear bounded automata are exactly the context-sensitive languages, the Type 1 class of the Chomsky hierarchy. Kuroda proved this in 1964; Landweber had shown in 1963 that the deterministic machines give context-sensitive languages too. VERIFIED FACT

The grammar side says the same thing in one sentence: a language is context-sensitive exactly when some grammar generates it with no rule that makes a string shorter.

4 — Why the cut buys decidability

A configuration is the state, the tape contents and the head position. All three are finite in number once n is fixed: |Q| · (n + 2) · |Γ|n + 2. A run that has not accepted within that many moves must have repeated a configuration, and from a repeat it repeats for ever. So simulating for the count and then answering is a decision procedure. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

This is why every context-sensitive language is recursive, and why the class sits below recursive in the line rather than beside it.

5 — Worked example, in words

On aabbcc the machine makes three marks per round: one a becomes X, one b becomes Y, one c becomes Z, then the head walks back to the left marker. Two rounds give X X Y Y Z Z. A third pass walks the three blocks and accepts at the right marker. Total: 29 moves, computed.

The same machine takes 10 moves on abc, 58 on aaabbbccc and 97 on aaaabbbbcccc, and it accepts exactly the three-block words and nothing else across all 29,523 test strings.

6 — Boundaries: when the concept applies

The bounded-tape argument needs a tape whose size depends only on the input length — here exactly n + 2. A machine allowed k times n cells for a fixed constant k is the same class, by the linear speedup argument.

The argument does not apply to a machine whose tape may grow with the run, because then the configurations are infinite and the counting cut-off disappears.

Worked example 1 — aabbcc, move by move

Start: ⊢ a a b b c c ⊣, head on the first a, state q0. q0 skips X marks and turns the first unmarked a into X, then moves right into q1. q1 crosses the remaining a letters and the Y marks and stops on the first b, writing Y and entering q2. q2 crosses the b letters and the Z marks, writes Z over the first c, and enters q3. q3 has one job: move left over everything until the left endmarker, then step right into q0.

Two rounds leave ⊢ X X Y Y Z Z ⊣. The third round starts in q0, finds no unmarked a, and steps into q6, which walks right over the Y and Z blocks and accepts only if the right endmarker follows. The run is 29 moves, and the tape is 8 cells throughout. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION computed by the page’s simulator.

Worked example 2 — the count for a nine-letter word

aaabbbccc has 9 letters, so the tape is 11 cells and the two end cells are fixed. Configurations: 7 × 11 × 89 = 77 × 134,217,728 = 10,334,765,056.

The run uses 58 moves. The count is 178 million times larger than the run, which is why nobody uses the bound as an algorithm and everybody uses it as a proof.

Worked example 3 — the same language from four rules

S → abc  |  a S B c

c B → B c     b B → b b

Derivation of aabbcc: S ⇒ aSBc ⇒ aabcBc ⇒ aabBcc ⇒ aabbcc. The third rule is the context-sensitive one: two symbols on the left, so a context-free grammar cannot write it.

The exhaustive search to length 9 finds 17 sentential forms and exactly three terminal strings, and those three are exactly the words of the language of length at most 9.

Worked example 4 — the count that checks itself

For an input of 1 letter the formula gives 7 × 3 × 81 = 168. The page enumerates every (state, tape, head) triple, keeps the tapes that begin with the left marker and end with the right, and counts 168.

For an input of 2 letters the formula gives 7 × 4 × 82 = 1,792, and the enumeration agrees again. The formula is not trusted; it is checked.

Worked example 5 — a machine that never halts

Two states. p0 moves right and becomes p1; p1 moves left and becomes p0. On ⊢a⊣ it repeats its first configuration after 2 moves and oscillates for ever.

The decider runs it for 168 moves — the whole configuration count of a one-letter input — finds no accepting state, and answers reject. The run never ends; the answer still does.

Edge case 1 — the empty word

At n = 0 the tape is the two markers and nothing else: 2 cells, one tape content, so 7 × 2 × 80 = 14 configurations. The bound is still finite, so the argument survives the empty input.

What changes is the grammar side: a context-sensitive grammar may use S → ε only if S never appears on a right-hand side, which is exactly why that exception is written into the definition. VERIFIED FACT

Edge case 2 — deterministic against nondeterministic

For finite automata the two coincide; for push-down automata they do not; for linear bounded automata nobody knows. This is the first LBA problem, and it is the one collapse question in this hierarchy that is still open. VERIFIED FACT

Do not answer “they are equal” by analogy with Turing machines. For Turing machines determinism is free because the machine can simulate its own branches; a bounded machine has no room to store a branch.

Edge case 3 — a language that is decidable and not context-sensitive

List every context-sensitive grammar, and take the language L = { wi : wi is not in L(Gi) }. Membership is decidable because membership for this class is decidable, so L is recursive. If L equalled some L(Gk) then wk would be in L exactly when it is not.

So the inclusion is strict. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION The same conclusion follows from the space hierarchy theorem, which is the route to quote if a question asks for the named theorem.

Edge case 4 — a language outside even the recognisable classes

Every context-sensitive language is recursive, so every one is also recognisable. The complement of the halting language is neither, and it is the standard witness that the top of the line is not closed under complement while every class below recursive is.

The one asymmetry to keep: the class on this page is closed under complement, and the recognisable class is not.

Edge case 5 — what “linear bounded” counts

The bound is on the cells the head can reach, not on the number of moves. A bounded machine may make exponentially many moves; the run on a word of length n may visit up to |Q| · (n+2) · |Γ|n+2 configurations, and only the space is linear.

Confusing the two is the source of the claim that a bounded machine must run in linear time, which is false.

Misconception 1 — “decidable membership means decidable everything”

The wrong belief. Membership is decidable, so emptiness and equivalence must be decidable too.

Why it is wrong. Membership fixes the language and asks about one word; emptiness quantifies over every word. A finite search over configurations answers the first and cannot answer the second. For this class emptiness is undecidable (Bar-Hillel, Perles and Shamir, 1961), and equivalence, finiteness and universality fail with it.

The repair. Write the question as a pair: one object, one question. Then look the pair up in the decision table.

Misconception 2 — “bounded tape means linear time”

Linear space, not linear time. The count of configurations is exponential in n, so the obvious decider runs in exponential time; the problem is complete for polynomial space.

Repair. Say “linear space” whenever you mean the tape, and never let it imply a time bound.

Misconception 3 — “complement closure comes free from the class below”

Context-free languages are not closed under complement, and this class is. The two rows of the closure table are not inherited from one another.

Repair. Complement closure here is the second LBA problem, answered in 1988 by Immerman and Szelepcsényi. Quote it as a theorem, not as an obvious fact.

Misconception 4 — “non-contracting and context-sensitive are different classes”

They are the same class. A context-sensitive rule never shortens its string, and any non-contracting grammar can be rewritten into the αAβ → αγβ form with helper nonterminals.

Repair. Treat “non-contracting”, “Type 1”, “context-sensitive” and “accepted by a linear bounded automaton” as four names for one thing.

Misconception 5 — “a bigger class means a harder language”

Class membership is about what a machine can do with a fixed amount of tape, not about how hard a particular word is. anbncn is decided in 6n² moves; a context-free language can have a far slower parser.

Repair. Classify by the resource the machine needs, never by the size of the numbers in the definition.

The four shapes, and the procedure for each

Shape 1, classify. Count the independent equalities in the definition. Three blocks that must match need a tape that can be crossed: linear bounded. Two equalities are one stack: context-free. One equality or none: regular. This answers the classification before any pumping argument.

Shape 2, is the statement true? Two statements are used over and over, and both are true: every context-sensitive language is recursive, and there is a recursive language that is not context-sensitive. The first is the configuration bound; the second is diagonalisation.

Shape 3, which automaton? The linear bounded automaton, and the one feature to quote is that the tape is the input plus two endmarkers. The distractors are the Turing machine above and the push-down automaton below.

Shape 4, decidable or not? Name the object and the question, then read the table: membership decidable, emptiness undecidable, equivalence undecidable, finiteness undecidable, universality undecidable.

Previous-year anchor: GATE CSE 1991

The paper asked which is the strongest correct statement about a finite language over a finite alphabet, offering: it could be undecidable; it is Turing-machine recognisable; it is a context-sensitive language; it is a regular language; none of these.

The answer is that it is regular, because regular sits inside every other class on the line. The question is a shape-2 question with the line as its subject. The two slips that cost marks

First: carrying “decidable” from membership to emptiness. Second: answering “deterministic and nondeterministic are equal” because they are equal for finite automata and for Turing machines.

Both slips are repaired by naming the object and the question before writing anything down.

MCQ GATE CSE 1991

Which of the following is the strongest correct statement about a finite language over some finite alphabet Σ?

  1. It could be undecidable.
  2. It is Turing-machine recognizable.
  3. It is a context-sensitive language.
  4. It is a regular language.

Answer: (4). Options 2 and 3 are true as well, which is why the word “strongest” is doing the work: regular sits inside context-sensitive, which sits inside recognizable, so the regular statement implies the other two and is implied by neither.

A finite language is a finite list of words, and a finite list is accepted by a machine with one path per word, so it is regular. The page’s own computation checks the same idea on a small scale: a three-word language is matched by a path of three dots in scene 6.

MSQ original practice

A linear bounded automaton has 7 states and a tape alphabet of 8 symbols, and the tape is the input plus two endmarkers. Which statements are TRUE?

I. On a word of length 4 the tape has 6 cells.
II. On a word of length 4 there are 172,032 configurations.
III. A run that has not accepted after that many moves never accepts.
IV. The machine runs in time linear in the input length.

  1. I, II and III only
  2. I and IV only
  3. II, III and IV only
  4. All four

Answer: (1). I is true: 4 + 2 = 6 cells. II is true: 7 × 6 × 84 = 172,032, the same formula scene 4 computes. III is true: a repeat of a configuration is a loop, so the count is an exact cut-off.

IV is the trap. The bound is linear in space and exponential in the number of configurations; the run on a word of length 6 already takes 205 moves and the growth is quadratic here, but a general bounded machine may take exponentially many moves.

NAT original practice

A nondeterministic linear bounded automaton with one state reaches exactly 3 · 2k − 1 configurations on the input ak, counting accepting configurations. How many configurations does it reach on a4?

Answer: 47. 3 · 16 − 1 = 47, and the page’s breadth-first walk computes the same number.

The decomposition is 2k+1 − 1 running configurations, one per prefix length, plus 2k accepting ones, one per distinct tape: 31 + 16 = 47. A wrong answer of 48 usually comes from counting the start configuration twice.

MCQ original practice

Which one of the following problems about context-sensitive languages is DECIDABLE?

  1. Given a context-sensitive grammar G, is L(G) empty?
  2. Given a context-sensitive grammar G and a word w, is w in L(G)?
  3. Given two context-sensitive grammars G1 and G2, is L(G1) = L(G2)?
  4. Given a context-sensitive grammar G, is L(G) finite?

Answer: (2). Membership is decidable by the configuration bound; it is complete for polynomial space, so it is expensive but decidable.

The other three are undecidable. This is the sharpest contrast in the topic: the class is decidable word by word and undecidable language by language. Note the asymmetry with the row below, where emptiness and finiteness are decidable and equivalence is not.

MSQ original practice

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

I. Every context-sensitive language is recursive.
II. There exists a recursive language that is not context-sensitive.
III. The context-sensitive languages are closed under complement.
IV. The context-sensitive languages are closed under arbitrary homomorphism.

  1. I, II and III only
  2. I and III only
  3. II and IV only
  4. All four

Answer: (1). I follows from the configuration bound. II follows by diagonalisation, or from the space hierarchy theorem. III is the second LBA problem, answered by Immerman and Szelepcsényi in 1988.

IV is false: a homomorphism that maps a letter to the empty string can shrink a word and carry the language out of the class. Epsilon-free homomorphisms, and inverse homomorphisms, do stay inside.

MCQ original practice

A grammar has the rules S → abc, S → aSBc, cB → Bc and bB → bb. Which language does it generate?

  1. { anbncn : n ≥ 1 }
  2. { anbmck : n, m, k ≥ 1 }
  3. { anbnc2n : n ≥ 1 }
  4. { w ∈ {a, b, c}* : w contains equally many a, b and c }

Answer: (1). Computed: the exhaustive derivation to length 9 produces exactly abc, aabbcc and aaabbbccc, and an independent membership test over all 29,523 strings of length at most 9 finds exactly the same three words.

Option 4 is the near miss: equal counts alone would allow aabccb, which no derivation produces, because the sorting rule cB → Bc keeps every a before every b before every c.

MCQ original practice

The language L = { anbncn : n ≥ 1 } is placed in the hierarchy. Which statement is correct?

  1. L is context-free but not regular.
  2. L is context-sensitive but not context-free, and recursive.
  3. L is recursive but not context-sensitive.
  4. L is recursively enumerable but not recursive.

Answer: (2). The page exhibits both halves: a linear bounded automaton that decides L in 6n² moves, and a four-rule non-contracting grammar that generates it. Both put L inside the class; the pumping lemma for context-free languages puts it outside the class below.

Option 3 is the common slip: “not context-free” is not the same as “not context-sensitive”. The hierarchy has a rung between them, and L is on it.

NAT original practice

A linear bounded automaton has 3 states and a tape alphabet of 4 symbols, and the tape for a word of length n has n + 2 cells. For n = 2, how many configurations are there?

Answer: 192. 3 × 4 × 42 = 12 × 16 = 192.

The two wrong answers to expect are 48, from forgetting the head positions, and 3,072, from counting the two end cells as variable and using 44. The count is states times head positions times tape contents, and the end cells can never be written over.

The five facts to carry

1. A linear bounded automaton is a Turing machine whose tape is the input plus two endmarkers, and no transition may write over a marker or move past one.

2. It accepts exactly the context-sensitive languages (Kuroda, 1964), which are exactly the languages of non-contracting grammars.

3. Configurations = |Q| · (n+2) · |Γ|n+2. Finite, therefore membership is decidable.

4. Closed under union, intersection, complement, concatenation, star, reversal, epsilon-free homomorphism and inverse homomorphism; not closed under arbitrary homomorphism.

The decision table

membership — decidable (PSPACE-complete)

emptiness — undecidable

equivalence — undecidable

finiteness — undecidable

universality — undecidable

The two LBA problems

First: is NSPACE(n) = DSPACE(n)? Open since 1964.

Second: is the class closed under complement? Answered yes, 1988, Immerman and Szelepcsényi.

One line to carry into the exam

The tape is bounded, so the configurations are finite, so membership is decidable — and nothing else follows.
