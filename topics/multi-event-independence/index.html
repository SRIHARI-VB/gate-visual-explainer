Topic and objective

TOPIC: Independence when there are more than two events — what pairwise independence is, what mutual independence is, the space between them, and conditional independence, which is a third statement about a different sample space.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a family of three events on a small sample space, decide whether it is pairwise independent, mutually independent, neither or both, by computing every product the definition names. Count the conditions ($$2^n - n - 1$$). Say what a failure costs: the at-least-one formula stops being exact, and it errs in both directions. Decide whether two events that are independent become dependent once a third is conditioned on, and explain why.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class B — a prerequisite technique for a named clause. Section 1 of the syllabus names “Probability and Statistics: … Conditional probability and Bayes theorem”. Independence is the definition that conditional probability is built on, and the multi-event case is what a three-statement question actually tests. Two previous-year CSE items are recomputed on the GATE patterns tab, and one of them is a three-event item of exactly this shape.

PREREQUISITES: a finite sample space, an event as a subset, and probability as a count of equally likely atoms (Probability, random variables and distributions); the product rule for two events, already met there; counting subsets of a small set (Counting and combinatorics).

VISUALIZATION PLAN: eleven scenes. First independence of two events on a four-cell grid, so that the product rule is seen as a definition and not as a theorem. Then how many conditions three events impose. Then Bernstein’s four tickets, where all three pairs pass and the triple fails, drawn as four cells with the pair overlaps highlighted one at a time. Then the two-dice space, where the triple product holds and every pair fails. Then two bits and their parity, where any two determine the third. Then the at-least-one formula, checked against the truth on both spaces, where it is too big on one and too small on the other. Then conditioning on the parity bit, where an independent pair becomes dependent. Then the three boundaries: disjointness, probability zero or one, and complements. Then the exact counts over all sixteen events of a four-point space. Then the verified 2023 item, all four statements recomputed. Every number on the canvas is computed by the page from the space, and the two counterexamples are checked in both directions.

I read this topic as the multi-event case and its two neighbours, pairwise and conditional, not as a first course in probability. The two-event definition, conditional probability and Bayes are taught in the prerequisite lesson and are used here without re-derivation.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A sample space as a set of equally likely atoms, and an event as a subset of it.
  • $$P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$$, and that it needs $$P(B) > 0$$.
  • Two events are independent when $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$, and that this is the definition.
  • Counting the subsets of a small set, so that “sixteen events on four atoms” is obvious.

Helpful, not required: having seen a binomial probability, since the last scene says where the multiplication is used.

Approximate learning time: 45–60 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. Independence is a product, checked on both sides. It is never inferred from a picture or from an English description.
  2. Three events need four conditions, not three. The count is $$2^n - n - 1$$; the extra one is the triple.
  3. Pairwise does not imply mutual, and mutual does not imply pairwise. Both failures are real, and both are on this page with computed numbers.
  4. Only mutual independence licenses multiplication. The at-least-one formula is exact under it and wrong in both directions without it.
  5. Conditional independence is a statement about a smaller space. Conditioning can create dependence and can destroy it.

Intuition: two coins that do not talk

Toss a coin twice. Knowing the first toss tells you nothing about the second, and the arithmetic that says so is one line: the chance of two heads is the chance of a head, twice.

Now add a third event that is built from the first two — say, “the two tosses differ”. Each pair still looks unrelated: the first toss does not tell you whether they differ, because whatever the first toss was, the second can make them differ or not. And yet the three together are as far from unrelated as possible: from any two of them you can read the third exactly.

That gap between “every pair” and “all of them” is the whole subject. It is not a technicality: it is the difference between a formula that works and a formula that is quietly wrong.

Educational interpretation The analogy is exact about the structure — pairs carry no information, the triple carries all of it — and loose about cause, since nothing here is causal. Independence is arithmetic, not a story about influence.

Formal definitions

Two events are independent when $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$. Equivalently $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$ whenever $$P(B) > 0$$. The first form is the definition; the second is a rearrangement that fails to exist when $$P(B) = 0$$.

A family $$A_1, \dots, A_n$$ is mutually independent when, for every subset of two or more of the events, the probability of the intersection equals the product of the probabilities.

The same family is pairwise independent when only the subsets of size exactly two satisfy it.

$$\text{conditions} \;=\; 2^n - n - 1$$

There are $$2^n$$ subsets of the family. One is empty and $$n$$ are singletons, and neither imposes anything, so $$2^n - n - 1$$ product conditions remain. For two events: 1. For three: 4. For four: 11.

Conditionally independent given $$C$$ means the same product rule holds inside the conditioned space: $$P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)P(B \mid C)$$, which requires $$P(C) > 0$$.

Mathematical derivation The count is a count of subsets, so it needs no probability: $$\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k} = 2^n - \binom{n}{0} - \binom{n}{1}$$. The page checks it by enumerating subsets for $$n = 2 \ldots 7$$.

Why the conditions are separate, not nested

It is tempting to read the conditions as a ladder: check the pairs, then check the triple, and the triple is the strict one. Neither half of that is true.

Bernstein’s four tickets pass all three pairs and fail the triple. The two-dice example passes the triple and fails all three pairs. So passing one group of conditions tells you nothing about the other group.

What is true is a one-way implication: mutual independence implies pairwise independence, because the pairs are among the subsets that mutual independence quantifies over. The converse is what fails, and it is what questions are built on.

Mathematical derivation Mutual implies pairwise: the pair condition is one of the conditions in the definition, so it holds by definition. Pairwise does not imply mutual: a counterexample is required, and the page carries two.

What every number here assumes

All four spaces in this lesson are uniform: every atom has the same probability. That is why the product test can be done in whole numbers, as $$|A \cap B| \cdot |S| = |A| \cdot |B|$$, with no fractions anywhere.

On a non-uniform space the same definition holds, but the test becomes arithmetic on the actual probabilities and the counting shortcut is gone. Nothing in the definitions changes; only the computation does.

The second assumption is that the sample space is finite and small enough to enumerate. Every count on this page is exhaustive over the atoms or over the events, never sampled.

If a question gives you probabilities rather than a space, use them directly: the definition is about probabilities, and the space is only a way of computing them.

Level 1–3: the definition as a procedure

  1. Write the family. How many events, and on what space? The count of conditions follows from $$n$$.
  2. List every subset of size two or more. For three events that is four rows; for four events, eleven.
  3. Compute the intersection probability and the product, separately. Two numbers per row, from the same space.
  4. Compare. All rows equal: mutually independent. Only the pair rows equal: pairwise, not mutual. Any pair row unequal: not even pairwise.
  5. Say which word the question used. Unqualified “independent” means the full list.

The procedure is mechanical, and that is the point: the marks in this topic are lost by skipping a row, not by misunderstanding the idea.

Level 4–5: the three statements, side by side

Pairwise
$$P(A_i \cap A_j) = P(A_i)P(A_j)$$ for every pair
Mutual
the same for every subset of size two or more
Conditional
$$P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)P(B \mid C)$$

The first two are statements about one probability measure. The third is a statement about a different one, obtained by conditioning, so it cannot be read off the first two — in either direction.

A useful sanity rule: if the question contains the word given, the space has changed and the unconditional verdict is not automatically the answer.

Worked examples, page 1 of 2 — the two counterexamples

Both are computed by the page from the space itself. Every fraction below is a count over the atoms, reduced.

Example 1 — Bernstein’s four tickets

Four tickets, labelled 112, 121, 211 and 222, drawn uniformly. Let $$A_1$$ be a one in the first place, $$A_2$$ a one in the second, $$A_3$$ a one in the third.

  1. $$A_1 = \{112, 121\}$$, so $$P(A_1) = 1/2$$. The same for $$A_2$$ and $$A_3$$, each holding two of the four tickets.
  2. $$A_1 \cap A_2 = \{112\}$$, so the overlap is $$1/4$$, and $$1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4$$. This pair passes.
  3. $$A_1 \cap A_3 = \{121\}$$ and $$A_2 \cap A_3 = \{211\}$$, each $$1/4$$ against the same product. Both pass.
  4. $$A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3$$ needs a one in every place, and no ticket has three ones. So the intersection is empty, probability $$0$$, against a product of $$1/8$$.

Verdict: pairwise independent, not mutually independent. The failure is the fourth condition, and it is the only one.

The four tickets are exactly the strings with an even number of ones, which the page checks atom by atom. That is not a coincidence: it is the same object as the parity example in scene five.

Example 2 — the two-dice space

Two dice, 36 cells. $$A$$: first die 1, 2 or 3. $$B$$: first die 3, 4 or 5. $$C$$: faces sum to 9, which is $$\{3,6\}, \{4,5\}, \{5,4\}, \{6,3\}$$.

  1. $$P(A) = 18/36 = 1/2$$, $$P(B) = 1/2$$, $$P(C) = 4/36 = 1/9$$.
  2. $$A \cap B$$ is the row where the first die is 3, six cells: $$1/6$$ against a product of $$1/4$$. Fails.
  3. $$A \cap C = \{3,6\}$$, one cell: $$1/36$$ against $$1/18$$. Fails. $$B \cap C = 3$$ cells: $$1/12$$ against $$1/18$$. Fails.
  4. $$A \cap B \cap C = \{3,6\}$$, one cell: $$1/36$$, and $$1/2 \times 1/2 \times 1/9 = 1/36$$. The triple product holds.

Verdict: not pairwise independent, and yet the triple product holds. A student who checked only the triple would call this family independent.

This is why the conditions cannot be treated as a hierarchy with one strict top rung. Each subset is its own test.

Worked examples, page 2 of 2 — the two bits, and the formula

Example 3 — a parity bit

$$X$$ and $$Y$$ are fair bits, and $$Z = X \oplus Y$$ is one exactly when they differ. The four rows are 000, 011, 101, 110.

  1. Each column is one in two of the four rows, so each of $$X, Y, Z$$ has probability $$1/2$$.
  2. Any pair shares two rows: $$X$$ and $$Y$$ share 101 and 110, and so on. Each pair is $$2/4 = 1/4 = 1/2 \times 1/2$$.
  3. All three together would need the row 111, which is not in the space. Probability $$0$$ against $$1/8$$.

Knowing any two bits gives you the third: from $$X$$ and $$Y$$ you compute $$Z$$, from $$X$$ and $$Z$$ you solve for $$Y$$. So the family is maximally dependent while every pair is independent.

This is the standard counterexample in every textbook, and it is also the definition of a parity check, which is why the same arithmetic appears in error detection.

Example 4 — what the failure costs

The at-least-one formula multiplies the complements: $$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - \prod (1 - P(A_i))$$. It is derived from the product rule, so it needs mutual independence.

Four tickets, formula
$$1 - (1/2)^3 = 7/8$$
Four tickets, truth
$$3/4$$ — only 222 is outside the union
Two dice, formula
$$1 - (1/2)(1/2)(8/9) = 28/36$$
Two dice, truth
$$31/36$$ — the cell 6,3 is in $$C$$ and outside $$A$$ and $$B$$

So the pairwise version of the formula is too big on one space and too small on the other. It is not an approximation and not a bound; it is simply not the right computation.

Under mutual independence the same formula is exact, which the page checks on four mutually independent families built from cylinder events.

Example 5 — conditioning on the parity bit

Take the same three bits and condition on $$Z = 1$$. Two rows survive: 011 and 101.

  1. $$P(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) = 1/2$$, because one of the two surviving rows has $$X = 1$$.
  2. $$P(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1) = 1/2$$, for the same reason.
  3. $$P(X = 1 \text{ and } Y = 1 \mid Z = 1) = 0$$, because no surviving row has both bits one.
  4. $$0 \neq 1/4$$, so $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are dependent given $$Z$$, having been independent without it.

This is the smallest instance of explaining away: two independent causes, one observation that either could explain, and learning the observation makes them dependent.

Note the shape of the trap: the unconditional verdict was correct, and it is the answer to a different question than the one asked.

Example 6 — counting on a four-point space

Space $$\{1,2,3,4\}$$, uniform. There are sixteen events, because each atom is in or out. Enumerate every pair and every triple and test the products.

Events
16
Independent of every other event
2 — the empty event and the whole space
Ordered independent pairs
84 of 256
Unordered independent pairs
43
Pairwise-independent triples
46
Pairwise but not mutual
8, all of them triples of two-element events, out of 20 such triples
Ordered disjoint pairs, both non-empty
50, and every one is dependent

These are exhaustive counts, not estimates. On a space this small the enumeration is faster than any formula, and it is the method to reach for when a question asks how many.

Probability zero or one

If $$P(A) = 0$$ then $$P(A \cap B) = 0 = 0 \cdot P(B)$$ for every $$B$$, so an impossible event is independent of everything, including itself. If $$P(A) = 1$$ then $$P(A \cap B) = P(B) = 1 \cdot P(B)$$, so a certain event is too.

On the four-point space exactly two of the sixteen events have this property: the empty event and the whole space. Nothing else qualifies, which the page checks by testing all sixteen against all sixteen.

This is a boundary case, not a rule. It is also why a question can contain a probability of 0 or 1 and still have a determinate independence answer.

Disjoint is not independent

If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are disjoint and both have positive probability, then $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$ while $$P(A)P(B) > 0$$, so they are dependent — maximally so, in the sense that one rules out the other.

On the four-point space there are 50 ordered pairs of disjoint non-empty events, and all 50 fail the product test. The claim is exhaustive, not illustrative.

The one exception is a member with probability zero, which is the previous card. Keep the two cards together: the exception is exactly the boundary.

Complements are free

If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent then so are $$A$$ and $$B^c$$, because $$P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = P(A) - P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 - P(B))$$.

The same argument replaces either event, or both, so any independence verdict survives complementing any subset of the family. The page checks this over every independent pair on the four-point space.

Use it when a question gives you the probability of an event and asks about its complement: the answer is usually that nothing changes, and the derivation is the mark.

A nested event is always dependent

If $$A \subseteq B$$ and $$P(A) > 0$$ and $$P(B) < 1$$, then $$A$$ and $$B$$ are dependent: the intersection is $$A$$ itself, so the overlap has probability $$P(A)$$, while the product is $$P(A)P(B) < P(A)$$.

This is the fastest test in the 2023 item: the event “a head on both throws” sits inside “a head on the first throw”, so those two cannot be independent, and no arithmetic is needed.

The degenerate exceptions are $$P(A) = 0$$ and $$P(B) = 1$$, where the product does equal the overlap. Say so if a question turns on it.

Two events are a special case, and an easy one

For $$n = 2$$ the condition count is $$2^2 - 2 - 1 = 1$$: a single product, and pairwise and mutual coincide. Every distinction on this page disappears at $$n = 2$$.

That is why the prerequisite lesson can teach independence correctly with one equation, and why a three-event question needs the extra machinery. If a question has three or more events and you are checking one product, you are answering the $$n = 2$$ question.

The count grows fast: 1, 4, 11, 26, 57 for two to six events. That growth is why exam questions stop at three, and why a four-event question is usually about a structure rather than a table.

Uniformity, and what changes without it

Every space here is uniform, so the product test is integer arithmetic on counts. On a non-uniform space the definition is unchanged but the shortcut is gone: you must compute $$P(A \cap B)$$ and $$P(A)P(B)$$ as numbers.

A worked instance of the difference: on a space with atoms of probability 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8, two events of equal size can be independent or not, so counting atoms is not enough. The definition never mentions atoms.

Practical rule: if the question gives a table of probabilities, use the table. If it gives a space of equally likely outcomes, counting is legal and faster.

Six beliefs that cost marks

Each is stated as a learner would state it, then corrected with the smallest counterexample or the sharpest distinction. Every number is computed by the page.

1. “If every pair is independent, the three are independent”

Why it tempts: independence feels like a property of relationships, and there are only three relationships among three events, so checking them feels exhaustive.

Smallest refutation: tickets 112, 121, 211, 222. All three pairs are $$1/4$$ against $$1/4$$; the triple is $$0$$ against $$1/8$$.

Rule: the triple is a fourth, separate condition. Mutual independence is pairwise plus the triple, and the word “independent” without a qualifier means the whole list.

The four tickets are the standard example, due to Bernstein in 1928. It is worth memorising, because a question can be answered by naming it.

2. “If the triple product holds, the events are independent”

Why it tempts: the triple condition looks like the strongest one, so it is natural to think it dominates the pairs.

Smallest refutation: the two-dice space. $$A$$ first die in 1–3, $$B$$ first die in 3–5, $$C$$ sum 9. The triple is $$1/36 = 1/2 \cdot 1/2 \cdot 1/9$$, while $$A \cap B$$ is $$1/6$$ against $$1/4$$.

Rule: the conditions are not nested in either direction. Every subset of size two or more is its own test, and passing one says nothing about the others.

This is the rarer failure and the more dangerous one, because a solution that checks only the triple looks complete.

3. “Independent means disjoint, or means one does not cause the other”

Why it tempts: the everyday sense of “independent” is “unconnected”, and disjoint events are as unconnected as two events can be.

Smallest refutation: two disjoint events of probability $$1/2$$ each on a four-point space. The overlap is $$0$$, the product is $$1/4$$. They are dependent.

Rule: independence is a product of probabilities, nothing else. Disjointness fixes the overlap at zero, which is a product only when one of the probabilities is zero.

Causation is not part of the definition either: two events can be independent by symmetry and one can still be a physical consequence of the other, as in the parity example.

4. “If two events are independent, they stay independent given anything”

Why it tempts: conditioning feels like zooming in, and zooming in on an independent pair feels like it should preserve the relationship.

Smallest refutation: $$X, Y$$ fair bits, $$Z = X \oplus Y$$. Given $$Z = 1$$, $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are perfectly dependent: one determines the other. The joint probability is $$0$$ against a product of $$1/4$$.

Rule: conditional independence is a product rule inside a smaller space. It is a different statement, and neither it nor its negation follows from the unconditional one.

Conditioning can also create independence where there was dependence. The implication fails in both directions, which is why the word given must change your computation.

5. “One minus the product of the complements always works”

Why it tempts: it is the standard at-least-one formula, it is exact on the examples where the trials really are independent, and it is one line.

Smallest refutation: the four tickets give $$7/8$$ by the formula and $$3/4$$ in truth; the dice give $$28/36$$ by the formula and $$31/36$$ in truth.

Rule: the formula is licensed by mutual independence, because it is the product rule applied to the complements. Under pairwise independence alone it is not a bound either — it can be too big or too small.

The safe route when independence is only pairwise: compute the union by inclusion and exclusion, which never needs independence at all.

6. “The picture shows they are independent”

Why it tempts: a Venn diagram with two symmetric blobs looks independent, and symmetry usually is a sign of it.

Smallest refutation: the four tickets draw as four symmetric cells, and the family is not independent. Symmetry of the drawing is not a property of the measure.

Rule: independence is established by two computed numbers per condition, never by a diagram. A picture can only show a candidate, or show a failure.

This lesson’s animations are a case in point: they draw the cells so the counts can be read, and every caption quotes a number the model computed.

Pattern 1 — “which of these statements are true”

A family of two or three events on a small space, and four or five statements to mark. Each statement is one product, or one conditional equality, computed on the same space.

Reasoning it demands: compute every statement, do not stop at the first true one. In a multiple-select item there are usually two.

Common wrong approach: judging by containment. If one event sits inside another, they are dependent — that is a shortcut, and it is the only one in this topic that is safe.

Time budget: on a four-cell space this is under a minute if the space is drawn first.

Pattern 2 — independence by symmetry or by counting

A uniform space described combinatorially — permutations, strings, random selections — with two events named, and the question asks whether they are independent.

Reasoning it demands: count the space, count each event, count the intersection. Three counts, then one comparison.

Common wrong approach: arguing that the events “cannot affect each other”. That is the right intuition and not a proof; the counts settle it.

The 2024 Set 1 item below is exactly this shape: 24 permutations, 12, 12 and 6.

Pattern 3 — the conditional that looks independent

A conditional probability is given or asked, and the tempting answer is the unconditional one. The question turns on whether the conditioning event is independent of the event being conditioned on.

Reasoning it demands: recompute inside the conditioned space. Shrink the sample space, renormalise, then compare.

Common wrong approach: quoting $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$ from memory. It is true exactly when the pair is independent, which is the thing being asked.

If the conditioning event is a function of the other two, as a parity bit is, expect dependence.

Pattern 4 — “how many” over a small space

A small space, a property of pairs or triples of events, and a count. The answer is an integer, often a NAT.

Reasoning it demands: enumerate the events, test the property, count. There is no formula worth deriving at this size.

Common wrong approach: counting unordered pairs when the question counts ordered ones, or forgetting that the empty event and the whole space always qualify.

On four atoms: 84 ordered independent pairs, 43 unordered, 8 pairwise-but-not-mutual triples out of 20.

GATE CSE 2023 — two coins, three events

Consider a random experiment where two fair coins are tossed. Let A be the event that denotes HEAD on both the throws, B be the event that denotes HEAD on the first throw, and C be the event that denotes HEAD on the second throw. Which of the following statements is/are TRUE?

(A) A and B are independent   (B) A and C are independent   (C) B and C are independent   (D) $$P(B \mid C) = P(B)$$

  1. The space is HH, HT, TH, TT. $$A = \{HH\}$$, $$B = \{HH, HT\}$$, $$C = \{HH, TH\}$$.
  2. $$A \cap B = \{HH\}$$, so $$1/4$$ against $$P(A)P(B) = 1/8$$. (A) is false. By the same computation, (B) is false.
  3. $$B \cap C = \{HH\}$$, so $$1/4$$ against $$1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/4$$. (C) is true.
  4. $$P(B \mid C) = (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2 = P(B)$$. (D) is true.

Answer: (C) and (D). The shortcut for the false pair: $$A$$ sits inside both $$B$$ and $$C$$, and a nested event of positive probability is dependent on its container.

Note that (C) and (D) are the same arithmetic written two ways, which is why a multiple-select item so often has two true options.

GATE CSE 2024, Set 1 — two events on a permutation

Consider a permutation sampled uniformly at random from the set of all permutations of $$\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$$ for some $$n \ge 4$$. Let X be the event that 1 occurs before 2 in the permutation, and Y the event that 3 occurs before 4. Which one of the following statements is TRUE?

(A) X and Y are mutually exclusive   (B) X and Y are independent   (C) either X or Y must occur   (D) X is more likely than Y

  1. For $$n = 4$$ the space has $$24$$ permutations; the same argument works for every $$n \ge 4$$ by symmetry.
  2. $$|X| = 12$$ and $$|Y| = 12$$, each by symmetry: exactly one of the two orders of the pair occurs.
  3. $$|X \cap Y| = 6$$, computed by enumerating all 24 permutations. So $$6/24 = 1/4 = 1/2 \cdot 1/2$$.
  4. They are not mutually exclusive, since 6 permutations satisfy both; and neither is more likely.

Answer: (B). The general form is the useful one: with $$n$$ elements the counts are $$n!/2$$, $$n!/2$$ and $$n!/4$$, so the pair is independent for every $$n \ge 4$$.

This is pattern 2: three counts and one comparison. The tempting answer (A) confuses independence with disjointness.

MCQ original

Q1 — the four tickets

Tickets 112, 121, 211, 222 are drawn uniformly. $$A_1$$ is a one in the first place, $$A_2$$ in the second, $$A_3$$ in the third. Which statement is TRUE?

(a) the three events are mutually independent   (b) pairwise independent but not mutually independent   (c) not pairwise independent   (d) $$A_1$$ and $$A_2$$ are dependent

Answer: (b)

Each pair meets in exactly one ticket: $$A_1 \cap A_2 = \{112\}$$, $$A_1 \cap A_3 = \{121\}$$, $$A_2 \cap A_3 = \{211\}$$. Each overlap is $$1/4$$ and each product is $$1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/4$$, so all three pairs pass.

The triple would need a ticket with three ones, and there is none: $$0$$ against $$1/8$$. So (a) fails at the fourth condition and (c) fails at the first three.

(d) is refuted by the first computation. The space is the four strings with an even number of ones.

Q2 — three bits

$$X$$ and $$Y$$ are fair bits and $$Z = X \oplus Y$$. Which statements are TRUE?

(a) $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are independent   (b) $$X, Y, Z$$ are mutually independent   (c) $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are independent given $$Z = 1$$   (d) $$P(Z = 1) = 1/2$$

Answer: (a) and (d)

(a) $$X \cap Y$$ is one of the four rows, $$1/4 = 1/2 \cdot 1/2$$. (d) $$Z$$ is one in two of the four rows, so $$1/2$$.

(b) fails because $$X \cap Y \cap Z$$ is empty: $$0 \neq 1/8$$. (c) fails because given $$Z = 1$$ the two bits differ, so $$P(X = 1, Y = 1 \mid Z = 1) = 0$$ while the product of the conditionals is $$1/4$$.

Both wrong options are the interesting ones: they are the pairwise-versus-mutual trap and the conditional trap, in one question.

Q3 — counting the conditions

Five events are to be checked for mutual independence. The number of product conditions that must hold is ______.

Answer: 26

$$2^5 - 5 - 1 = 32 - 6 = 26$$. By subset size: 10 pairs, 10 triples, 5 quadruples, 1 quintuple.

The common wrong answer is 10, which counts only the pairs, or 31, which forgets to remove the five singletons.

MCQ original

Q4 — disjoint with positive probability

$$A$$ and $$B$$ are disjoint, with $$P(A) = 0.4$$ and $$P(B) = 0.3$$. Which is TRUE?

(a) independent   (b) dependent   (c) independent if the space is uniform   (d) cannot be decided

Answer: (b) dependent

Disjoint means $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$. The product is $$0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.12$$. Zero is not $$0.12$$, so they are dependent.

(d) is the trap for anyone who thinks a space is needed: the probabilities alone settle it. (c) is refuted because the verdict came from the two given probabilities.

Q5 — the at-least-one formula

$$A, B, C$$ are pairwise independent, each with probability $$1/2$$, and $$P(A \cap B \cap C) = 0$$. Which are TRUE?

(a) $$P(A \cup B \cup C) = 7/8$$   (b) the three are mutually independent   (c) $$P(A \cup B \cup C) = 3/4$$   (d) the complement formula is not licensed here

Answer: (c) and (d)

Inclusion and exclusion: $$3(1/2) - 3(1/4) + 0 = 3/2 - 3/4 = 3/4$$. That computation never uses independence, which is why it is safe.

(a) is the complement formula, which needs mutual independence: $$1 - (1/2)^3 = 7/8$$, and $$7/8 \neq 3/4$$. (b) fails on the fourth condition, exactly the numbers given.

These are precisely the numbers of the four-ticket space, so the question is that counterexample with the labels removed.

Q6 — independent pairs on four points

Let the space be $$\{1,2,3,4\}$$, uniform, and consider all 16 events. The number of ordered pairs $$(A, B)$$ of events that are independent is ______.

Answer: 84

Enumerate all 256 ordered pairs and test $$|A \cap B| \cdot 4 = |A| \cdot |B|$$. The page does exactly this and reports 84.

Sanity check by size: every pair with $$A$$ empty or full qualifies, which is $$16 + 16 - 1 = 31$$ ordered pairs on its own, before any of the real coincidences are counted.

43 is the unordered count, which is the common wrong answer to a question that says ordered.

MCQ original

Q7 — a nested pair

$$A \subseteq B$$ with $$P(A) = 0.2$$ and $$P(B) = 0.5$$. Which is TRUE?

(a) independent   (b) dependent   (c) independent only if $$A$$ is empty   (d) depends on the sample space

Answer: (b) dependent

$$A \subseteq B$$ forces $$A \cap B = A$$, so the overlap is $$0.2$$ while the product is $$0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1$$.

(c) is nearly right and worth reading twice: an empty $$A$$ does give independence, but it has probability zero, not 0.2. With the probabilities given, nothing else can rescue it.

Q8 — complements and consequences

$$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent with $$P(A) = 0.6$$ and $$P(B) = 0.5$$. Which are TRUE?

(a) $$A$$ and $$B^c$$ are independent   (b) $$P(A \cap B^c) = 0.3$$   (c) $$A^c$$ and $$B^c$$ are independent   (d) $$P(A \cup B) = 0.8$$

Answer: (a), (b), (c) and (d)

$$P(A \cap B) = 0.3$$. Then $$P(A \cap B^c) = 0.6 - 0.3 = 0.3$$, which is $$0.6 \times 0.5$$, so (a) and (b) hold.

Complementing both preserves independence by applying the rule twice. Union: $$0.6 + 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.8$$.

All four are true, which is the point: complementing is free. A question with one false option usually has it in the union arithmetic.

The procedure in one place

  1. Count the events. That fixes how many conditions there are.
  2. List every subset of size two or more. Not just the pairs, not just the triple.
  3. Compute both sides. Intersection probability on the left, product on the right, from the same space.
  4. Name the verdict. All rows pass: mutual. Pairs only: pairwise, not mutual. Any pair row fails: not pairwise.
  5. If the word given appears, shrink the space first and redo the rows inside it.

The last step is the one that separates a correct answer from a familiar-looking wrong one.

These five cover most of what a multiple-select item offers as a distractor, and each one is a one-line argument rather than a computation.

The unsafe shortcut is the one to avoid: assuming that a passing pair test, or a passing triple test, settles the whole family.

What to say in one sentence

Independence of more than two events is a list of product conditions — every subset of size two or more — and pairwise independence is the shorter list that questions offer as a distractor because it sounds the same.

If you remember one number: four tickets, three passing pairs, one failing triple, union three quarters rather than seven eighths.
