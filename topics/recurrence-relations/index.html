Topic and objective

TOPIC: Recurrence relations — the two halves of a recurrence (base cases and the rule); setting one up from a counting constraint by conditioning on the last symbol; solving a first-order recurrence by unrolling and telescoping; second-order linear homogeneous recurrences with constant coefficients and their characteristic equation; distinct roots, repeated roots and the extra factor of n; non-homogeneous recurrences and the change of variable that removes an exponential forcing term; divide-and-conquer recurrences, the recursion tree and the master theorem with its three cases and the gap between them; and the verification of a closed form by substitution or induction.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a recurrence in any of those shapes, name its type, choose the one method that applies, carry it through to a closed form or a tight bound, and check the answer against the base cases. In particular: read a counting condition and produce the recurrence; unroll a first-order recurrence into a sum; solve a constant-coefficient recurrence from its roots and then solve for the coefficients; recognise when the master theorem is silent instead of forcing a case; and tell a tight bound from a mere upper bound.

I read this as the syllabus item "Combinatorics: counting, recurrence relations, generating functions" cut at the words recurrence relations. Generating functions are a separate entry in the tracker and are not taught here, though the two subjects share the same sequences. This lesson builds directly on Counting and Combinatorics (the sum and product rules, which are how every recurrence here is derived) and on Functions and Their Properties (a sequence is a function from the natural numbers). The divide-and-conquer half is the same machinery that later drives the algorithms lessons, so it is treated here as mathematics rather than as code.

Prerequisites

  • Counting and combinatorics: the sum rule and the product rule, because every recurrence in this lesson is derived by splitting the objects into disjoint cases. The bit-string derivation in scene 2 is that split, written in symbols.
  • Functions, sequences and indices: a sequence is a function whose inputs are whole numbers, and an is the value at input n. The subscript is an index, never an exponent.
  • Sum formulas: 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2, and 12 + 22 + ... + n2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6. Unrolling produces these sums and you are expected to recognise them.
  • Quadratic equations: the roots of r2 − c1r − c2 = 0, including the discriminant test for a repeated root.
  • Logarithms base two: log n is the number of times you can halve n before reaching one. The recursion tree in scene 7 counts exactly that.
  • Nothing from probability, calculus or programming is assumed. Mathematical induction is used at the end of the lesson as the verification step, but it is not required to follow the methods.

The five ideas

  1. A recurrence is a rule plus base cases. The rule says how to move one step; the base cases say where you start. Either one alone determines nothing.
  2. First order means unroll: write the rule out until the base case, cancel what cancels, and you are left with a sum you already know.
  3. Constant coefficients mean roots: substitute rn, solve the characteristic equation, then use the base cases to find the coefficients — and look at the coefficients, because one of them can be zero.
  4. A repeated root costs you a solution, and the repair is to multiply the second copy by n. That is where every "n appears" rule in this unit comes from.
  5. Divide and conquer means compare: the forcing term against the number of leaves. The comparison decides the answer, and when the forcing sits between two cases the theorem says nothing at all.

Conventions used throughout

Verified fact Symbols: n is the input size; an and T(n) are the term at position n, and the two notations are used interchangeably because a sequence is a function on the whole numbers. Order is how far back the rule reaches: an = an−1 + 3 is first order, an = an−1 + an−2 is second order. Linear means no term is squared and no two terms are multiplied. Homogeneous means no added term free of a. Constant coefficients means the multipliers do not depend on n. log always means log base two in this lesson, written log2 where the base matters. Θ is a tight bound, O an upper bound only.

Intuition first: a staircase you describe instead of climb

Suppose someone asks how high the hundredth step of a staircase is, and tells you only this: each step is fifteen centimetres above the one before, and the first step is twenty centimetres high. You do not need a formula for the height at step n. You can walk the description forward. But if the question is "how high is step one thousand", walking is slow, so you want the description turned into a formula: twenty plus fifteen times n minus one.

That is the whole subject. A recurrence is the description; a closed form is the formula. Four things make the walk harder than the staircase, and each one gets a method in this lesson: the step may add a growing amount instead of a constant (unrolling into a sum); the step may refer back two places, and then you need the characteristic equation; the step may be described by a rule that halves the problem, so the walk becomes a tree; and the step may multiply by something that grows, which is where the master theorem lives.

Level 1 — the formal definition

Verified fact A recurrence relation for a sequence a0, a1, a2, … is an equation that expresses an in terms of some of the earlier terms a0, …, an−1, valid for all n from some starting point n0 onwards. The initial conditions (or base cases) are the values given outright. Together they determine the sequence uniquely: the rule plus the base cases produce an₀, then an₀+1, and so on, with no choice at any step.

Derivation Uniqueness. Suppose two sequences satisfy the same rule and the same base cases. They agree on the base cases. If they agree on all terms up to n−1, the rule forces agreement at n. By induction they agree everywhere. This is why a solution found by any legitimate method is the solution, and why checking a closed form on the base cases plus one general substitution is enough.

Level 2 — the four labels that choose the method

Every recurrence in this unit carries four labels. They are not decoration: each one rules methods in or out.

Order
How far back the rule reaches. Order 1 → unroll. Order k with constant coefficients → characteristic equation.
Linear or not
Linear (no a-term squared, no two a-terms multiplied) → the root method works. Non-linear, e.g. an = an−1·an−2, → substitution or taking logarithms.
Homogeneous or not
Homogeneous → the characteristic equation alone. Non-homogeneous (an added term with no a in it) → homogeneous solution plus a particular solution, or a change of variable.
Constant or variable coefficients
Constant → characteristic equation. Variable, e.g. n·an = (n−1)an−1 + 1 → divide through, or substitute, until the coefficients become constant.

Level 3 — unrolling, and why it always works at first order

Derivation Take an = an−1 + g(n) with a1 given. Substitute repeatedly:

an = an−1 + g(n) = an−2 + g(n−1) + g(n) = … = a1 + Σk=2..n g(k).

The a-terms cancel in pairs because each line's left side reappears on the right of the next. So a first-order recurrence is a summation, and the only skill left is recognising the sum. If the recurrence is an = c·an−1 + g(n) with c ≠ 1, the same unrolling gives a1cn−1 + Σ cn−kg(k) — a geometric factor in front of every term. When g(n) is a polynomial and c ≠ 1, dividing by cn first (scene 6) turns it back into the c = 1 case.

Level 4 — the characteristic equation

Verified fact For an = c1an−1 + c2an−2 + … + ckan−k with constants ci and ck ≠ 0, the characteristic equation is

rk − c1rk−1 − c2rk−2 − … − ck = 0.

Derivation Substituting an = rn and dividing by rn−k gives exactly that polynomial. Because the recurrence is linear and homogeneous, any sum of solutions is a solution, so if the roots r1, …, rk are distinct the general solution is A1r1n + … + Akrkn, and the k base cases give k linear equations in the k unknowns Ai.

Repeated root. If a root r has multiplicity m, its contribution is (A1 + A2n + … + Amnm−1)rn — a polynomial of degree m−1 in n, times rn. A simple root is the case m = 1, which is why it carries no n.

Level 5 — non-homogeneous recurrences

Verified fact For an = c1an−1 + … + ckan−k + F(n) with F(n) not identically zero, every solution is an = (homogeneous solution) + (one particular solution). The base cases are applied to the sum, never to the two parts separately.

The guessing rule. If F(n) is a polynomial of degree d, guess a polynomial of degree d; if 1 is a root of the characteristic equation with multiplicity s, multiply the guess by ns. If F(n) is C·bn, guess A·bn; if b is a root with multiplicity s, multiply by ns.

Educational interpretation The multiplier ns is the same idea as the repeated root in Level 4, and for the same reason: if the forcing term has the same shape as a homogeneous solution, the naive guess is already a solution of the homogeneous equation and cannot also solve the non-homogeneous one, so it must be raised by a power of n.

Level 6 — divide and conquer: the recursion tree

Verified fact For T(n) = a·T(n/b) + f(n) with a ≥ 1 and b > 1, draw a tree: the root carries cost f(n) and has a children of size n/b; each child carries f(n/b), and so on until the problem size reaches 1. The total is the sum of the level costs.

Derivation Level j holds aj nodes of size n/bj, so its cost is ajf(n/bj). With f(n) = n and b = 2, that is 2j·(n/2j) = n for every j — a constant per level. The number of levels is logbn, so the total is n·logbn plus the leaves.

Educational interpretation The tree is a way of seeing where the cost lives: at the leaves when a is large, spread evenly when a·f(n/b) = f(n), at the root when f grows fast. The master theorem is that observation turned into a theorem with conditions attached.

Level 7 — the master theorem, with its assumptions

Verified fact Let T(n) = a·T(n/b) + f(n), with a ≥ 1, b > 1, and f asymptotically positive. Let w = logba, so nw is the number of leaves.

  • Case 1. If f(n) = O(nw−ε) for some ε > 0, then T(n) = Θ(nw). The leaves dominate.
  • Case 2. If f(n) = Θ(nw), then T(n) = Θ(nw log n).
  • Case 3. If f(n) = Ω(nw+ε) for some ε > 0 and a·f(n/b) ≤ c·f(n) for some c < 1 and all large n, then T(n) = Θ(f(n)). The root dominates.

The ε is not decoration. If f(n) is larger than nw only by a factor of log n, no ε exists and no case applies; that is the gap, and scene 8 works the example T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log n, whose answer is Θ(n log2n).

Six worked examples, one per method

Each example below is a complete chain from the recurrence to the answer, with the arithmetic shown. The first two are previous-year questions whose paper and question number are recorded in the GATE patterns tab; the rest are worked here for the method, and every number was recomputed by iterating the recurrence on this page.

Example 1 — set one up (2016 Set 1, Q2)

Let an be the number of n-bit strings with no two consecutive 1s. Find a recurrence.

Step 1. Split by the last bit. Every valid string either ends in 0 or ends in 1, and no string does both, so the sum rule applies with no correction.

Step 2. Ends in 0: delete that 0 and a valid string of length n−1 remains, and every such string extends. Count an−1.

Step 3. Ends in 1: the previous bit must be 0. Delete the ending 01 and a valid string of length n−2 remains. Count an−2.

Step 4. Base cases: a1 = 2 ("0", "1"), a2 = 3 ("00", "01", "10").

Answer. an = an−1 + an−2. The sequence is 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21 — the Fibonacci numbers shifted by two places.

Example 2 — unroll a polynomial forcing (2016 Set 1, Q27)

a1 = 8, an = 6n2 + 2n + an−1. Find K where a99 = K × 104.

Step 1. Unroll: a99 = a1 + Σn=2..99(6n2 + 2n).

Step 2. Σn=1..99n2 = 99·100·199/6 = 328 350, so Σn=2..99n2 = 328 349.

Step 3. Σn=1..99n = 99·100/2 = 4 950, so Σn=2..99n = 4 949.

Step 4. a99 = 8 + 6(328 349) + 2(4 949) = 8 + 1 970 094 + 9 898 = 1 980 000.

Answer. K = 198. Derivation The closed form is an = 2n(n+1)2 − 8 + 8 = 2n(n+1)2, and at n = 99 that is 2·99·10 000 = 1 980 000.

Example 3 — characteristic roots, and a vanishing coefficient (2024 Set 2, Q15)

T(0) = 1, T(1) = 2, T(n) = 5T(n−1) − 6T(n−2). Which bound holds?

Step 1. Characteristic equation: r2 − 5r + 6 = 0, so (r−2)(r−3) = 0 and the roots are 2 and 3.

Step 2. General solution T(n) = A·2n + B·3n.

Step 3. Base cases: A + B = 1, and 2A + 3B = 2. Subtract twice the first from the second: B = 0, hence A = 1.

Step 4. So T(n) = 2n exactly. Check: T(2) = 5·2 − 6·1 = 4 = 22; T(3) = 5·4 − 6·2 = 8 = 23.

Answer. Θ(2n) — the official key. The larger root 3 exists but its coefficient is zero, so it contributes nothing. Reading the largest root off the characteristic equation and stopping would give Θ(3n), which is wrong.

Example 4 — repeated root

T(0) = 1, T(1) = 4, T(n) = 4T(n−1) − 4T(n−2). Find T(5).

Step 1. r2 − 4r + 4 = (r−2)2, a double root at 2.

Step 2. General solution T(n) = (A + Bn)·2n.

Step 3. T(0) = A = 1. T(1) = (A + B)·2 = 4, so A + B = 2 and B = 1.

Step 4. T(n) = (1 + n)·2n. Iterating gives 1, 4, 12, 32, 80, 192.

Answer. T(5) = 6·32 = 192, which matches the iteration.

Example 5 — change of variable (2025 Set 1)

T(0) = 1, T(n) = 2T(n−1) + n·2n for n > 0. Which bound holds?

Step 1. Divide by 2n: T(n)/2n = T(n−1)/2n−1 + n.

Step 2. Let S(n) = T(n)/2n. Then S(n) = S(n−1) + n, with S(0) = 1.

Step 3. Unroll: S(n) = 1 + (1 + 2 + … + n) = 1 + n(n+1)/2.

Step 4. So T(n) = 2n(1 + n(n+1)/2). Values: 1, 4, 16, 56, 176, 512.

Answer. Θ(n22n), option A on that paper. The first three values look like 4n; the fourth, 56 against 64, already shows they are not.

Example 6 — the tree and the gap

(a) T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n with T(1) = 1. (b) T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log n with T(1) = 0.

(a) Step 1. Level j has 2j nodes of size n/2j, each costing n/2j, so every level costs n. Step 2. There are log n levels plus a leaf level costing n. Step 3. T(n) = n log n + n, so Θ(n log n). Check at n = 8: 8·3 + 8 = 32, and iterating gives 32.

(b) Step 1. Now level j costs 2j·(n/2j)·log(n/2j) = n(log n − j). Step 2. Summing j = 0 to log n − 1 gives n·(log n)(log n + 1)/2. Step 3. That is Θ(n log2n). Check at n = 8: 8·3·4/2 = 48, and iterating gives 48.

Why (b) is not a master-theorem question. The watershed here is nlog₂2 = n and the forcing is n log n, which exceeds it by a factor of log n — no ε exists, so neither case 1 nor case 3 applies. The tree is the method, and the answer is strictly between n log n and n1+ε.

Where the standard methods stop applying

Every method in this lesson has a precondition. Below are the preconditions and the case that breaks each one, with what to do instead. These are the boundary cases a GATE question is most likely to sit on, because they separate candidates who know the method from candidates who know its conditions.

Edge 1 — the base cases are part of the answer

an = 2an−1 with a0 = 1 gives 1, 2, 4, 8, … = 2n. With a0 = 3 it gives 3, 6, 12, … = 3·2n−1. Same rule, different sequence.

What survives: the Θ class. Both are Θ(2n). What does not: the exact value. So a question asking for a number needs the base cases solved for; a question asking for a bound usually does not.

Edge 2 — a is 1 in a divide-and-conquer recurrence

T(n) = T(n/2) + 1: here w = log21 = 0, the watershed is n0 = 1, and f(n) = 1 = Θ(1), so case 2 gives Θ(log n). Iterating confirms it: the recursion stops after log n halvings.

With T(n) = T(n/2) + n, case 3 applies (f = n = Ω(n0+1), and T(n/2) ≤ cn/2 with c = 1/2), giving Θ(n). Same a, different forcing, different answer.

Edge 3 — n is not a power of two

Real inputs are not powers of two, so the recursion sees ⌊n/2⌋ and ⌈n/2⌉. The level sums are then not exactly n but are bounded above and below by constant multiples of n, which is all Θ needs.

What breaks: an exact closed form like n log2n + n. That formula is exact only for powers of two. The Θ class holds for every n, and the class is what GATE asks for.

Edge 4 — b must exceed 1

T(n) = 2T(n−1) + 1 is not T(n) = 2T(n/b) + f(n) with b > 1; the argument is reduced by subtraction, not division. The master theorem says nothing about it.

It is solved by unrolling: T(n) = 2nT(0) + (2n − 1) = Θ(2n). Substitution, not the theorem. This is the most common misapplication in the whole topic.

Edge 5 — a root of zero

an = 3an−1 − 2an−2 has characteristic equation r2 − 3r + 2 = 0, roots 1 and 2. But an = an−1 + 0·an−2 written as second order has equation r2 − r = r(r−1), with a root at 0.

A zero root contributes 0n, which is 1 at n = 0 and 0 afterwards — it affects the n = 0 term only. It is legal, and the general solution formula still holds; it is simply that the term disappears for n ≥ 1.

Edge 6 — multiplicity three and higher

an = 6an−1 − 12an−2 + 8an−3 has equation (r−2)3 = 0, a root of multiplicity three at 2.

The general solution is (A + Bn + Cn2)·2n — a quadratic factor, not a linear one. The rule is degree = multiplicity − 1, so a triple root needs three constants to match three base cases. Writing only (A + Bn)2n leaves the system unsolvable.

Edge 7 — negative coefficients and oscillation

an = −an−1 has root r = −1 and solution A·(−1)n: the sequence alternates. an = an−1 − an−2 has roots that are complex, and the solution oscillates with bounded amplitude.

What this shows: a recurrence whose terms stay small can have roots of magnitude greater than one, if the coefficients cancel. This is exactly the 2024 Set 2 phenomenon in its extreme form — the root does not decide the growth, the coefficient does.

Edge 8 — the forcing equals a homogeneous solution

an = 2an−1 + 2n. The naive guess A·2n is already a homogeneous solution, so it cannot work; the correct guess is A·n·2n.

Unrolling gives the same answer without guessing: an = 2na0 + n·2n = Θ(n2n), not Θ(2n). Forgetting the n here is the non-homogeneous twin of the repeated-root trap.

Six beliefs that cost marks, each with the case that breaks it

Each card states the belief as a candidate would hold it, gives the smallest counterexample, and says what to do instead. The counterexamples are computed on this page: the check list on the Animation tab verifies every number quoted here.

1. "The largest root decides the growth"

Belief. Solve the characteristic equation, take the biggest root, write Θ(rn).

Counterexample. T(n) = 5T(n−1) − 6T(n−2), T(0) = 1, T(1) = 2. Roots 2 and 3, but the solution is exactly 2n: the coefficient of 3n is zero. The official key for GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Question 15 is Θ(2n), not Θ(3n).

Instead. Always solve the linear system for the coefficients. The roots list the candidate shapes; only a non-zero coefficient puts a shape into the answer.

2. "Three values tell you the pattern"

Belief. T(0) = 1, T(1) = 4, T(2) = 16, so the answer is 4n.

Counterexample. The recurrence T(n) = 2T(n−1) + n·2n gives 1, 4, 16, 56, 176. The fourth value is 56, not 64, and the true class is Θ(n22n) — this is GATE CSE 2025 Set 1.

Instead. Small values are for checking a derived formula, never for deriving it. Unroll, or substitute, or use the tree; then check the result on small n.

3. "The master theorem always answers"

Belief. If f(n) looks bigger than nlogba, use case 3.

Counterexample. T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log n. The watershed is n, the forcing is n log n, which is bigger but not by a polynomial factor, so no ε exists and no case applies. The answer, from the tree, is Θ(n log2n).

Instead. Compute w = logba, then check whether f(n)/nw is bounded by n−ε, constant, or n+ε. If the ratio is a logarithm, the theorem is silent.

4. "A repeated root changes nothing"

Belief. A double root at 2 still gives A·2n.

Counterexample. T(n) = 4T(n−1) − 4T(n−2), T(0) = 1, T(1) = 4 gives 1, 4, 12, 32, 80. That is (1+n)·2n; no single constant times 2n fits, because 1 and 4 are not in the ratio 2 to 1.

Instead. Count the multiplicity and write the polynomial factor of degree multiplicity − 1. Two base cases need two constants; a double root plus one constant leaves the system with no solution.

5. "Big O and Theta are interchangeable"

Belief. If the answer is Θ(n log n), then saying O(n log n) or O(n2) is just as good.

Counterexample. 2n is O(3n) but not Θ(3n): the ratio (2/3)n tends to zero, so no positive constant bounds it from below. Both statements are true, but only one is tight.

Instead. Read the option list first. If a Θ option is present and true, that is the intended answer; O is the fallback only when the question asks explicitly for an upper bound.

6. "Θ(2n) means the values are 2n"

Belief. A recurrence whose answer is Θ(2n) must take the value 2n at n = 0, so a base case of 1 rules that option out.

Counterexample. T(n) = 2T(n−1) + 1 with T(0) = 4 has closed form 5·2n − 1, whose value at n = 0 is 4, not 1. It is still Θ(2n). The Θ class describes growth, not any particular value.

Instead. Test an asymptotic option with a large n and a ratio, never with the base case. Base cases are for the exact closed form.

How this topic is actually asked

Two question shapes dominate. Shape one gives a counting condition and asks for the recurrence — no solving, just the case split (2016 Set 1 Q2, 2015 Set 1 Q49). Shape two gives a recurrence and asks for its class among four Θ options (2024 Set 2 Q15, 2025 Set 1). A third, older shape asks for the exact closed form as a formula or a number (2004 Q45, 2016 Set 1 Q27). The marks are almost always 1 or 2, and the work is short once the type is named.

The recurrence questions in the Algorithms section of the paper and the ones in Discrete Mathematics are the same mathematics. Nothing in the syllabus separates them, and the same methods answer both.

The decision table

an = an−1 + g(n)
Unroll; the answer is a sum. Recognise Σk and Σk2.
an = c·an−1 + g(n), c ≠ 1
Divide by cn first, then unroll. If g is exponential with the same base, the n appears.
Linear, homogeneous, constant coefficients
Characteristic equation, roots, then solve for the coefficients. Watch multiplicity and zero coefficients.
Non-homogeneous with polynomial or exponential forcing
Homogeneous solution plus a particular solution; multiply the guess by ns if the forcing matches a root.
T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n), a ≥ 1, b > 1
Compare f with nlogba. Three cases; if the ratio is a logarithm, no case applies — use the tree.
Division inside the argument, unusual forcing
Substitute, e.g. n = 2m for T(√n) forms, or divide by the exponential.

The three things that are actually being tested

  1. Can you name the type? Most wrong answers come from using the wrong method — the master theorem on a subtract-one recurrence, or a characteristic equation on a non-constant-coefficient one.
  2. Do you finish the algebra? The 2024 and 2025 questions both punish stopping early: one at the roots, the other at three sample values.
  3. Do you know the definitions? Θ against O, and the meaning of "homogeneous", decide several options without any calculation.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q2 1 mark

Let an be the number of n-bit strings that do not contain two consecutive 1s. Which one of the following is the recurrence relation for an?

(A) an = an−1 + an−2 + 2n−2   (B) an = an−1 + an−2
(C) an = 2an−1 + an−2   (D) an = 2an−1 + 2an−2

Answer: (B) an = an−1 + an−2. Split by the last bit. Ends in 0: delete it, a valid (n−1)-bit string remains — an−1. Ends in 1: the bit before must be 0, delete the pair 01, a valid (n−2)-bit string remains — an−2. The two cases are disjoint, so they add.

Why the others fail. (A) adds 2n−2, which belongs to the containing version of this question (2015 Set 1, Q49), not this one. (C) and (D) multiply a term by 2, which would double-count: appending a 0 to a string already counted does not create a second string.

Check. a1 = 2, a2 = 3, so a3 = 5. Listing the 3-bit strings with no 11 gives 000, 001, 010, 100, 101 — five. The page enumerates this for n up to 14 and the rule holds throughout.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q27 2 marks

Consider the recurrence relation a1 = 8, an = 6n2 + 2n + an−1. Let a99 = K × 104. The value of K is ______.

Answer: 198. Unroll: a99 = 8 + Σn=2..99(6n2 + 2n) = 8 + 6(328 349) + 2(4 949) = 1 980 000, so K = 198.

The closed form. Σk=1..n(6k2 + 2k) = n(n+1)(2n+1) + n(n+1) = 2n(n+1)2. With the base 8 removed and re-added, an = 2n(n+1)2 exactly, and 2·99·10 000 = 1 980 000.

Traps. Starting the sum at n = 1 instead of n = 2 adds a spurious 8 and shifts the answer. Reporting 1 980 000 instead of 198 ignores the ×104 in the question. Both are checked here: the page sums term by term and evaluates the closed form.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q15 1 mark

Let T(n) be defined by T(0) = 1, T(1) = 2, and T(n) = 5T(n−1) − 6T(n−2) for n ≥ 2. Which one of the following statements is TRUE?

(A) T(n) = Θ(2n)   (B) T(n) = Θ(n2n)   (C) T(n) = Θ(3n)   (D) T(n) = Θ(n3n)

Answer: (A) Θ(2n). Characteristic equation r2 − 5r + 6 = 0 gives r = 2 and r = 3, so T(n) = A·2n + B·3n. From T(0) = 1: A + B = 1. From T(1) = 2: 2A + 3B = 2. Subtracting twice the first gives B = 0, so A = 1 and T(n) = 2n exactly.

Why (C) is the popular wrong answer. Three is the larger root, and choosing it requires no algebra. But a root with coefficient zero contributes nothing: 0·3n is not part of the sequence. (B) and (D) add a factor of n that no repeated root justifies — both roots are simple.

Check. T(2) = 5·2 − 6·1 = 4 = 22 and T(3) = 5·4 − 6·2 = 8 = 23. The page iterates the recurrence to n = 25 and compares with 2n at every step.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 1 mark

Consider the recurrence T(n) = 2T(n−1) + n·2n for n > 0, with T(0) = 1. Which one of the following options is CORRECT?

(A) Θ(n22n)   (B) Θ(n2n)   (C) Θ((log n)22n)   (D) Θ(4n)

Answer: (A) Θ(n22n). Divide by 2n: with S(n) = T(n)/2n, the recurrence becomes S(n) = S(n−1) + n with S(0) = 1. Unrolling gives S(n) = 1 + n(n+1)/2, so T(n) = 2n(1 + n(n+1)/2).

Why (D) is the trap. The values are 1, 4, 16, 56, 176, 512. The first three are exactly 4n, and stopping there gives (D). At n = 3 the true value 56 is already below 64, and the gap widens. (B) and (C) are the wrong powers of n.

Method note. The substitution that works is division by 2n, not by n·2n: dividing by n·2n would leave a different denominator at n−1 and the step would not cancel. The page checks the closed form against iteration for n up to 19.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2004, Q45 2 marks

The recurrence T(1) = 1, T(n) = 2T(n−1) + n for n ≥ 2 evaluates to which of the following? The paper asks this as an MCQ; here it is asked as a numeric so that you produce the closed form yourself. Give the value of T(5).

Answer: T(5) = 57. Unrolling: T(n) = 2n−1 + Σk=2..n2n−k·k, which simplifies to 2n+1 − n − 2. At n = 5 that is 64 − 5 − 2 = 57.

The closed form used on the paper. 2n+1 − n − 2, which is the option the official key marks. Iterating gives 1, 4, 11, 26, 57, 120, 247 — the same values.

Traps. Writing 2n − n or 2n+1 − 2n − 2 are the two nearby wrong shapes: both fit the first value and both fail at n = 3, where the truth is 11. The page iterates to n = 19 and compares with the closed form at every step.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2015 Set 1, Q49 2 marks

Let an represent the number of bit strings of length n containing two consecutive 1s. What is the recurrence relation for an?

(1) an−2 + an−1 + 2n−2   (2) an−2 + 2an−1 + 2n−2
(3) 2an−2 + an−1 + 2n−2   (4) 2an−2 + 2an−1 + 2n−2

Answer: (1) an−2 + an−1 + 2n−2. The clean route is the complement. Let bn be the strings with no two consecutive 1s, so bn = bn−1 + bn−2 and an = 2n − bn. Substituting bn = 2n − an in bn = bn−1 + bn−2 gives an = an−1 + an−2 + 2n − 2n−1 − 2n−2 = an−1 + an−2 + 2n−2.

Why the direct split is harder. If you condition on the last bit, a string ending in 1 may or may not already contain 11 in its prefix, and the cases stop being disjoint — the complement avoids that mess entirely.

Check. a2 = 1 ("11"), a3 = 3 ("110", "011", "111"), so a4 = 1 + 3 + 22 = 8. Enumerating the 16 strings of length 4 gives exactly 8 containing 11.

NAT Original practice question not an official question

Let T(0) = 1, T(1) = 4, and T(n) = 4T(n−1) − 4T(n−2) for n ≥ 2. The value of T(5) is ______.

Answer: 192. The characteristic equation is r2 − 4r + 4 = (r−2)2, a double root at 2, so T(n) = (A + Bn)2n. From T(0) = 1, A = 1; from T(1) = 4, (1 + B)·2 = 4 gives B = 1. So T(n) = (1 + n)2n and T(5) = 6·32 = 192.

Iterating as a check. 1, 4, 12, 32, 80, 192 — the same value. Both routes are computed on this page.

Trap. Writing T(n) = A·2n gives A = 1 from the first base case and T(1) = 2, contradicting T(1) = 4. The contradiction is the signal that the root is repeated and an n is missing.

MSQ Original practice question not an official question

Which of the following recurrences have T(n) = Θ(n log n)? Select all that apply.

(i) T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n   (ii) T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n2
(iii) T(n) = T(n/2) + 1   (iv) T(n) = 2T(n/2) + 5n

Answer: (i) and (iv). In both, a = 2 and b = 2, so the watershed is nlog₂2 = n. In (i) the forcing is n, which is Θ(n) — case 2, giving Θ(n log n). In (iv) the forcing is 5n, which is also Θ(n) because constant factors do not change a Θ class — again case 2, and again Θ(n log n).

(ii) has forcing n2 = Ω(n1+ε) with the regularity condition satisfied, so case 3 gives Θ(n2). (iii) has a = 1, so the watershed is n0 = 1 and the forcing 1 is Θ(1) — case 2 gives Θ(log n), the number of halvings.

Distinction to hold on to. Multiplying the forcing by a constant changes nothing; multiplying it by n or by n2 changes everything. (i) against (ii) is that distinction, and (i) against (iv) is its opposite.

MCQ Original practice question not an official question

For n > 1, T(n) = T(n/2) + 1 with T(1) = 1, where n is a power of two. Which bound is tight?

(A) Θ(log n)   (B) Θ(n)   (C) Θ(log log n)   (D) Θ(√n)

Answer: (A) Θ(log n). Each step halves n and adds one, so the number of steps is the number of halvings needed to reach 1, which is log2n. Concretely T(1) = 1, T(2) = 2, T(4) = 3, T(8) = 4, T(16) = 5.

Via the master theorem. a = 1 and b = 2 give watershed n0 = 1; the forcing is 1 = Θ(1), so case 2 applies and the answer is Θ(n0 log n) = Θ(log n).

Why (C) is tempting. log log n does occur in recurrences such as T(n) = T(√n) + 1, where the exponent halves. Here the size halves, not the exponent, so the answer is log n. This is the closest confused pair in the whole topic.

NAT Original practice question not an official question

Let T(1) = 0 and T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log2n for n > 1, with n a power of two. The value of T(8) is ______.

Answer: 48. T(2) = 2·0 + 2·1 = 2. T(4) = 2·2 + 4·2 = 12. T(8) = 2·12 + 8·3 = 24 + 24 = 48.

Closed form. The level-j cost is n(log n − j), and summing j = 0 to log n − 1 gives n·log n·(log n + 1)/2. At n = 8: 8·3·4/2 = 48.

Why this recurrence is special. It is the gap case: the forcing n log n exceeds the watershed n, but not by a polynomial factor, so the master theorem does not apply. Answering Θ(n log n) by forcing case 2, or Θ(n log n · log n) by forcing a case that does not exist, both miss the point — the tight class is Θ(n log2n).

Revision sheet

Everything on this tab fits on one screen and is meant to be read the night before. Definitions first, then the decision table, then the traps, then six questions to test recall.

Definitions and formulas

Recurrence
an expressed through earlier terms, plus base cases. Order = how far back it reaches.
Linear, homogeneous, constant coefficients
Characteristic equation rk − c1rk−1 − … − ck = 0.
Distinct roots
an = A1r1n + … + Akrkn.
Root r of multiplicity m
(A1 + A2n + … + Amnm−1)·rn.
First order
an = a1 + Σk=2..ng(k) when an = an−1 + g(n).
Sums worth knowing
Σk = n(n+1)/2; Σk2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6; Σk=0..n−12k = 2n − 1.
Master theorem
w = logba. f = O(nw−ε) → Θ(nw); f = Θ(nw) → Θ(nwlog n); f = Ω(nw+ε) with regularity → Θ(f).
Standard answers
T(n−1)+1 → n; T(n−1)+n → n2; 2T(n−1)+1 → 2n; T(n/2)+1 → log n; 2T(n/2)+n → n log n; 2T(n/2)+n2 → n2; 2T(n/2)+n log n → n log2n; T(√n)+1 → log log n.

Procedure in four steps

  1. Name the type. Order? Linear? Homogeneous? Constant coefficients? Divide and conquer?
  2. Pick the one method. Unroll, characteristic equation, particular solution, or the watershed comparison. Never two at once.
  3. Finish the algebra. Solve for the coefficients; count the multiplicity; check whether the forcing sits in a gap.
  4. Verify. Substitute back for general n, and confirm the base cases. Then convert to Θ if the options are asymptotic.

Four traps in one line each

  • The largest root can have coefficient zero (2024 Set 2).
  • A repeated root needs the n factor, or the base cases cannot both hold.
  • The master theorem has a gap: forcing between nw and nw+ε is unanswered by it.
  • Three sample values are a hypothesis, not a result (2025 Set 1).

Six questions to test recall

1. an = an−1 + 3, a1 = 5. What is an?

an = 5 + 3(n−1) = 3n + 2. Unroll: the sum of n−1 copies of 3.

2. T(n) = 3T(n−1) + 2, T(0) = 0. What is the class?

Θ(3n). Unrolling gives 3n − 1. The forcing is constant but the recursion multiplies by 3, so the geometric term wins.

3. r2 − 6r + 9 = 0. What is the general solution?

A double root at 3, so (A + Bn)·3n. Two constants for two base cases.

4. T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n. Which master-theorem case, and what is the answer?

w = log24 = 2, so the watershed is n2. The forcing n is O(n2−ε), so case 1 applies and T(n) = Θ(n2) — the leaves dominate.

5. Is 3n = O(2n)?

No. The ratio (3/2)n grows without bound, so no constant bounds 3n above by 2n. The other direction, 2n = O(3n), is true.

6. Why does T(n) = T(n−1) + n give n2 while T(n) = T(n−1) + 1 gives n?

Because unrolling sums the forcing: Σk=1..nk is about n2/2, while Σk=1..n1 is n. The rule is the same; the added term differs.
