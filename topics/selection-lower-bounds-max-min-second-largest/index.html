Topic and objective

TOPIC: Exact comparison counts for three selection problems — the largest element alone, the largest and smallest together, and the second largest — with the algorithm that attains each count and the adversary argument that forbids anything smaller.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given one of the three problems and a value of $$n$$, state the minimum number of comparisons in the worst case and justify it twice: by exhibiting an algorithm that uses that many, and by counting the information any correct algorithm must collect. Convert between the two forms of the pair bound, $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 2\lceil n/2 \rceil - 2$$ and $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$, and say why the ceiling is there. Find the second largest in $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$ comparisons and explain where the logarithm comes from.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class A — Section 5 of the syllabus names “Searching” and “Asymptotic worst case time and space complexity”, and this lesson is exactly a worst-case comparison count for a searching problem. Two previous-year CSE items ask it, one as a numerical answer and one as an inequality, and both are recomputed on the GATE patterns tab.

PREREQUISITES: reading a loop as a sequence of comparisons and counting them (Asymptotic complexity); the decision-tree argument that a comparison sort needs at least $$\log_2(n!)$$ comparisons (Sorting and the comparison lower bound); the adversary idea as used for binary search (Binary search and its variants). Nothing else is assumed: no probability, no data structures.

VISUALIZATION PLAN: eleven scenes. First what is being counted, on eight numbers with a running ledger, so that “how many” is separated from “how fast it grows”. Then the maximum, as a knockout: one comparison eliminates one candidate, so seven eliminations are needed and seven suffice. Then the obvious method for the pair, two scans and $$2n-2$$, and the observation that one comparison carries two facts. Then the pairing algorithm, drawn as four pairs reduced into winners and losers, giving 10 at $$n=8$$ and 148 at $$n=100$$. Then the adversary accounting that proves 148 is the floor: four categories, $$2n-2$$ units required, only $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ comparisons able to pay two units. Then odd $$n$$ and the table from 2 to 12. Then the second largest the obvious way, $$2n-3$$. Then the tournament tree with its victim lists, 7 plus 2 equals 9. Then the adversary that forces the winner to beat $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ elements. Then an exhaustive minimax over every strategy for $$n=2\ldots5$$, and the point at which the two formulas part. Then the two previous-year items, recomputed.

I read this topic as the counting questions about selection, not as a first course in algorithms. Sorting is the neighbouring lesson and is used here only for contrast: sorting must determine the whole order, selection must not.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A loop that keeps a “best so far” variable, and counting how many times it compares.
  • $$\lfloor x \rfloor$$ is the floor and $$\lceil x \rceil$$ the ceiling: the nearest integers below and above.
  • $$\log_2 n$$ is the number of times you halve $$n$$ before reaching 1; $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ is that count rounded up.
  • That a lower bound is a statement about every possible algorithm, not about one algorithm.

Helpful, not required: having seen the decision-tree argument for sorting, since the same counting idea appears here in a simpler form.

Approximate learning time: 45–60 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. A count is exact, a growth rate is not. Both methods for the pair are linear; only one of them is the minimum.
  2. One comparison can carry two facts. The loser cannot be the largest and the winner cannot be the smallest. The naive method throws the second fact away, at a cost of $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ comparisons.
  3. The pair costs $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$. At $$n=100$$ that is 148, and the adversary shows nothing smaller works.
  4. The second largest costs $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$. Only elements the winner beat can be second, and a balanced bracket keeps that list at $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$.
  5. The two formulas are not the same formula. They agree for $$n \le 6$$ and diverge from $$n = 7$$, which is where marks are lost.

Intuition: you are paying per weighing

Put eight coins on a table and a balance scale beside them. You may weigh two coins against each other as often as you like, and each weighing costs one rupee. What is the cheapest way to find the heaviest coin? Seven weighings: put each coin against the champion so far, and the champion at the end is the heaviest.

Now the shopkeeper asks for the heaviest and the lightest. The obvious answer is to do the same seven-weighing scan twice, once for each question, for fourteen. But each weighing already tells you two things — of the two coins, one is a candidate for heaviest and the other for lightest — so the second scan is paying again for information you already own.

The whole lesson is the bookkeeping that follows from that one sentence, plus a proof that the bookkeeping cannot be improved.

Educational interpretation The scale is an analogy for the comparison model: the algorithm sees numbers only through a test of two of them. The analogy is exact about the cost per operation and silent about everything else.

Formal definitions

The comparison model counts only the operations that take two elements and return which is larger. All other work — indexing, moving, adding — is free. A count is therefore an integer, and the question “the minimum number of comparisons” asks for the smallest worst-case integer over all correct algorithms.

$$\text{largest} \;=\; n - 1$$

Every element except the largest must lose a comparison, and one comparison produces one loser.

$$\text{largest and smallest} \;=\; \left\lceil \tfrac{3n}{2} \right\rceil - 2$$

Also written $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 2\lceil n/2 \rceil - 2$$: order each pair, then reduce the winners and the losers separately.

$$\text{second largest} \;=\; n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$

Find the largest by a balanced knockout, then the largest among the $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ elements it beat.

Mathematical derivation Each formula is derived in the cards that follow and then checked against an executed algorithm for every $$n$$ from 2 to 64.

Derivation 1 — the pair, both parities

Make $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ pairs and compare inside each pair. Every pair is now ordered, so it has a larger and a smaller member. The overall largest is one of the $$\lceil n/2 \rceil$$ larger members, and the overall smallest is one of the $$\lceil n/2 \rceil$$ smaller members.

Finding the largest among $$\lceil n/2 \rceil$$ candidates costs $$\lceil n/2 \rceil - 1$$; the same for the smallest. If $$n$$ is odd, one element stands outside the pairs and must be compared with both champions, at a cost of 2.

$$\underbrace{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}_{\text{the pairs}} \;+\; 2\underbrace{\left(\lceil n/2 \rceil - 1\right)}_{\text{two reductions}} \;=\; \lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$

Even $$n$$: $$n/2 + n - 2$$. Odd $$n$$: $$(n-1)/2 + n - 1 = 3(n-1)/2$$. Both equal the ceiling form.

Mathematical derivation Checked numerically: the executed algorithm's count equals the closed form for $$n = 2\ldots64$$, and the saving over $$2n-2$$ equals $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ on every one of those values.

Derivation 2 — why no algorithm can do better

Track each element in one of four states: untouched, has only won, has only lost, has both won and lost. When the algorithm stops, the largest has only won, the smallest has only lost, and the other $$n-2$$ elements have both — because an element that has never lost could be the largest, and one that has never won could be the smallest.

So the algorithm must collect $$1 + 1 + 2(n-2) = 2n-2$$ facts. A comparison between two untouched elements delivers two facts at once, because the winner gains a win and the loser a loss. Every other comparison delivers at most one. At most $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ comparisons can be of the generous kind, since each consumes two untouched elements.

$$\text{comparisons} \;\ge\; (2n-2) - \lfloor n/2 \rfloor \;=\; \lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$

At $$n=100$$: $$198 - 50 = 148$$. The bound is attained, so it is the answer.

Mathematical derivation The accounting is recomputed for $$n = 2\ldots64$$ and compared with the closed form; the page reports the result as a model check.

Derivation 3 — the second largest

The second largest lost exactly one comparison that matters: the one against the largest. Anyone who lost to a third element is smaller than that element and so cannot be second. Therefore the candidates for second are exactly the elements the largest beat.

In a balanced knockout the winner plays one match per round and there are $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ rounds, so its victim list has that length. Finding the largest of the victims costs one comparison fewer.

$$\underbrace{(n-1)}_{\text{the winner}} \;+\; \underbrace{(\lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 1)}_{\text{the victims}} \;=\; n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$

At $$n=8$$: $$7 + 2 = 9$$. At $$n=100$$: $$99 + 6 = 105$$, against 197 for the two-pass method.

Mathematical derivation The page builds the bracket, checks that the winner's list has exactly $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ entries, and checks the second largest it reports against a sort.

Why the logarithm cannot be replaced

The logarithm is the depth of the bracket, not a decoration. If the tournament is unbalanced — a plain left-to-right scan is the extreme case — the winner plays $$n-1$$ matches, its victim list has $$n-1$$ entries, and searching it costs $$n-2$$. The total is then $$2n-3$$, which is the naive method again.

So the saving comes entirely from making the winner's path short, and a balanced tree makes it as short as possible: each match halves the number of survivors, so no element can win more than $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ matches.

Derivation The page runs the unbalanced variant as well and reports $$2n-3$$, so the contrast is computed rather than asserted.

Worked examples, page 1 of 2 — the pair at n = 8 and n = 100

Every number below is produced by running the algorithm on the array and counting, then compared with the closed form. The drawn array is $$7, 2, 9, 4, 8, 1, 6, 3$$.

Example 1 — the pairing algorithm at n = 8

Step 1, the pairs. Compare (7,2), (9,4), (8,1), (6,3): four comparisons. Winners 7, 9, 8, 6; losers 2, 4, 1, 3.

Step 2, the largest of the winners. 7 against 9, then 9 against 8, then 9 against 6: three comparisons, largest 9.

Step 3, the smallest of the losers. 2 against 4, then 2 against 1, then 1 against 3: three comparisons, smallest 1.

Total $$4 + 3 + 3 = 10$$, and $$\lceil 3\cdot 8/2 \rceil - 2 = 12 - 2 = 10$$.

Verified fact The two-pass method on the same array costs 14, which is $$2n-2$$. The saving is 4, which is $$\lfloor 8/2 \rfloor$$.

Example 2 — the same at n = 100

Fifty pairs: 50 comparisons. Fifty winners reduced: 49. Fifty losers reduced: 49.

Total $$50 + 49 + 49 = 148$$. The naive method gives $$2\cdot100 - 2 = 198$$.

The page runs both on one hundred distinct numbers and reports 148 and 198. The minimum and maximum the pairing algorithm returns are checked against a sort of the same array.

Verified fact This is the number the official key of GATE CSE 2014 Set 1 Q39 gives: 148, with an acceptance range of 147.1 to 148.1.

Worked examples, page 2 of 2 — the second largest, and odd n

Example 3 — the tournament at n = 8

Round 1: (7,2) gives 7; (9,4) gives 9; (8,1) gives 8; (6,3) gives 6. Four comparisons.

Round 2: 7 against 9 gives 9, and 9 now has beaten 7 and 4; 8 against 6 gives 8. Two comparisons.

Final: 9 against 8 gives 9, whose victim list is now 7, 4, 8. One comparison. Total 7, which is $$n-1$$.

Second: the largest of 7, 4, 8 costs two comparisons and is 8. Total $$7 + 2 = 9 = 8 + \lceil \log_2 8 \rceil - 2$$.

Verified fact The two-pass method on the same array costs 13. The saving of 4 is $$n - 1 - \lceil \log_2 n \rceil = 8 - 1 - 3$$.

Example 4 — five numbers, odd n

Take 3, 7, 1, 5, 2. Two pairs: (3,7) and (1,5), two comparisons, winners 7 and 5, losers 3 and 1. The leftover 2 is compared with the largest and with the smallest: two more.

Reduce the winners: 7 against 5, one comparison. Reduce the losers: 3 against 1, one comparison. Total $$2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6$$.

The closed form gives $$\lceil 15/2 \rceil - 2 = 8 - 2 = 6$$, and the odd form gives $$3(5-1)/2 = 6$$.

Derivation Forgetting the leftover's two comparisons gives 4, which is below the true minimum — an error that shows up as a model-check failure in the page.

Example 5 — the exhaustive search, n = 2 to 5

For four elements the page enumerates all 24 orderings, tries every pair, lets the adversary keep the larger half of the orderings, and computes the smallest worst-case count. The values are 1, 3, 4, 6 for both problems.

The search visits 1, 13, 183 and 3,851 states at $$n = 2,3,4,5$$, and both formulas reproduce its answers exactly.

Mathematical derivation This is a check on small cases, not a proof: it can refute a formula but cannot establish one for all $$n$$.

Example 6 — where the formulas part

The pair and the second largest give the same number for $$n = 2,3,4,5,6$$: 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 7. At $$n = 7$$ the pair needs 9 and the second largest needs 8.

At $$n = 8$$: 10 against 9. At $$n = 12$$: 16 against 14. The pair bound grows at $$1.5n$$, the second-largest bound at $$n + \log_2 n$$, so the gap widens without limit.

Derivation Computed for $$n = 2\ldots30$$; the first disagreement is at $$n = 7$$, which the page asserts as a model check.

n = 1 and n = 2

With one element there is nothing to compare: the largest, the smallest and the second largest are not all defined, and the count is 0. With two elements one comparison settles the largest and the smallest at once, which is why the pair formula gives 1 and not 2.

The second largest at $$n=2$$ is the smaller of the two, and one comparison finds it: $$2 + 1 - 2 = 1$$.

Derivation Both formulas are checked at $$n=2$$ in the page; the ceiling form is the one that survives the check without a special case.

Equal elements

Every count here assumes distinct values. With ties, “the second largest” is ambiguous: in $$4, 4, 1$$ the second largest is 4 if you mean the second largest value and 1 if you mean the second largest element after removing one copy.

The comparison counts do not change — the algorithms still run and still cost the same — but the answer they return may not be what the question means. The page breaks ties with a consistent rule and says so.

Educational interpretation A question that says “distinct integers” removes the ambiguity; one that does not is testing whether you notice it.

The odd leftover is not optional

At odd $$n$$ the pairing method leaves one element outside the pairs. It must be compared with the current largest and the current smallest — two comparisons — or it may itself be the extreme.

Omitting those two comparisons is the most common arithmetic slip in this topic, and it produces a count one or two below the true minimum.

Derivation The odd form $$3(n-1)/2$$ and the ceiling form $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$ agree for every odd $$n$$ from 3 to 63, which the page verifies.

The comparison model is an assumption

Every bound here is a bound on algorithms that learn about the numbers only by comparing them. Counting sort and radix sort step outside the model: they inspect the values themselves, and they can sort in linear time.

None of that contradicts these counts. The counts are conditional on the model, and a question that asks for a minimum number of comparisons is asking inside it.

Educational interpretation The same distinction governs the sorting lower bound: $$\log_2(n!)$$ is a wall for comparison sorts and not a wall for sorting.

What about the median?

The median is a different and harder problem. It cannot be found in $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$ comparisons, and its exact worst-case bound is not one of the three results in this lesson.

Do not extend these formulas to the median by analogy. The candidate sets that make the maximum and the second largest cheap do not exist for the middle element.

Educational interpretation The syllabus does not name selection bounds beyond searching, so no further result is quoted here rather than quoted unverified.

Two order statistics are not two searches

The pair bound is smaller than $$2(n-1)$$ because the two answers share work. The same reasoning extends to any fixed number of order statistics: finding the largest $$k$$ can share the pair comparisons, but the saving is bounded and each extra statistic costs more.

The lesson stops at three results because those are the ones the syllabus and the papers use; the method, not the list, is what transfers.

Educational interpretation

1. “Finding the largest and smallest costs 2n − 2”

Wrong. That is the cost of the obvious method, not the minimum. Counterexample: at $$n = 8$$ the naive method costs 14 and the pairing method costs 10; at $$n = 100$$, 198 against 148.

Derivation The saving is exactly $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$, one comparison for every pair whose second fact the naive method discards.

2. “The second largest also costs 3n/2 − 2”

Wrong in general, right by accident for small n. The two formulas give the same value for $$n \le 6$$ and differ from $$n = 7$$: 9 against 8. At $$n = 8$$: 10 against 9. At $$n = 12$$: 16 against 14.

Derivation $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$ grows like $$1.5n$$; $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$ grows like $$n + \log n$$. Equal at small $$n$$, separated for good afterwards.

3. “The second largest needs 2n − 3, and that is that”

Wrong. $$2n-3$$ is the two-pass method: find the largest, then scan everything again. Counterexample: at $$n = 8$$ that is 13, and the tournament does it in 9.

Derivation The second pass re-examines elements already proved smaller than the largest. Only the largest's own victims can be second.

4. “Any tournament makes the winner's list short”

Wrong. The list is the winner's number of matches, which is the depth of the tree. A left-to-right scan is a tournament of depth $$n-1$$, so the list has $$n-1$$ entries and the total is $$2n-3$$ again.

Derivation The saving comes from the balance, not from the word “tournament”. The page runs both shapes and reports both counts.

5. “A lower bound for one algorithm is a lower bound for the problem”

Wrong. Showing that the naive method needs $$2n-2$$ says nothing about any other method. A lower bound must count something every correct algorithm is forced to achieve — eliminations for the maximum, units of information for the pair, victim-list length for the second largest.

Educational interpretation This is the single most common reasoning error in the topic, and it is why the lesson insists on both halves of every answer.

6. “Counting sort sorts in O(n), so these bounds are false”

Confusion of models. Counting sort does not compare elements, so a bound on comparisons does not apply to it. Within the comparison model the bounds stand; outside it, the question is a different question.

Educational interpretation Always say which model a count is in before quoting it.

How this unit is actually asked

Three shapes, all of them answered by the same four steps. The two items below are verified previous-year questions; their years, sets and question numbers are given, and their wordings come from past-paper archives rather than from the official papers.

Pattern A — the numerical answer

GATE CSE 2014, Set 1, question 39. “The minimum number of comparisons required to find the minimum and the maximum of 100 numbers is ______.”

Step 1. Two order statistics, so the pair bound applies, not the single one. Step 2. $$n = 100$$ is even: $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2 = 150 - 2 = 148$$. Step 3. Sanity check: the naive method gives 198, so 148 is smaller, as it must be.

Answer 148. The published key gives 148 with an acceptance range of 147.1 to 148.1, and the page reproduces 148 by running the algorithm on one hundred numbers.

Verified fact Wording and key from two independent past-paper archives that agree; the official paper was not opened this run.

Pattern B — the inequality

GATE CSE 2021, Set 1, question 2. Let $$t$$ be the lowest upper bound on the comparisons needed to find the minimum and maximum of $$n$$ elements. Which is correct?

The exact count is $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$, so $$t$$ is that number. Then $$t \le 3\lceil n/2 \rceil$$ for every $$n \ge 2$$, and $$t > n$$ from $$n = 5$$ onwards; the exact count equals $$n$$ at $$n = 3$$ and $$n = 4$$, which is the only place the strict inequality fails. Answer: $$t > n$$ and $$t \le 3\lceil n/2 \rceil$$.

The options $$t > 2n-2$$ and $$t > \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ with $$t \le n$$ are both refuted by the exact count. The page checks $$t \le 3\lceil n/2 \rceil$$ for $$n = 2\ldots64$$ and $$t > n$$ for $$n = 5\ldots64$$, and reports the two small values where $$t = n$$ rather than hiding them.

Verified fact Wording and answer from past-paper archives; the count itself is recomputed in the page.

Pattern C — which statement is true

A statement question offers four claims about the same count, usually one correct and three that confuse the three problems. Read each option as a number at a named $$n$$ and check it.

The distractors are predictable: the naive count, the pair count used for the second largest, and a count that omits the odd leftover. Naming the problem and substituting is faster than reasoning about the options.

Educational interpretation

The four-step procedure

  1. Name the problem. Largest alone, largest and smallest, or second largest. Each has its own formula.
  2. Name the parity. Even and odd $$n$$ differ by a leftover, and the ceiling form absorbs the difference.
  3. Substitute and sanity-check. The answer must be below the naive count and above $$n-1$$.
  4. Say what attains it. A question that asks for a minimum expects the method as well as the number.

The third step catches almost every arithmetic slip, because the naive count is easy to compute and the optimal count must be smaller.

NAT original

Q1 — the pair at n = 64

The minimum number of comparisons required to find both the minimum and the maximum of 64 distinct numbers is ______.

Answer: 94

$$n = 64$$ is even, so the count is $$3n/2 - 2 = 96 - 2 = 94$$. By the parts: 32 pairs, 31 reductions of winners, 31 reductions of losers.

The trap answers are 126 ($$2n-2$$) and 96 (forgetting the $$-2$$).

NAT original

Q2 — the pair at n = 27

The minimum number of comparisons required to find both the minimum and the maximum of 27 distinct numbers is ______.

Answer: 39

$$\lceil 3\cdot27/2 \rceil - 2 = \lceil 40.5 \rceil - 2 = 41 - 2 = 39$$. The odd form gives $$3(27-1)/2 = 39$$.

The trap is 38, which drops one of the two comparisons the leftover element needs. Using $$\lfloor 3n/2 \rfloor - 2$$ also gives 38.

MCQ original

Q3 — the second largest at n = 16

The minimum number of comparisons to find the largest and the second largest of 16 distinct numbers is:

(a) 22   (b) 29   (c) 18   (d) 20

Answer: (c) 18

$$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2 = 16 + 4 - 2 = 18$$: fifteen comparisons to find the largest, four victims, three comparisons among them.

(a) is the pair bound $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2 = 22$$, which answers a different question. (b) is $$2n - 3 = 29$$, the two-pass method. (d) forgets that the victim list has length 4, not 5.

Option (a) is the trap that the whole lesson is built around.

MSQ original

Q4 — which statements are true

For distinct numbers and $$n \ge 2$$, which statements are TRUE?

(a) the maximum alone needs exactly $$n-1$$ comparisons   (b) the pair needs exactly $$2n-2$$   (c) the second largest needs at most $$2n-3$$   (d) the pair needs at most $$3\lceil n/2 \rceil$$

Answer: (a), (c) and (d)

(a) is exact: $$n-1$$ is both attainable and necessary. (c) holds because the two-pass method attains $$2n-3$$, and the optimal count is below it. (d) holds because $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2 \le 3\lceil n/2 \rceil$$.

(b) fails at $$n = 8$$: 10 is attainable, and $$2n-2 = 14$$ is not the minimum.

(b) is the misconception the lesson refutes; (c) is deliberately an upper bound, not an exact count.

MCQ original

Q5 — the victim list

The largest of $$n$$ distinct numbers is found by a balanced knockout tournament. How many elements can possibly be the second largest?

(a) $$n-1$$   (b) $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$   (c) $$n-2$$   (d) 2

Answer: (b)

Only elements that lost directly to the largest can be second, and the largest plays one match per round. A balanced bracket has $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ rounds.

(a) is the answer for an unbalanced scan, which is why the balance matters. (c) counts everyone except the largest and itself. (d) is true only at $$n \le 4$$.

The page checks this list length for every $$n$$ from 2 to 64.

NAT original

Q6 — the two-pass saving

For $$n = 12$$, by how many comparisons does the optimal method for the second largest beat the two-pass method?

Answer: 7

Two-pass: $$2n-3 = 21$$. Optimal: $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2 = 12 + 4 - 2 = 14$$. Saving $$21 - 14 = 7$$, which is also $$n - 1 - \lceil \log_2 n \rceil = 11 - 4$$.

Answering 6 uses $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil = 3$$, which is the value for $$n \le 8$$.

MCQ original

Q7 — the odd leftover

In the pairing method for the largest and smallest at odd $$n$$, what happens to the element that is left outside the pairs?

(a) it is ignored, since it cannot be an extreme   (b) it is compared with both champions, costing 2   (c) it is compared with one champion, costing 1   (d) it is paired with the first pair, costing 1

Answer: (b)

The leftover has lost nothing, so it is still a candidate for both the largest and the smallest. Two comparisons settle both, and the count becomes $$3(n-1)/2$$.

(a) and (c) produce a count below the true minimum, which the page's own check would flag.

What to do with a wrong answer

Write down which of the three problems the question names, then which formula you used. Almost every wrong answer in this topic is one of two slips: the pair formula applied to the second largest, or a count that drops the odd leftover.

If your number is above the naive count, the formula is wrong; if it is below $$n-1$$, an element was assumed to be excluded for free.

Three proofs in one sentence each

  • Largest: every comparison creates one loser, and $$n-1$$ elements must lose.
  • Pair: $$2n-2$$ facts are needed, and only $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ comparisons can deliver two facts at once.
  • Second: the winner must beat $$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$$ elements, and the second largest is the largest of them.

If you can say these three sentences, you can rebuild every formula in the exam.

Above $$n-1$$
two statistics cannot be cheaper than one
Even $$n$$
$$3n/2 - 2$$ exactly
Odd $$n$$
$$3(n-1)/2$$, or use the ceiling form
Power of two
$$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil = \log_2 n$$, so no rounding

These five cover every arithmetic check the papers ask for; the unsafe shortcut is assuming the pair formula answers the second-largest question.

What to say in one sentence

Finding the largest and smallest at once costs $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$ because one comparison between two untouched elements answers two questions, and finding the second largest costs $$n + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil - 2$$ because only the elements the winner beat can be second.

If you remember one number: 148 at $$n = 100$$, and 9 rather than 13 at $$n = 8$$ for the second largest.

The four-step procedure, again

  1. Name the problem and its formula.
  2. Check the parity, and whether $$\log_2 n$$ is exact.
  3. Substitute, then check the answer sits between $$n-1$$ and the naive count.
  4. Say what attains it — pairing for the pair, a balanced bracket for the second largest.

The last step is what separates a number from an answer.

Textbook-level references consulted

  • CLRS, chapter 9 section 1, “Minimum and maximum”. Supports the pairing algorithm, the $$\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$-based count and the statement that the pair costs at most $$3\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$; exercises 9.1-1 and 9.1-2 state the two results this lesson proves, $$n + \lceil \lg n \rceil - 2$$ and the $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$ lower bound.
  • Jørgen Bang-Jensen, University of Southern Denmark, lecture notes on lower bounds for selection. Supports the adversary formulation as an acyclic partial orientation, Theorem 4.2 ($$n \geq$$ pair bound $$\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$$) and Theorem 5.2 (second smallest $$\geq n + \lceil \log n \rceil - 2$$).
  • York University, EECS 3101 lecture notes on minimum and maximum. Supports the four-category accounting (untouched, won only, lost only, both) and the adversary's answers per category.
  • CMU 15-451 lecture notes on lower bounds. Supports the two-pass upper bound $$2n-3$$ and the tournament upper bound $$n + \lg n - 2$$ for the second largest.

Each was read through a search summary, not in full. Every number quoted from them was recomputed by this page, so no figure depends on a source being read correctly.
