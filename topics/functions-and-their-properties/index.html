Topic and objective

TOPIC: Functions and their properties — a function as a relation that is total and single-valued, the three sets involved (domain, codomain, range), injective, surjective and bijective, the counting formulas for each, composition and its one-way inheritance rules, the inverse function versus the preimage of a set, and how images and preimages behave under union, intersection and complement.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a function described by a rule, a list of arrows, a matrix or two finite sets, decide injectivity, surjectivity and bijectivity by a test you can defend; produce the smallest witness when a property fails; count functions, injections, surjections and bijections between finite sets; and state exactly which properties survive composition and which set operations commute with images and preimages.

I read "functions" as the syllabus item "Sets, relations, functions, partial orders and lattices" cut at functions — the three properties, the counts, composition, inverse and preimages. Partial orders, Hasse diagrams and lattices are a separate lesson and are not assumed here. It uses Relations and Their Properties for "a function is a special relation" and Sets and Set Operations for subsets, the Cartesian product and inclusion–exclusion.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus.

Monoids, Groups. Graphs: connectivity, matching, colouring. Combinatorics: counting, recurrence relations, generating functions."

The word "functions" is named directly. Functions are also the vocabulary the rest of the paper assumes: permutations in counting, the transition function of an automaton, the mapping from states to next states in a compiler's data-flow analysis, and cardinality arguments in algorithms.

Prerequisites

  • Relations and their properties: a relation as a subset of A × B, and the matrix and arrow pictures. A function is one particular kind of relation.
  • Sets and set operations: subset, union, intersection, complement, and |A × B| = |A|·|B|.
  • The product rule of counting: if one choice is made independently for each of m items, the number of outcomes is the product.
  • Inclusion–exclusion for two and three sets, from the sets lesson. It is re-derived here for counting surjections.
  • Nothing about groups, partial orders or lattices is assumed.

The five ideas

  1. A function is a relation with two promises: total, and single-valued. Everything else follows from the matrix having exactly one 1 in each row.
  2. The codomain is declared; the range is computed. Surjectivity is the question of whether they are equal.
  3. Read the matrix by columns: at most one 1 is injective, at least one 1 is surjective, exactly one 1 is bijective.
  4. On finite sets of equal size the three properties are one property. Drop finiteness or equal size and they separate.
  5. Images commute with union only; preimages commute with union, intersection and complement. One asymmetry, many questions.

Intuition first: the seat-allocation sheet

A class has six students and three benches, two seats each. The teacher writes a sheet: for every student, one bench. The sheet is a function — every student appears exactly once, and no student appears twice. What the sheet does not promise is that every bench gets used. A bench nobody was sent to is still a bench.

Educational interpretation Injective is a rule that never sends two students to the same bench — impossible here, since there are more students than benches, which is the pigeonhole principle stated as a seating fact. Surjective is a rule that uses every bench. Bijective is a rule that uses every bench and puts at most one student on each: the sheet is then a complete pairing, and it can be read backwards. The codomain is the list of benches that exist; the range is the list of benches actually used.

Formal definition

A function f from A to B is a relation f ⊆ A × B such that:

(1) totality: for every a ∈ A there is some b ∈ B with (a, b) ∈ f;

(2) single-valuedness: if (a, b) ∈ f and (a, b′) ∈ f then b = b′.

Equivalently, f is a subset of A × B in which every element of A occurs exactly once as a first coordinate. Write f(a) = b for (a, b) ∈ f. Verified fact This is the standard definition (Rosen, Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications, functions chapter); the two clauses are usually compressed into "each element of A is assigned exactly one element of B".

Where the three properties live

Draw the m × n matrix of f: a 1 in row i, column j when f(ai) = bj. Being a function already means exactly one 1 in every row. The three properties are then read down the columns.

  • injective: at most one 1 in every column.
  • surjective: at least one 1 in every column.
  • bijective: exactly one 1 in every column — the matrix becomes a permutation matrix.

Why the range is not the codomain

The notation f : A → B declares B once and for all. Two functions with identical arrows but different declared codomains are different functions: f : {1} → {a} with f(1) = a is onto, while f : {1} → {a, b} with f(1) = a is not, although the arrow is the same. This is why "is f onto?" is a question about the declaration as much as about the rule. Educational interpretation The codomain is a promise about where values may live; the range is a report about where they landed.

The four questions, in the order that avoids wasted work

First: is the object a function at all — is every domain element used exactly once? Second: what is the range, computed by listing the images? Third: does the range equal the codomain (surjective), and does its size equal |A| (injective)? Fourth, and only then: which count is being asked for. Nearly every lost mark in this topic comes from answering question three before question one, or from confusing the size of the domain with the size of the codomain in the exponent of a counting formula.

Five worked examples, each one step at a time

Example 1 — is this relation a function?

Let A = {1, 2, 3} and B = {a, b, c}. Take R = {(1, a), (2, a), (3, b)}.

Totality. The first coordinates are 1, 2, 3 — all of A appears. Pass.

Single-valuedness. No first coordinate is repeated. Pass.

Verdict. R is a function. Its range is {a, b}.

Now change one pair. R′ = {(1, a), (1, b), (2, a), (3, b)}. The first coordinate 1 appears twice, so R′ is not single-valued and therefore not a function, even though every element of A does appear. Derivation Both verdicts were recomputed on the page.

Example 2 — the three properties on one function

f : {1, 2, 3} → {a, b, c} with f(1) = a, f(2) = a, f(3) = c.

Matrix. Row 1 has a 1 in column a; row 2 has a 1 in column a; row 3 has a 1 in column c. Column b is empty.

Injective? No. Column a holds two 1s, so f(1) = f(2) with 1 ≠ 2. Witness found.

Surjective? No. Column b is empty, so b is never reached.

Bijective? No, since it fails both.

Range. {a, c}, of size 2 against a domain of size 3 — which already shows injectivity must fail, by pigeonhole.

Example 3 — counting onto functions, 4 into 3

The number of onto functions from a 4-element set to a 3-element set.

All functions: 34 = 81.

Miss a named element: for each of the 3 codomain elements, the functions that avoid it number 24 = 16, giving 3 × 16 = 48. Subtract.

Miss two named elements: for each of the 3 pairs, the functions that avoid both number 14 = 1, giving 3. Add back.

Miss all three: zero, since a function must land somewhere.

Answer: 81 − 48 + 3 = 36. Derivation Enumerating all 81 functions and testing surjectivity gives exactly 36.

Example 4 — the smallest counterexample to a false inheritance

Claim: if f ∘ g is injective then f is injective. Refute it on the smallest sets that work.

Take A = {1}, B = {x, y}, C = {p}. Let g(1) = x and let f(x) = p, f(y) = p.

Is f ∘ g injective? Its domain has one element, so no two distinct inputs exist. Vacuously injective.

Is f injective? f(x) = f(y) = p with x ≠ y. Not injective.

Conclusion: the claim is false, and the inner function g is the only part forced to be injective. Derivation The page checks the true half — "f ∘ g injective ⇒ g injective" — on all 216 ordered pairs of functions on a 3-element set.

Example 5 — images and preimages on one function

Let X = {1, 2, 3}, Y = {p, q}, and f(1) = p, f(2) = p, f(3) = q. Take S = {1, 3} and T = {2, 3}.

Images. f(S) = {p, q} and f(T) = {p, q}. Their intersection is {p, q}.

Intersection first. S ∩ T = {3}, so f(S ∩ T) = {q}.

So f(S ∩ T) = {q} ⊊ {p, q} = f(S) ∩ f(T). The equality fails, and the failure is exactly the collision f(1) = f(2).

Union first. S ∪ T = {1, 2, 3}, so f(S ∪ T) = {p, q} = f(S) ∪ f(T). The union equality holds, as it always does.

Preimages. f−1({p}) = {1, 2} and f−1({q}) = {3}. Then f−1({p} ∩ {q}) = f−1(∅) = ∅ = {1,2} ∩ {3}. The intersection equality holds for preimages, and it held because of the definition, not by luck. Derivation All three preimage laws were verified on every function between two 3-element sets, over every pair of subsets.

Boundaries: where the rules stop applying

Each item below is a condition that a shortcut silently assumes. Removing the condition breaks the shortcut, and the smallest example that breaks it is given.

The empty domain

With A = ∅ there is exactly one relation A × B → namely ∅ — and it is a function: totality is vacuous and single-valuedness is vacuous.

So there is exactly one function from ∅ to any B, namely the empty function. It is injective (vacuously) and it is surjective only when B = ∅. This is the case where "injective therefore surjective" is false at equal sizes of zero — actually both hold, but it shows why nm = 1 for m = 0. Derivation The enumeration on this page starts at m = 0 and returns one function.

The empty codomain

If B = ∅ and A ≠ ∅, there is no function from A to B at all: no element of A has anywhere to go, so totality fails for every relation.

The formula nm agrees: 0m = 0 for m ≥ 1. A question that fixes |B| = 0 and asks for the number of onto functions is asking a question whose answer is 0 for a different reason than usual — there is nothing to count. Derivation Counted on the page.

Equal sizes, but infinite

On the natural numbers, s(n) = n + 1 is injective and not surjective (0 is never hit), and g(0) = 0, g(n) = n − 1 for n ≥ 1 is surjective and not injective.

So on an infinite set, injective and surjective are genuinely different, even though the "two sides" are the same set. The collapse of the three properties requires finiteness as well as equal size. Verified fact This is the standard definition of a Dedekind-infinite set.

A rule is not a function until its domain is fixed

The rule x ↦ x² is not a function from ℝ to ℝ that is injective, and it is not surjective onto ℝ either, since no real squares to −1.

Restricted to x ≥ 0 with codomain x ≥ 0 it becomes a bijection. Restricted to x ≥ 0 with codomain ℝ it is injective but not surjective. Three different functions from one formula, decided entirely by the two sets. Educational interpretation Always read the declared domain and codomain before testing any property.

m = n = 1

With one element on each side there is exactly one function, and it is a bijection. The counts are 1, 1, 1, 1 for functions, injections, surjections and bijections.

This is the smallest case in which the equal-size collapse is visible, and it is worth checking a formula against it before trusting the formula. Derivation Computed on the page.

Composition when the middle sets do not match

f ∘ g is defined only when the codomain of g is contained in the domain of f. If g : A → B and f : B′ → C with B ⊄ B′, the composite is not defined on all of A.

Every statement in this lesson assumes the types line up: g : A → B and f : B → C. A question that composes functions with mismatched middle sets is either a typo or a question about partial functions, and it should be read as the latter only if it says so.

Six beliefs that cost marks

Each is stated the way a learner states it, with the smallest structure that refutes it and the rule to use instead. The counterexamples are computed on this page, so they can be checked rather than trusted.

1. "Range and codomain are the same thing"

Why it seems right: in school examples the codomain is usually chosen to be exactly the set of values, so the two coincide and the distinction never bites.

Why it is wrong: the codomain is part of the declaration f : A → B; the range is computed. They differ precisely when f is not onto.

Minimal counterexample: f : {1, 2} → {a, b, c} with f(1) = f(2) = a. The range is {a}; the codomain is {a, b, c}.

Rule: compute the range by listing images, then compare it to the declared codomain. Never assume the two are equal.

2. "One-to-one means one-to-one correspondence"

Why it seems right: both phrases contain the same words, and informal writing uses them interchangeably.

Why it is wrong: "one-to-one" is injective; "one-to-one correspondence" is bijective. Injectivity says nothing about coverage of the codomain.

Minimal counterexample: f : {1, 2} → {a, b, c} with f(1) = a, f(2) = b. One-to-one, not a correspondence, because c is never reached.

Rule: read "one-to-one" as injective and "onto" as surjective. Only the pair together is a correspondence.

3. "Injective on a finite set implies surjective"

Why it seems right: it is true — but only when the two sets have the same size, which is the case that appears in most textbook examples.

Why it is wrong: f : {1, 2} → {a, b, c} with f(1) = a, f(2) = b is injective and not surjective. The sizes are 2 and 3.

Rule: state the two sizes before using the collapse. For finite sets, injective ⇒ |A| ≤ |B| and surjective ⇒ |A| ≥ |B|; only together with equality do the properties coincide. Derivation Of the 27 functions on a 3-element set, exactly 6 are injective and exactly 6 are surjective, and the page confirms the two lists are the same list.

4. "f(A ∩ B) = f(A) ∩ f(B)"

Why it seems right: it is the natural analogue of the union law, which is true, and it is true for preimages.

Why it is wrong: only one inclusion survives. Two different elements may map to the same value from opposite sides of the intersection.

Minimal counterexample: f(1) = f(2) = p, f(3) = q; S = {1, 3}, T = {2, 3}. Then f(S ∩ T) = {q} while f(S) ∩ f(T) = {p, q}.

Rule: f(A ∩ B) ⊆ f(A) ∩ f(B) always, with equality exactly when f is injective. Derivation Checked over all functions between two 3-element sets and all 64 pairs of subsets: equality holds for precisely the injective ones.

5. "If f ∘ g is injective then both parts are injective"

Why it seems right: the composite inherits the property, so it feels as though the property must come from both parts.

Why it is wrong: the composite is only ever evaluated on the range of g, so the outer function may collide freely outside it.

Minimal counterexample: A = {1}, B = {x, y}, C = {p}; g(1) = x, f(x) = f(y) = p. The composite is injective; f is not.

Rule: f ∘ g injective ⇒ g injective. That is the only half that survives. Derivation Verified on all 216 ordered pairs of functions on a 3-element set, with the counterexample for f exhibited.

6. "If f ∘ g is onto and f is onto, then g is onto"

Why it seems right: every value of C is reached, and it is reached through g and then f, so g appears to have to cover B.

Why it is wrong: f may collapse several elements of B onto the same value of C, so the elements of B that g misses are simply invisible in the composite.

Minimal counterexample: A = {1, 2}, B = {x, y, z}, C = {c}; g(1) = g(2) = x and f(x) = f(y) = f(z) = c. Both f and f ∘ g are onto; g is neither injective nor onto.

Rule: f ∘ g onto ⇒ f onto. Nothing is forced about g unless f is also injective. Verified fact This is exactly the GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, Q17 question, whose keyed answer is that g need be neither.

What GATE actually asks about functions

Five patterns cover the function questions in the papers: a counting question about one named property, a property verdict on a rule-defined function, a claim about which property survives composition, a claim about images or preimages of subsets, and a cardinality argument that turns two bijections into an equation. The first and the fourth are the most common.

Pattern 1 — count functions with one property

Asks: "the number of functions from an m-element set to an n-element set", "the number of one-one mappings", "the number of onto functions".

Procedure: identify the row of the counting table, then substitute. Check the answer against the boundary case m = n or n = 1, where the value is obvious. Verified fact The count of all functions, nm, is GATE CSE 1998 Q19; the count of one-one mappings for m < n, ⁿPm, is GATE CSE 1993 Q8.6.

Pattern 2 — verdict on a rule-defined function

Asks: a function is given by a formula or a piecewise rule on the integers, then "which of the following is true" about injective, surjective, bijective.

Procedure: for injectivity solve f(x) = f(y) and see whether x = y is forced. For surjectivity solve f(x) = y for x in the domain. Try the smallest values, including 0 and negatives, before concluding.

Pattern 3 — which property survives composition

Asks: given that f, g or f ∘ g has a property, which of the following must be true.

Procedure: remember the two positive rules and the two one-way rules; for any option that claims more, build the smallest counterexample — one element in the outer set and a collision in the middle. Verified fact This is GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, Q17, keyed to "g need be neither".

Pattern 4 — images and preimages of subsets

Asks: which of several set identities holds for every function, usually with four options about unions, intersections or complements.

Procedure: preimage statements are almost always the true ones, because the preimage is defined by a biconditional. For an image statement, test it on a two-element collision; if it survives, it is probably the union law. Verified fact This is GATE CSE 2014 Set 3, Q2, keyed to the preimage-of-intersection option.

Pattern 5 — two bijections become an equation

Asks: "there exist one-to-one and onto functions from A to B and from A × A to A ∪ B; the number of possible values of |A| is ___".

Procedure: a bijection between finite sets forces equal cardinalities, so each clause is an equation. Then bound the unknown quantity — here n ≤ |A ∪ B| ≤ 2n — and solve the resulting inequality in integers. Verified fact This is GATE CSE 2024 Set 1, Q32, keyed to 2.

The three-step habit for any function question

Write down m and n first — the sizes of the domain and the codomain — and the declared codomain, not just the range. Then decide which of the three properties is being asked about, and test it with the witness that would refute it rather than by searching for confirmation. Then check the answer against the boundary case m = n, n = 1 or m = 0, where the correct value is obvious by hand. Most wrong answers in this topic are a correct formula applied to the wrong pair of sizes.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 1998, Q19 1 mark

The number of functions from an m element set to an n element set is:

(A) m + n   (B) mn   (C) nm   (D) m·n

Answer: (C) nm. Each of the m domain elements independently chooses one of the n codomain elements, and the product rule gives n multiplied by itself m times.

Why the others are wrong. (B) mn is the count of functions in the other direction, from an n-element set to an m-element set. (A) and (D) are not counts of functions at all; they count something like pairs of choices.

Exam trap The exponent is the size of the domain, because the domain is what makes the free choices. Reading "m element set to an n element set" quickly and writing mn is the intended error. Derivation For m = 4, n = 3 the page counts 81 = 34 functions by enumeration.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 1993, Q8.6 2 marks

Let A and B be sets with cardinalities m and n respectively. The number of one-one mappings from A to B, when m < n, is:

(A) mn   (B) nPm   (C) mCn   (D) nCm

Answer: (B) nPm = n(n−1)…(n−m+1) = n!/(n−m)!. The first element of A has n choices; the second must avoid the first image, leaving n−1; and so on down to n−m+1 for the last.

Why (D) is the tempting wrong answer. nCm counts only the set of m images. Two different one-one mappings can use the same image set in a different order — with m = 2 there are two orderings for every 2-element image set — so choosing a subset is not enough. Order matters, so the answer is a permutation count.

Why (A) and (C) are wrong. mn is a count of functions in the reverse direction. mCn is zero whenever m < n, which cannot be the answer to a positive count.

Derivation For m = 2, n = 3 the formula gives 3 × 2 = 6, and the page enumerates exactly 6 injections among the 9 functions.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2014 Set 3, Q2 1 mark

Let X and Y be finite sets and f : X → Y be a function. Which one of the following statements is TRUE?

(A) For any subsets A and B of X, |f(A ∪ B)| = |f(A)| + |f(B)|
(B) For any subsets A and B of X, f(A ∩ B) = f(A) ∩ f(B)
(C) For any subsets A and B of X, |f(A ∩ B)| = min{|f(A)|, |f(B)|}
(D) For any subsets S and T of Y, f−1(S ∩ T) = f−1(S) ∩ f−1(T)

Answer: (D). An element x is in f−1(S ∩ T) exactly when f(x) ∈ S ∩ T, which is exactly when f(x) ∈ S and f(x) ∈ T, which is exactly when x is in both preimages. The chain is a biconditional at every step, so the two sets are equal for every function, with no hypothesis on f.

Why (A) is wrong. Take X = {a, b, c}, Y = {1, 2}, f(a) = f(b) = 1, f(c) = 2, with A = {a, c} and B = {b, c}. Then |f(A ∪ B)| = |{1, 2}| = 2, while |f(A)| + |f(B)| = 2 + 2 = 4.

Why (B) is wrong. With A = {a, c}, B = {b, c}: A ∩ B = {c}, so f(A ∩ B) = {2}, while f(A) ∩ f(B) = {1, 2} ∩ {1, 2} = {1, 2}.

Why (C) is wrong. In the same example |f(A ∩ B)| = 1 but min{2, 2} = 2. The true bound is |f(A ∩ B)| ≤ min{|f(A)|, |f(B)|}, and it can be strictly smaller.

Exam trap Three options are about images and one is about preimages. Images commute with union but only half-commute with intersection; preimages commute with both. Spotting that asymmetry answers the question without computing anything. Derivation All three preimage laws and the strict inclusion for images were verified on this page over every function between two 3-element sets.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2024 Set 1, Q32 1 mark

Let A and B be non-empty finite sets such that there exist one-to-one and onto functions (i) from A to B and (ii) from A × A to A ∪ B. The number of possible values of |A| is _______.

Answer: 2. Write n = |A| ≥ 1. Clause (i) is a bijection between A and B, so |B| = n. Clause (ii) is a bijection between A × A and A ∪ B, so |A ∪ B| = |A × A| = n2.

The bound. For any two sets, |A ∪ B| is between max(|A|, |B|) = n and |A| + |B| = 2n. So n ≤ n2 ≤ 2n, and dividing by n gives 1 ≤ n ≤ 2.

Both values occur. n = 1: take A = B = {x}; then |A ∪ B| = 1 = 12. n = 2: take A = {1, 2}, B = {3, 4} disjoint; then |A ∪ B| = 4 = 22.

Exam trap Assuming A and B are disjoint fixes |A ∪ B| = 2n and leaves only n = 2; assuming A = B fixes it at n and leaves only n = 1. The union size is free anywhere from n to 2n, so both values are possible and the answer counts values, not solutions. Derivation The page evaluates the two inequalities for n = 1 to 6 and finds exactly n = 1 and n = 2 admissible.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2025 Set 1, Q17 1 mark

g(·) is a function from A to B, f(·) is a function from B to C, and their composition f(g(·)) is a mapping from A to C. If f(·) and f(g(·)) are onto (surjective) functions, which ONE of the following is TRUE about g(·)?

(A) g must be onto
(B) g must be one-to-one
(C) g must be bijective
(D) g is not required to be one-to-one or onto

Answer: (D). A composite that is onto forces the outer function to be onto, and forces nothing at all on the inner one.

Counterexample to (A), (B) and (C) at once. A = {1, 2}, B = {x, y, z}, C = {c}. Let g(1) = g(2) = x and f(x) = f(y) = f(z) = c. Then f is onto C and f ∘ g is onto C, while g is neither injective (1 and 2 collide) nor onto (y and z are never reached).

Why the intuition fails. Everything the composite reaches is reached through f, so f must cover C. But f may collapse all of B onto a single value, which hides whatever g misses.

Exam trap If the question had also said f is injective, then g would be forced to be onto. Adding that hypothesis is the classic over-read. Derivation The page confirms the true half — f ∘ g onto ⇒ f onto — on all 216 pairs of functions on a 3-element set, and exhibits this counterexample.

NAT Original GATE-style medium

The number of onto functions from a set of 4 elements to a set of 3 elements is _______.

Answer: 36. Inclusion–exclusion: 34 − C(3,1)·24 + C(3,2)·14 − C(3,3)·04 = 81 − 48 + 3 − 0.

Common wrong answer: 33. That is 81 − 48, which subtracts the functions missing one named element but forgets to add back the ones missing two, each of which was subtracted twice.

Another wrong answer: 24. That is 4! , which counts onto functions only when the two sizes are equal; here they are not, so every bijection-style count is zero.

Derivation All 81 functions were enumerated on this page and exactly 36 are surjective.

MSQ Original GATE-style medium

Let f : B → C and g : A → B be functions. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

(A) If f and g are injective then f ∘ g is injective
(B) If f and g are surjective then f ∘ g is surjective
(C) If f ∘ g is injective then f is injective
(D) If f ∘ g is surjective then f is surjective

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). If g never collides and f never collides, the composite never collides: (f∘g)(a₁) = (f∘g)(a₂) gives f(g(a₁)) = f(g(a₂)), so g(a₁) = g(a₂) by injectivity of f, so a₁ = a₂ by injectivity of g.

(B) Take any c ∈ C. Surjectivity of f gives b with f(b) = c; surjectivity of g gives a with g(a) = b; so (f∘g)(a) = c.

(D) Every value the composite reaches is reached through f, so the range of f already contains the range of f ∘ g, which is all of C.

Why (C) is false. A = {1}, B = {x, y}, C = {p}; g(1) = x, f(x) = f(y) = p. The composite is injective on a one-element domain, but f collides.

Exam trap (C) and (D) are not symmetric partners. For injectivity the inner function is forced; for surjectivity the outer one is. Memorising them as a pair in the same direction is the error. Derivation (A), (B) and (D) were checked on all 216 ordered pairs of functions on a 3-element set.

NAT Original GATE-style easy

The number of injective functions from a set of 3 elements to a set of 5 elements is _______.

Answer: 60. 5 × 4 × 3 = 60, which is 5P3 = 5!/2!.

Common wrong answer: 10. That is C(5,3), which counts only the choice of the 3-element image set. The three domain elements can be matched to it in 3! = 6 orders, and 10 × 6 = 60.

Common wrong answer: 125. That is 53, the count of all functions, which includes the ones that collide.

Derivation The page checks the falling factorial against enumeration for every pair of sizes up to 4, and the same argument extends: the first element has 5 choices, the second 4, the third 3.

MCQ Original GATE-style hard

Let f : X → Y be a function and let A, B ⊆ X. Which one of the following is TRUE for every such f?

(A) f(A ∩ B) = f(A) ∩ f(B)
(B) f(A ∩ B) ⊆ f(A) ∩ f(B), and equality holds for every A, B exactly when f is injective
(C) f(A ∪ B) = f(A) ∪ f(B), and equality holds exactly when f is injective
(D) f(A ∖ B) = f(A) ∖ f(B)

Answer: (B). The inclusion is immediate: an element of f(A ∩ B) has a preimage in both A and B, so it lies in both images. For the converse, if f is injective and y ∈ f(A) ∩ f(B), then y = f(a) = f(b) with a ∈ A, b ∈ B; injectivity gives a = b, so y ∈ f(A ∩ B).

Why (A) fails. f(1) = f(2) = p, f(3) = q, A = {1, 3}, B = {2, 3}: f(A ∩ B) = {q} but f(A) ∩ f(B) = {p, q}.

Why (C) is wrong. The union equality is true for every function, so its second clause — "exactly when injective" — is false.

Why (D) is wrong. The same function with A = {1, 3}, B = {2} gives f(A ∖ B) = {p, q} while f(A) ∖ f(B) = {q}. The true statement is f(A) ∖ f(B) ⊆ f(A ∖ B).

Exam trap Options (A) and (B) differ only in the direction of the inclusion and the equality condition; options (C) and (D) mix a true law with a false extra clause. Read every clause, not just the first symbol. Derivation The page confirms that intersection equality holds for exactly the injective functions, over every function and every subset pair between two 3-element sets.

NAT Original GATE-style medium

The number of functions f : {1, 2, 3} → {1, 2, 3} that are neither injective nor surjective is _______.

Answer: 21. There are 27 functions in all. By the equal-size collapse, the injective ones and the surjective ones are the same 6 functions, so 6 functions have at least one of the two properties. Hence 27 − 6 = 21 have neither.

Common wrong answer: 15. That is 27 − 6 − 6, which subtracts the bijections twice. Inclusion–exclusion gives 27 − 6 − 6 + 6 = 21.

Sanity check by shape. A function on a 3-element set that is neither injective nor surjective must use exactly 2 distinct values with one of them taken twice: choose the unused value (3 ways), choose which value is doubled (2 ways), choose the two positions that agree (3 ways) — 18 — plus the 3 constant functions, giving 21.

Derivation All 27 functions were enumerated and classified on this page: 6 injective, 6 surjective, 6 bijective, 21 with neither property.

Revision sheet — functions

Definitions in one line each, then the counts, then the decision procedure, then the traps with the counterexample that defuses each. Everything is stated for f : A → B with |A| = m, |B| = n, both finite and non-empty unless said otherwise.

Definitions

  • function: a relation on A × B that is total and single-valued; every row of its matrix holds exactly one 1.
  • domain: A. codomain: B, as declared. range: f(A) = {f(a) : a ∈ A}, computed.
  • injective (one-to-one): f(a) = f(b) ⇒ a = b. At most one 1 per column.
  • surjective (onto): every b ∈ B equals some f(a). At least one 1 per column; range = codomain.
  • bijective: both. Exactly one 1 per column; the matrix is a permutation matrix.
  • composition: (f ∘ g)(a) = f(g(a)); g applied first; associative, not commutative.
  • inverse function: exists iff f is bijective; then f−1(f(a)) = a and f(f−1(b)) = b.
  • preimage of a set: f−1(T) = {a ∈ A : f(a) ∈ T}; defined for every function.

Decision procedure

  1. Write m = |A| and n = |B|, and write down B itself, not just the range.
  2. Confirm it is a function: every domain element used exactly once.
  3. Compute the range by listing images.
  4. Injective? Compare |range| with m. Equal ⇒ injective (for a function, |range| = m exactly when no collisions).
  5. Surjective? Compare the range with the declared codomain. Equal ⇒ onto.
  6. If m = n and both are finite, one test settles all three.
  7. If a count is asked, pick the row of the table and substitute, then check the boundary case.

Composition and set-operation laws

  • injective ∘ injective ⇒ injective.
  • surjective ∘ surjective ⇒ surjective.
  • f ∘ g injective ⇒ g injective. f need not be.
  • f ∘ g surjective ⇒ f surjective. g need not be, even if f is.
  • f(A ∪ B) = f(A) ∪ f(B). Always.
  • f(A ∩ B) ⊆ f(A) ∩ f(B). Equality ⇔ f injective.
  • f(A) ∖ f(B) ⊆ f(A ∖ B). Equality ⇔ f injective.
  • f−1 of union, intersection and complement: all three are exact, for every function.

Traps, with the counterexample that defuses each

  • "range = codomain" — false; f : {1,2} → {a,b,c} with f(1) = f(2) = a.
  • "one-to-one = one-to-one correspondence" — false; injective without onto.
  • "injective ⇒ surjective" — only when |A| = |B| and both are finite.
  • "f(A ∩ B) = f(A) ∩ f(B)" — false; two elements colliding from opposite sides.
  • "f ∘ g injective ⇒ f injective" — false; A = {1}, B = {x, y}, f(x) = f(y).
  • "f ∘ g onto and f onto ⇒ g onto" — false; C a single element.
  • "nm versus mn" — the exponent is the size of the domain.
  • "onto count = n! × C(m, n)" — only correct when m = n.
  • "x ↦ x² has an inverse" — not on ℝ; the domain must be restricted.

Six sentences to carry into the exam

A function is a relation with exactly one 1 in every row. The codomain is declared and the range is computed; surjectivity is their equality. Read the matrix by columns: at most one 1 is injective, at least one is surjective, exactly one is bijective. On finite sets of equal size the three are one property, and outside that case they are three. Composition passes injectivity from the left part and surjectivity from the right part, and nothing more. Images commute with union only, while preimages commute with union, intersection and complement.
