Topic and objective

TOPIC: What the definite integral is for. Signed area against geometric area, the area between two curves, the choice of the variable of integration, the accumulation function and its derivative, net change against total when a rate changes sign, the average value of a function, and probability read as an area under a density. This is the word Integration in the Calculus clause of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, carried into the Probability and Statistics clause of the same section.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a region or a rate, decide which of the six readings is being asked for; set up the integral with the right limits and the right variable; and check the answer against a bound before choosing an option.

I read the topic as one integral, six questions. The integral is the same object in all six; what changes is whether signs are kept, whether the limits are given or must be computed, and whether the interval is in x or in y. Every number on the page is computed by the model and checked against a closed form or a second route.

Prerequisites

  • Integration: techniques and definite integrals — the Riemann sum, the fundamental theorem, substitution, by parts, the p-test.
  • Limits, continuity and differentiation — what a derivative is, and why continuity is a hypothesis rather than decoration.
  • Probability and random variables — density, distribution function, expectation.

Approximate learning time: 50 to 70 minutes including the practice set. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. The integral is signed. The area is not. Both are questions you can be asked.
  2. Between two curves the height is upper minus lower, and the crossings are the limits.
  3. The variable is a choice; the number must not depend on it.
  4. A rate integrates to a net change; the absolute value of a rate integrates to a total.
  5. An average divides by the width, and a probability is an area of a density.

Intuition first: the integral is a machine with two settings

A definite integral adds up height times width along an interval. Two things about that sum decide everything in this lesson. First, the height carries a sign, so pieces below the axis subtract. Second, the width can be measured along x or along y, whichever makes the strips easier to describe. Everything else — areas between curves, net change, averages, probabilities — is one of those two settings applied to a particular question.

Educational interpretation The machine image is mine. What is not mine is the sign rule and the freedom to choose the variable: both are properties of the Riemann sum itself, and the model checks them numerically on this page rather than asserting them.

Formal definitions

Signed integral. The limit of the Riemann sums, with the sign of each height kept as it is.

Geometric area between a curve and an axis. The same limit with the absolute value of each height, equivalently the integral of the absolute value of the function.

Area between two curves. The integral of upper minus lower over the interval between two consecutive crossings, when the curves do not change order inside that interval.

Accumulation function. With the lower limit fixed at a, the function of x defined as the integral from a to x. Its derivative is the integrand, where the integrand is continuous.

Average value. One over b minus a times the integral from a to b.

Probability from a density. The probability that X lies between a and b is the integral of the density over that interval, which equals F of b minus F of a for the distribution function F.

Symbols, in words

  • the integral from a to b — add up over the interval whose ends are a and b; the ends are numbers, not variables.
  • dx — the width of one strip measured along the x axis. Written dy, the width is measured along y and every other symbol must change with it.
  • upper minus lower — the height of a strip between two curves, positive when the named upper curve really is above.
  • a crossing — a point where the two curves have the same height, found by solving their equation, not read off a sketch.
  • F of x — the accumulated area from a fixed start up to x; the distribution function in the probability setting.
  • f of x as a density — a non-negative function whose total integral is 1.

The three assumptions that are easy to lose

Upper minus lower needs an order. The formula is valid on an interval where one curve stays above the other. Where they cross, the interval must be split, or the two contributions cancel instead of adding.

The first half of the fundamental theorem needs continuity. At a jump the accumulation function is continuous but not differentiable, so the derivative statement fails exactly there.

The mean value theorem for integrals needs continuity too. Drop it and the promised point may not exist: a step function that is 0 on the left half and 2 on the right half has average 1 and never takes the value 1.

Why one integral answers six questions

A definite integral is a number attached to a function and an interval. The six questions of this lesson differ only in what is put into that slot. For a signed integral the function is the given curve. For a geometric area it is the absolute value of the curve. For an area between curves it is the difference of two curves, on each interval between crossings. For net change it is a rate, and for a total it is the absolute value of a rate. For an average it is the function divided by the width. For a probability it is a density, and the answer is a difference of two values of the accumulation function. Recognising which function and which interval is the entire skill; the integration itself is the previous lesson.

Five worked examples, every number computed

Each example below is evaluated by the model on this page twice: once by composite Simpson quadrature over the interval named, and once from the antiderivative. Where the two routes differ by more than a millionth the page says so; none of them does.

Example 1 — one region, two answers

Question. For the function x squared minus 2x on the interval from 0 to 3, find the signed integral and the geometric area.

Step 1, the crossings. Factor: x times (x minus 2). Zero at 0 and at 2.

Step 2, the pieces. From 0 to 2 the integral is minus 4/3, which is minus 1.333333. From 2 to 3 it is plus 4/3.

Step 3, signed. Minus 4/3 plus 4/3 is 0.000000.

Step 4, geometric. Take magnitudes first: 4/3 plus 4/3 is 8/3, which is 2.666667.

Example 2 — area between a line and a parabola

Question. The area enclosed by y equals x squared and y equals 2x plus 3.

Step 1, the crossings. x squared equals 2x plus 3 gives x squared minus 2x minus 3 equals 0, which factors as (x minus 3)(x plus 1). So minus 1 and 3.

Step 2, the order. The line is above the parabola throughout the open interval, so the height is 2x plus 3 minus x squared.

Step 3, integrate. The antiderivative is x squared plus 3x minus x cubed over 3.

Step 4, evaluate. At 3 it is 9; at minus 1 it is minus 5/3. The difference is 32/3, which is 10.666667. The model also gets this by subtracting the two separate integrals, and the two routes agree.

Example 3 — the same region sliced two ways

Question. The area between y equals x squared and y equals the square root of x on 0 to 1, by both variables.

Vertical. Height is root x minus x squared; the integral is 2/3 minus 1/3, which is 1/3 or 0.333333.

Horizontal. Read the curves backwards: the right boundary is x equals root y and the left boundary is x equals y squared. The integral in y of root y minus y squared is again 2/3 minus 1/3.

The check. The two values agree to twelve decimal places. Agreement is evidence that the two integrals describe the same region, not that the region was the right one, so the bounding curves still have to be read again.

Example 4 — displacement against distance

Question. A particle has velocity t squared minus 4t plus 3 for t from 0 to 4. Find the displacement and the distance travelled.

Step 1, the zeros. (t minus 1)(t minus 3): the velocity changes sign at 1 and at 3.

Step 2, the pieces. 0 to 1 gives plus 4/3; 1 to 3 gives minus 4/3; 3 to 4 gives plus 4/3.

Step 3, displacement. The signed total is 4/3, which is 1.333333.

Step 4, distance. Three pieces of magnitude 4/3, all positive, give 4.000000. The distance is three times the displacement here, which is why both numbers are plausible options.

Example 5 — an average value and its points

Question. Find the average value of sine on 0 to pi and every point where sine attains it.

Step 1, the integral. Sine integrates to 2 over that interval.

Step 2, divide by the width. 2 over pi, which is 0.636620.

Step 3, solve. Sine equals 2 over pi at 0.690107 and again at 2.451486.

Step 4, the theorem. The mean value theorem for integrals promised at least one point; here there are two. Solving and stopping at the first is the error, not finding two.

Where the standard recipe stops working

Each case below is a boundary of the method, not a curiosity. A question that sits on one of them is testing whether the recipe was understood or memorised.

Curves that cross more than twice

Between two curves that cross several times, the area is the sum of the pieces between consecutive crossings, each with the order that holds there. The signed integral of the difference over the whole interval is not that sum, because alternate pieces cancel.

On the page: y equals x cubed against y equals x over minus 1 to 1. The signed integral is 0.000000 and the enclosed area is 0.500000. The same pair of curves, two different numbers.

A region with no closed form in x

Some regions are describable in y and not in x, or the reverse, because the boundary curve cannot be written with x alone on one side without splitting into branches. The classic case is a curve that fails the vertical line test.

Reading a curve backwards is not always allowed: the inverse of y equals x squared does not exist on the whole line. On the region used in this lesson only the right branch lies inside, which is why the square root appears with no plus or minus in front of it.

An unbounded region with a finite area

A region can run to infinity and still enclose a finite area, and it can be bounded and still have an infinite area only if the function is unbounded. The two words unbounded and divergent are not synonyms.

The previous lesson in this repository covers this: the integral of 1 over x squared from 1 to infinity is 1, while the integral of 1 over x from 1 to infinity diverges. Nothing about the picture tells you which case you are in; the limit does.

The probability of a single point

For a continuous variable the probability of any one exact value is zero, because the area over a zero-width interval is zero. That does not make the value impossible; it makes the event measure zero.

The consequence for this lesson: a probability is always computed over an interval, and an answer of the form "the probability that X equals 2" is not a small positive number but exactly zero.

An average of a discontinuous function

The average value still exists for a step function, since the integral does. What fails is the guarantee that the function attains it.

Take a function that is 0 on the left half of the interval and 2 on the right half. Its average is 1, and it never equals 1. So a question that asks for the point guaranteed by the theorem has no answer here, and that is a property of the hypothesis, not a calculation error.

The counterexample that matters most

A function that is positive on part of the interval and negative on another can have a signed integral of zero, a signed integral smaller than one of its own pieces, or a signed integral of the opposite sign to the region a sketch suggests.

x squared minus 2x over 0 to 3 is the smallest version: signed 0.000000, geometric 2.666667, same region. Any argument of the form "the area must be positive so the integral must be positive" fails here.

Six beliefs that produce a wrong option

Each entry states the belief, says why it is wrong, and gives the smallest case that refutes it. The numbers are the ones the model computes on this page.

"The integral of a positive region must be positive"

Why it is wrong. The integrand, not the region, decides the sign of each strip. A function that dips below the axis contributes negatively even though the enclosed region has an area.

Counterexample. x squared minus 2x over 0 to 3: signed 0.000000, geometric 2.666667.

"Area between curves is the integral of the difference"

Why it is wrong as stated. It is right only on an interval where the order does not change. Across a crossing the pieces cancel, which is the opposite of what an area does.

Counterexample. y equals x cubed against y equals x over minus 1 to 1: the difference integrates to 0.000000, the area is 0.500000.

"The average value is the integral"

Why it is wrong. The average divides by the width of the interval. Leaving that division out inflates the answer by exactly the width.

Counterexample. Sine over 0 to pi: the integral is 2, the average is 0.636620. The factor between them is pi.

"Averaging the endpoint values gives the average"

Why it is wrong. Averaging the endpoints is the trapezoid rule, which estimates the integral. It has nothing to do with the average value of the function.

Counterexample. Sine on 0 to pi has endpoint values 0 and 0, so the endpoint average is 0, while the true average is 0.636620.

"The density value is the probability"

Why it is wrong. A density is a height. Only its integral over an interval is a probability, and a density may exceed 1 at a point without any contradiction.

Counterexample. A uniform density on 0 to 0.5 has constant height 2, yet every probability computed from it lies in 0 to 1.

"Distance and displacement are the same integral"

Why it is wrong. Displacement is the signed integral of velocity. Distance integrates the magnitude, which needs a split at every zero of the velocity.

Counterexample. Velocity t squared minus 4t plus 3 on 0 to 4: displacement 1.333333, distance 4.000000.

How the topic is examined

What follows is a description of question shapes that this topic supports, written from the syllabus wording and the structure of the material. No question below is presented as an official previous-year item, and no year, paper or question number is claimed. The practice set on the next tab is labelled original for the same reason.

Pattern 1 — two numbers from one region

The item gives a function that changes sign and asks either for the integral or for the enclosed area, with both numbers among the options. The work is identical; only the final step differs.

First move. Find the zeros, evaluate each piece, then read the question's noun before adding.

Pattern 2 — limits that must be computed

The item names two curves and says "the region enclosed", without giving the limits. The limits are the crossings, and they are part of the answer.

First move. Set the curves equal and factor. A quadratic gives two crossings; a cubic can give three, which means three limits and two pieces.

Pattern 3 — net against total

The item gives a rate that changes sign and asks for a net quantity, or for a total. The two answers differ by the amount that was undone.

First move. Find the zeros of the rate, then decide: signed integral for net, absolute value with a split for total. "Displacement", "net change" and "final value" all mean signed.

Pattern 4 — an average in disguise

The item asks for a mean value, an average rate, or a utilisation, without using the word integral. All three are one integral divided by a width.

First move. Identify the numerator as an integral and the denominator as the interval length. For a utilisation the integrand is 1 while busy and 0 while idle.

Pattern 5 — probability as an area

The item gives a density or a distribution function and asks for a probability of an interval, or gives a probability and asks for a parameter.

First move. Write it as F of b minus F of a. If only the density is given, integrate; if the distribution function is given, subtract. Either way the answer is a difference of two numbers, and it must lie in 0 to 1.

What a wrong option usually is

In this topic the distractors are systematic: the other reading of the same region, the answer without the width divisor, the value at the midpoint, the integral with the sign dropped, and the answer computed with the old limits.

Use. When two options differ by exactly the width of the interval, one of them is the average and the other the integral. That observation settles the question without any arithmetic.

NATOriginal practice question

The area enclosed between the curve y equals x squared and the line y equals 2x plus 3 is _______. Give the answer to three decimal places.

Answer: 10.667

The crossings solve x squared equals 2x plus 3, giving minus 1 and 3. The line is above the parabola there, so the area is the integral of 2x plus 3 minus x squared from minus 1 to 3, which is 32/3, about 10.666667.

Why 9 appears. That is the antiderivative evaluated at the upper limit only, with the lower evaluation forgotten. Why 6.667 appears. That is the same integral taken from 0 to 3, which drops the piece left of the axis. Why 0.667 appears. That is the signed integral of the difference with the signs left in over a symmetric interval.

The page computes the area both as one integral of the difference and as the difference of the two separate integrals; the two routes agree to nine places.

MCQOriginal practice question

A particle moves with velocity v(t) equal to t squared minus 4t plus 3 for t from 0 to 4. The distance travelled is

  1. 4/3
  2. 4
  3. 8/3
  4. 0

Answer: B

The velocity is zero at 1 and at 3, so the motion splits into three pieces: plus 4/3, minus 4/3, plus 4/3. Distance counts every piece positively, so the total is 4.

Why A is wrong. 4/3 is the displacement, the signed total, which is what the question did not ask for. Why C is wrong. 8/3 is the sum of the two positive pieces only, ignoring the backward part. Why D is wrong. 0 would be the signed total if the pieces happened to cancel, which they do not here.

The lesson's fifth scene prints all three pieces and both totals, and the model checks that the distance equals the integral of the absolute velocity.

MSQOriginal practice question

For the function x squared minus 2x on the interval from 0 to 3, which of the following are true?

  1. The signed integral is zero
  2. The geometric area is 8/3
  3. The function is negative on the whole open interval from 0 to 3
  4. The geometric area equals the magnitude of the signed integral

Answer: A and B

The pieces are minus 4/3 from 0 to 2 and plus 4/3 from 2 to 3, so the signed integral is 0 and the geometric area is 8/3, about 2.666667.

Why C is wrong. The function is positive after 2, since both factors x and x minus 2 are positive there. Why D is wrong. The magnitude of the signed integral is 0, and the geometric area is 8/3; they are equal only when the function does not change sign.

This is the closely confused pair of the topic: the signed integral and the geometric area agree exactly when the integrand keeps one sign on the interval.

NATOriginal practice question

For an exponential random variable with rate 0.5, the probability that the value lies between 1 and 3 is _______. Give three decimal places.

Answer: 0.383

The distribution function is 1 minus e to the minus x over 2, so the probability is F(3) minus F(1), which is e to the minus 0.5 minus e to the minus 1.5, about 0.383400.

Why 0.303 appears. That is the difference of the two density values, 0.5 e to the minus 1.5 minus 0.5 e to the minus 0.5 taken without the integral; a density is not a probability. Why 0.393 appears. That is the probability from 0 to 2 with the parameter treated as 1. Why 0.632 appears. That is one minus e to the minus 1, the probability from 0 to 2 when the rate is 0.5.

The page integrates the density by quadrature and subtracts the two distribution values; the two routes agree to nine places, and the total area under the density is checked to be 1.

MCQOriginal practice question

The average value of sine over the interval from 0 to pi is

  1. 0
  2. 2
  3. 2 over pi
  4. 1

Answer: C

The integral of sine from 0 to pi is 2 and the width is pi, so the average value is 2 over pi, about 0.636620.

Why A is wrong. That is the mean of the endpoint values, which is the trapezoid idea rather than the average value. Why B is wrong. That is the integral, with the division by the width left out. Why D is wrong. That is the height at the midpoint, pi over 2, not the average.

The lesson prints all four numbers side by side in scene 6, and checks that sine attains its average at 0.690107 and at 2.451486.

NATOriginal practice question

The area of the region bounded by y equals x squared and y equals the square root of x, computed with horizontal strips, is _______.

Answer: 0.333

With horizontal strips the right boundary is x equals root y and the left is x equals y squared, and y runs from 0 to 1. The integral of root y minus y squared is 2/3 minus 1/3, which is 1/3.

Why 0.667 appears. That is the integral of root y alone, with the left boundary forgotten. Why 0.5 appears. That is the area of the triangle between y equals x and the axes, a different region. Why 1 appears. That is the area of the unit square that contains the region.

The page computes the same region vertically and horizontally and checks that the two answers agree, which is the only real evidence that the reversed limits are right.

One page to revise from

Definitions, the decision procedure, the numbers this lesson established, and the traps — in the order you would use them under exam pressure.

The formulas, with their conditions

Signed integral. The limit of the Riemann sums, signs kept. No condition beyond integrability.

Geometric area. The integral of the absolute value. Requires splitting at every zero of the function.

Area between curves. The integral of upper minus lower. Requires an interval on which the order does not change.

Accumulation. F of x equals the integral from a to x; its derivative is f, at points where f is continuous.

Average value. The integral divided by b minus a.

Probability. F of b minus F of a, where F is the accumulation function of the density.

The decision procedure

  1. Name the type: signed, geometric, between curves, net change, total, average, probability.
  2. For an area, solve for the crossings; they are the limits and the split points.
  3. Decide the variable: dx with x limits, or dy with y limits and curves written as x of y.
  4. Write the height or width as a difference, with the order that holds on that piece.
  5. Integrate, then bound the answer before looking at the options.

The numbers to remember from this page

x squared minus 2x over 0 to 3: signed 0.000000, geometric 2.666667.

x above x squared on 0 to 1: 0.166667. x squared against 2x plus 3: crossings minus 1 and 3, area 10.666667.

x cubed against x on minus 1 to 1: signed 0.000000, area 0.500000.

Velocity t squared minus 4t plus 3 on 0 to 4: displacement 1.333333, distance 4.000000.

Sine on 0 to pi: integral 2, average 0.636620, points 0.690107 and 2.451486.

Exponential with rate 0.5 between 1 and 3: 0.383400. Normal between minus 1 and 1: 0.682689. Rate 2 plus sine over one period: total 12.566371, average 2.000000.

The five traps, in one line each

The integral is signed, the area is not. A crossing must split an area and must not split a signed integral. An average divides by the width. A density is not a probability. A dy integral takes y limits.

Quick self-check

Can you state, without looking, why the geometric area of x squared minus 2x over 0 to 3 is 8/3 while its integral is 0?

Can you explain why the area between y equals x cubed and y equals x over minus 1 to 1 is a half, given that the difference integrates to zero?

Can you say why a distance can never be smaller than the magnitude of the corresponding displacement?
