Topic and objective

TOPIC: Linear algebra — matrices, determinants, systems of linear equations, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, LU decomposition. Section 1, Engineering Mathematics, GATE 2027 CS syllabus.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read a matrix as a linear function and a determinant as the factor by which it scales area, with a sign. Compute rank as a count of pivots and use rank plus nullity equals the number of columns. Decide whether a system has none, one or infinitely many solutions by comparing two ranks. Factor a matrix into a lower and an upper triangular part, say when that is possible, and solve with the two cheap passes. Find eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial, read them off triangular and all-ones shapes without expanding, and check any answer against the trace and the determinant.

I read this clause as four quantities and the questions they answer: the determinant answers "is it invertible", the rank answers "how many solutions", the eigenvalues answer "what does the map do", and LU answers "how is it computed". Almost every GATE item here asks for one of those four on a small matrix.

Prerequisites

  • Arithmetic with integers and fractions, including solving two equations in two unknowns by substitution. Everything here is that, done in an organised way.
  • Recurrence relations — for the one place a characteristic polynomial is solved as a polynomial, and for the fact that a degree n polynomial has n roots over the complex numbers.
  • Probability and random variables — covariance matrices are symmetric matrices, and the spectral theorem in this lesson is what makes them diagonalisable.
  • No programming, no software. Every number on this page is computed in the page's own model.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A matrix is a function. Ax mixes the columns of A, with the entries of x as the amounts.
  • The determinant is an area factor. Sign is orientation, zero is collapse, and it multiplies over products.
  • Rank counts surviving directions. Rank plus nullity equals the number of columns.
  • Two ranks decide a system. Equal ranks mean consistent; the shortfall from the column count is the number of free variables.
  • Eigenvalues are the stretch factors. They add to the trace and multiply to the determinant, which is a free check.

Intuition first: a machine that moves the whole plane

Imagine a sheet of rubber with a dot pattern printed on it. A matrix is an instruction for moving every point of the sheet at once: stretch it, squash it, turn it, or slide it through itself. Two facts about that motion turn out to answer almost every exam question. How much bigger did areas get? That is the determinant, with a minus sign if the pattern came out mirrored. How many independent directions are still distinguishable afterwards? That is the rank; if the sheet was squashed flat, some directions have collapsed onto others and information is lost.

The rest follows. A system of equations asks whether a particular target point can be reached at all, which is a question about the image of the sheet. An eigenvector is a direction that was not turned, only stretched. And LU decomposition is bookkeeping: it records the steps of the squashing so they can be replayed cheaply. Educational interpretation The rubber sheet carries the geometry honestly; it does not carry the arithmetic, which is why every formula below is stated separately.

Formal vocabulary, every symbol in words

Matrix — a rectangular array of numbers with m rows and n columns, standing for a function from n-number columns to m-number columns.

Ax — the product, read as x1 times column one plus x2 times column two, and so on.

Transpose AT — rows and columns exchanged. Symmetric — A equals AT.

Identity I — ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere; the map that changes nothing.

Singular — having no inverse; equivalently determinant zero, or rank below n, or zero as an eigenvalue.

The determinant, stated precisely

For a square matrix, the determinant is the unique function of the entries that is linear in each row, changes sign when two rows are exchanged, and equals one for the identity. For a 2×2 with rows (a, b) and (c, d) it is ad − bc.

det(AB) = det(A)·det(B)The area factor of a composition is the product of the area factors. This is why the rule is a product rule and not a sum rule.

Consequences used in this lesson. A row swap negates; adding a multiple of one row to another changes nothing; scaling a row by k multiplies the determinant by k; a triangular matrix has the product of its diagonal.

Rank, nullity and the one identity

Column space — every vector Ax that the map can produce. Rank — its dimension, equal to the number of pivots after elimination.

Null space — every x with Ax = 0. Nullity — its dimension, equal to the number of free columns.

rank(A) + nullity(A) = nn is the number of columns, which is the number of inputs. Every input direction either survives to the output or is crushed to zero, and the two counts add up.

Mathematical derivation Eliminate to echelon form: each pivot column contributes one dimension to the image, each non-pivot column contributes one free parameter to the null space, and the columns are counted exactly once.

Systems of equations: the rank test

Write the system as Ax = b, with A of shape m×n. Append b as an extra column and compare ranks.

rank A < rank [A|b] — no solution. The target is outside the image.

equal ranks, equal to n — exactly one solution. No free variable.

equal ranks, less than n — infinitely many, with n − rank A free parameters.

A homogeneous system (b = 0) is always consistent, because zero is always reachable. It has a non-zero solution exactly when rank A < n.

LU decomposition, stated precisely

A factorisation A = L·U with L unit lower triangular (ones on the diagonal, multipliers below) and U upper triangular (the pivots on the diagonal).

Exists and is unique exactly when every leading principal minor — the determinant of the top-left k×k block, for k = 1 to n − 1 — is non-zero.

With row exchanges every invertible matrix has P·A = L·U for some permutation matrix P.

Solving — L y = b by forward substitution, then U x = y by back substitution. Cost n³/3 multiplications to factor, about n² per right-hand side.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Eigenvector — a non-zero v with A v = λ v for some number λ. Eigenvalue — that λ: the factor by which the direction is stretched, flipped if negative, crushed if zero.

det(A − λI) = 0A − λI must be singular for a non-zero v to exist, and singular means determinant zero. Expanding gives the characteristic polynomial of degree n, with n roots over the complex numbers.

Two checks. The eigenvalues add to the trace (the sum of the diagonal) and multiply to the determinant.

Diagonalisable — A = P D P−1 with D diagonal, which needs n independent eigenvectors. Distinct eigenvalues always supply them; a repeated one may not.

The three equivalences worth memorising, for a square n×n matrix

det(A) ≠ 0 ⇔ rank(A) = n ⇔ elimination gives n pivots ⇔ zero is not an eigenvalue ⇔ A is invertibleFive statements, one fact. Each is cheapest on different data: the determinant on a 2×2, the pivots on a 3×3, the eigenvalue when the eigenvalues are already asked for.

Mathematical derivation Each equivalence is checked on this page by computing all of them for the same matrices and comparing the answers, rather than by assertion.

Example 1 — GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q52, the largest eigenvalue

Consider the 4×4 matrix with 0 on the diagonal and 1 everywhere else. The largest eigenvalue is ______.

  1. Every row sums to 3, so the all-ones vector (1,1,1,1) satisfies A v = 3v. Each output entry is the sum of the other three inputs, which is 3 when all four are 1.
  2. Write A as J − I, where J is all ones. J has rank 1, trace 4, so its eigenvalues are 4, 0, 0, 0.
  3. Subtracting I shifts every eigenvalue down by 1: 3, −1, −1, −1. A − I and A have the same eigenvectors.
  4. Answer: 3. The trace check holds: 3 − 1 − 1 − 1 = 0.

Trap. Answering 4 is answering for J, not for J − I. The subtraction is the whole question. Some archives index this item as Q21 of the same paper.

Example 2 — GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q2, the product of the eigenvalues

The product of all eigenvalues of the matrix with rows (1,2,3), (4,5,6), (7,8,9) is: −1, 0, 1 or 2.

  1. The product of the eigenvalues is the determinant. The constant term of the characteristic polynomial is ±det, and the roots multiply to it.
  2. Row 2 is the average of rows 1 and 3: (1+7)/2 = 4, (2+8)/2 = 5, (3+9)/2 = 6. So the rows are dependent and the determinant is 0.
  3. Rank is 2, so nullity is 1, so some non-zero vector maps to zero. That vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0, which also forces the product to be 0.
  4. Answer: 0. The other two eigenvalues are 16.1168 and −1.1168, and they multiply to zero because of the third.

Why the expansion is a trap. Expanding a 3×3 determinant costs a minute and invites an arithmetic slip. Recognising the arithmetic progression in each column costs five seconds.

Example 3 — GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q39, the wide matrix

Let A be any n×m matrix with m > n. Which statements about Ax = 0 are true?

  1. rank A ≤ min(n, m) = n, because there are only n rows. Rank cannot exceed the smaller side of the shape.
  2. nullity = m − rank A ≥ m − n. Rank-nullity, with the column count m.
  3. So there exist at least m − n independent solutions. That is exactly option A, and "at least" survives the case rank A < n.
  4. "Exactly m − n" fails, because the rank can be lower. With m = 4 and n = 3 the nullity could be 2 or 3, not necessarily 1.

Answer: only the "at least" statement. The question tests the quantifier, not the arithmetic: "any" makes it a for-all claim, so one counterexample kills an option.

Example 4 — GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q47, the row swap

Let A be an n×n real matrix and B be obtained from A by swapping two rows. Which statements are true?

  1. det B = −det A. A swap is an elementary operation with determinant −1, and determinants multiply.
  2. If A is invertible then B is invertible. det B = −det A, and a non-zero number stays non-zero when negated.
  3. Symmetry is not preserved: (1,2 / 2,3) swapped becomes (2,3 / 1,2), not symmetric. A concrete 2×2 is enough to refute a for-all claim.
  4. Trace is not preserved: the diagonal entries move. Swapping rows 1 and 3 in a matrix whose diagonal sums to 0 can give a non-zero sum.

Answer: the first two statements only. The question separates the properties a swap controls (determinant sign) from the ones it does not (symmetry, trace).

Example 5 — an LU factorisation, step by step (original)

Factor A with rows (0,2,1), (1,1,1), (2,1,3). The first pivot is 0, so plain LU is impossible; exchange rows 1 and 2 first.

  1. Leading minors are 0, −2, −3. The first is 0, which is exactly the condition that stops Doolittle elimination.
  2. Exchange rows 1 and 2. Now P·A has rows (1,1,1), (0,2,1), (2,1,3). The permutation is one swap, so det P = −1.
  3. Eliminate: row 3 minus 2×row 1 gives (0,−1,1); then row 3 plus 0.5×row 2 gives (0,0,1.5). The multipliers are 2 and −0.5, and they are stored in L.
  4. L has rows (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (2,−0.5,1) and U has rows (1,1,1), (0,2,1), (0,0,1.5). The product is P·A exactly, in rational arithmetic.
  5. det A = (−1)·(1·2·1.5) = −3. The sign comes from the swap; the magnitude is the product of the pivots.

Check. Direct expansion of the 3×3 gives −3 as well, and the page compares the two in code rather than by hand.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Each card states the condition a rule needs, and what happens when it fails. These are the cases that separate a correct answer from an almost-correct one.

A real matrix need not have a real eigenvalue

The quarter turn with rows (0,−1) and (1,0) sends every vector to one perpendicular to it. Its trace is 0 and its determinant is 1, so the characteristic polynomial is λ² + 1, whose roots are i and −i.

What saves you. Symmetry. For a real symmetric matrix the discriminant is (a−d)² + 4b², which cannot be negative, so both eigenvalues are real. A question that says "symmetric" is telling you the roots exist.

A repeated eigenvalue does not mean defective

The identity has eigenvalue 1 twice, and two independent eigenvectors, so it is already diagonal. The matrix with rows (1,1) and (0,1) also has 1 twice, but A − I has rank 1, so its nullity is 1 and there is only one eigenvector direction.

The test. Compare the geometric multiplicity (nullity of A − λI) with the algebraic one (the root's multiplicity). Equality for every eigenvalue is exactly diagonalisability.

A non-zero determinant does not guarantee plain LU

The matrix with rows (0,2,1), (1,1,1), (2,1,3) has determinant −3, so it is invertible. But its first leading minor is 0, so elimination without a row exchange stops at the first pivot.

The repair. A permutation. P·A = L·U always exists for an invertible matrix, and the determinant picks up a factor of −1 per swap, which is how the pivot product still gives det A.

Zero is a legal eigenvalue; the zero vector is not an eigenvector

A singular matrix has eigenvalue 0, with any non-zero null vector as its eigenvector. But the zero vector is excluded by definition, because A·0 = λ·0 for every λ and it would tell you nothing.

Why it matters. "Zero is an eigenvalue" and "A has a non-trivial null space" and "det A = 0" are three spellings of one fact. Any of them can be the intended route.

Consistency depends on b, not on the matrix alone

The matrix with rows (1,2) and (2,4) is consistent with b = (3,6), because the second equation is twice the first, and inconsistent with b = (3,7), because it is not.

Consequence. rank(A) alone never decides consistency; you need rank of the augmented matrix. Same matrix, two different right-hand sides, two different verdicts.

rank plus nullity counts columns, not rows

For a 3×2 matrix with rows (1,2), (2,4), (3,6), the rank is 1 and the nullity is 1, and 1 + 1 = 2 is the number of columns. Using the number of rows, 3, would give a wrong nullity of 2.

For a tall matrix the extra rows are redundant, so they add nothing to the rank and nothing to the count. Write the shape down first and this error disappears.

Five beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each card gives the belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule to use instead. These are drawn from the shapes of wrong options in past papers, not from a survey of learners.

1. "A determinant is linear, so det(A+B) = det A + det B"

Why it tempts. The determinant is linear in each row separately, and "linear" sounds like it should distribute.

Refutation. With A = B = I in two dimensions, det(2I) = 4 while 1 + 1 = 2. The determinant is multiplicative, not additive.

Correct rule. det(AB) = det A · det B, and there is no general formula for a sum.

2. "Rank adds up too"

Why it tempts. Rank is a dimension, and dimensions of subspaces usually add when the subspaces are independent.

Refutation. The identity and minus the identity both have full rank 2, and their sum is the zero matrix, rank 0. So rank 2 + rank 2 can give rank 0.

Correct rule. rank(A+B) ≤ rank A + rank B, and the inequality can be strict either way.

3. "Eigenvalues of a sum are the sums of eigenvalues"

Why it tempts. Trace is linear, and the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, so the two ideas get merged.

Refutation. Take A with eigenvalues 1, 2 and B with eigenvalues 3, 4 arranged differently; A + B generally has neither 4 nor 6 as eigenvalues, and its trace is still 10.

Correct rule. True only when A and B share the same eigenvectors. The always-safe version is A + cI, which shifts eigenvalues by c.

4. "Elimination gives the determinant directly"

Why it tempts. Elimination is the standard way to compute everything else, and the pivot product does look like a determinant.

Refutation. Row 2 + 2·row 1 leaves the determinant unchanged, but swapping two rows negates it and scaling a row by 3 triples it. Ignoring that bookkeeping gives the right size and the wrong sign.

Correct rule. Rank is untouched by all three operations; the determinant is not.

5. "A repeated eigenvalue means it cannot be diagonalised"

Why it tempts. The standard counterexample to diagonalisability is a matrix with a repeated eigenvalue, so the two get welded together.

Refutation. The identity has 1 repeated n times and is already diagonal. The defective example has 1 repeated twice and offers one direction.

Correct rule. Count eigenvectors, not roots. Defective means geometric multiplicity falls below algebraic.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

Four pairs get confused, and each is settled by writing down a definition: rank against determinant (a count of directions against an area factor), algebraic against geometric multiplicity, consistent against invertible (a property of the pair A, b against a property of A alone), and LU against PA = LU (no row exchange against row exchange allowed). In each pair the two members differ by one condition, and the question always states which one applies.

How this clause gets tested, and what each pattern demands

Four patterns cover the items in this clause. Each is listed with the quantity it computes, because the quantity is what the trap attacks.

Pattern 1 — a numeric answer about one number

Shape. A small matrix, one quantity asked: a determinant, a rank, an eigenvalue, a product of eigenvalues, a nullity. The answer is an integer or a short decimal.

Procedure. Read the shape; look for structure before expanding — equal rows, an arithmetic progression, a triangular form, a constant row sum.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q52 (largest eigenvalue 3) and GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q2 (product of eigenvalues 0).

Pattern 2 — a statement set about a system

Shape. Three or four statements about Ax = 0 or Ax = b for a general shape, and you select the true ones.

Procedure. Convert each statement into a rank statement. "At least m − n solutions" is nullity ≥ m − n; "exactly" is an equality that fails when the rank is short.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q39, answer: the "at least" statement only.

Pattern 3 — a statement set about an operation

Shape. A matrix is modified by a row swap, a scaling or an addition, and four properties are offered: determinant, invertibility, symmetry, trace, rank.

Procedure. For each property ask whether the operation touches it. A swap negates the determinant, keeps invertibility and rank, and destroys symmetry and trace.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q47, answer: determinant negates and invertibility survives.

Pattern 4 — eigenvalues by structure

Shape. A matrix with an obvious structure — triangular, all ones, all ones minus identity, a projection, a permutation, a rotation — and a question about its eigenvalues or their sum or product.

Procedure. Name the structure, recall its eigenvalue pattern, and confirm with trace and determinant. Only expand when nothing is recognised.

Seen in. GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q52, where recognising J − I replaces the whole expansion.

The four-step procedure, and where each step can go wrong

1. Shape. Write m and n. Rank is capped by the smaller. Fails when a tall matrix is treated as if it could have full column rank.

2. Invertibility. For a square matrix, compare determinant, pivots and zero as an eigenvalue. Fails when a rectangular matrix is handed a determinant.

3. Solution count. Compare rank A with rank [A|b], then subtract from the number of columns. Fails when rows are subtracted instead of columns.

4. Polynomial. Expand det(A − λI), solve, and check the sum against the trace and the product against the determinant. Fails when the check is skipped and a sign error survives.

Two of these four patterns are one-mark freebies — take them first

A structural eigenvalue question and an operation-statement question need no arithmetic beyond a definition, and both are answerable in under a minute. The wide-matrix statement set rewards one line of rank-nullity. Only a numeric determinant on an unstructured 3×3 costs real time, and even there the structure is usually planted. In a paper with a per-question average near two minutes, taking the cheap ones first is worth more than perfecting the expensive one.

NATGATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q52

Consider the 4×4 matrix with 0 on the diagonal and 1 in every other position. The largest eigenvalue of this matrix is ______.

Answer: 3

The matrix is J − I, where J is the 4×4 all-ones matrix. J has rank 1 and trace 4, so its eigenvalues are 4, 0, 0, 0. Subtracting I shifts each down by one: 3, −1, −1, −1. The largest is 3.

Why 4 appears. That is the eigenvalue of J. Why the row-sum shortcut works. Every row sums to 3, so the all-ones vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3 — and since the trace is 0, the remaining three must be −1 each.

MCQGATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q2

The product of all eigenvalues of the matrix with rows (1, 2, 3), (4, 5, 6), (7, 8, 9) is:

  1. −1
  2. 0
  3. 1
  4. 2

Answer: B — 0

The product of the eigenvalues is the determinant. Row 2 is the average of rows 1 and 3, so the rows are dependent and the determinant is 0. Equivalently the rank is 2, the nullity is 1, and 0 is an eigenvalue.

Why −1 tempts. It is the determinant of a different 3×3 that also looks patterned. Why the expansion is a trap. It costs a minute and invites a slip; the arithmetic progression down each column settles it in five seconds.

MSQGATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q39

Let A be any n×m matrix, where m > n. Which of the following statements is/are TRUE about the system of linear equations Ax = 0?

  1. There exist at least m − n linearly independent solutions to this system.
  2. There exist m − n linearly independent vectors such that every solution is a linear combination of these vectors.
  3. There exists a non-zero solution in which at least m − n variables are 0.
  4. There exists a solution in which at least n variables are non-zero.

Answer: A only

rank A ≤ n, so nullity = m − rank A ≥ m − n, which gives option A. Option B fails because the nullity can be larger than m − n, so "exactly m − n spanning vectors" is not guaranteed. Options C and D are claims about individual solutions that a particular matrix can defeat.

The misconception. Reading "any" as "some". The word makes every option a for-all claim, and a single counterexample removes it.

MSQGATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q47

Let A be an n×n matrix over the real numbers, and let B be a matrix obtained from A by swapping two rows. Which of the following statements is/are TRUE?

  1. The determinant of B is the negative of the determinant of A.
  2. If A is invertible, then B is also invertible.
  3. If A is symmetric, then B is also symmetric.
  4. If the trace of A is zero, then the trace of B is also zero.

Answer: A and B

A swap is an elementary row operation whose matrix has determinant −1, and B = E·A, so det B = −det A. A non-zero determinant stays non-zero under negation, so invertibility survives. Symmetry and trace do not survive, because the diagonal entries move.

Smallest refutations. Symmetric (1,2 / 2,3) becomes (2,3 / 1,2), which is not symmetric. A matrix with diagonal 1, −1, 0 and rows swapped can have trace 0 before and non-zero after.

MCQOriginal practice

For which of these matrices does an LU decomposition without any row exchange exist?

  1. The 2×2 with rows (0, 1) and (1, 0).
  2. The 2×2 with rows (1, 2) and (2, 4).
  3. The 2×2 with rows (2, 1) and (4, 3).
  4. All three.

Answer: C only

(2,1 / 4,3) has leading minors 2 and 2·3 − 1·4 = 2, both non-zero, so the decomposition exists and is unique: L = (1,0 / 2,1) and U = (2,1 / 0,1). (0,1 / 1,0) has first minor 0, so the first pivot is zero. (1,2 / 2,4) has minors 1 and 0, so it fails at the second pivot.

Why "all three" tempts. Every invertible matrix has a PA = LU. The question asks for the stronger A = LU, which needs every leading minor non-zero.

NATOriginal practice

A is a 3×5 matrix of rank 2. How many free variables does the homogeneous system Ax = 0 have, and how many independent solutions does its solution space have?

Answer: 3 and 3

nullity = columns − rank = 5 − 2 = 3. The solution space has dimension 3, so there are three free parameters and three independent solutions. The number of rows, 3, plays no part.

Why 2 appears. That is the rank, not the nullity. Why 1 appears. That would be right for a 3×3 matrix of rank 2, where the column count is 3.

The revision sheet: what to write on one page

Definitions first, then the identities, then the shapes worth recognising, then the traps. If you can reproduce this page from memory, the clause is covered.

Definitions in one line each

  • Matrix — a function from n-number columns to m-number columns.
  • Determinant — the area (or volume) scaling factor, signed; zero means singular.
  • Rank — dimension of the image; the number of pivots.
  • Nullity — dimension of the set of vectors sent to zero.
  • Eigenvector — a non-zero v with A v = λ v; eigenvalue — that λ.
  • Diagonalisable — A = P D P−1 with n independent eigenvectors.
  • LU — A = L U, unit lower triangular times upper triangular.

The identities, all of them used here

det(AB) = det A · det B
rank + nullity = number of columns
det(A − λI) = 0 gives the eigenvalues
Σλ = trace, Πλ = det
A = P D P−1 ⇒ Ak = P Dk P−1The first two carry most of the marks. The last three are the checks: the trace and determinant test an eigenvalue answer, and the power rule turns a matrix power into scalar powers.

Row operations. Swap: det × (−1). Scale a row by k: det × k. Add a multiple of a row to another: det unchanged. Rank: unchanged by all three.

Shapes worth recognising on sight

  • Triangular — eigenvalues are the diagonal; determinant is their product.
  • All ones — eigenvalues n and n − 1 zeros; rank 1.
  • All ones minus I — n − 1 once, −1 repeated n − 1 times.
  • Diagonal or permutation — eigenvalues read directly.
  • Idempotent A² = A — eigenvalues 0 or 1. Nilpotent — all zero.
  • Rotation — eigenvalues e±iθ; real only at 0° and 180°.
  • Symmetric — real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors.

The traps, in the order they cost marks

  • det(A + B) is not det A + det B.
  • rank(A + B) is not rank A + rank B.
  • eigenvalues of A + B are not the sums.
  • rank + nullity uses columns, not rows.
  • Consistency needs the augmented rank, not rank A alone.
  • Elimination changes the determinant but not the rank.
  • Repeated eigenvalue is not the same as defective.

Five quick self-questions, answers in your head

1. A 4×4 matrix has rank 3. What is its determinant, and what is its nullity? (0 and 1.)

2. A 2×2 matrix has trace 5 and determinant 6. What are the eigenvalues? (2 and 3, since they must add to 5 and multiply to 6.)

3. A 3×5 system is consistent with rank 2. How many free variables? (3, because 5 − 2 = 3.)

4. A matrix has eigenvalues 2, 2 and geometric multiplicity 1 for that value. Is it diagonalisable? (No: it needs two independent directions for the double root.)

5. A 3×3 matrix has first leading minor 0 and determinant 5. Does A = LU exist without a swap? (No: the first pivot is zero. PA = LU does exist.)

What is computed rather than cited

Every number on the canvas and in the worked examples is computed by this page's model: determinants, ranks and the LU factorisation in exact rational arithmetic, eigenvalues in floating point with a tolerance of 1e-9, and every structural claim re-checked against the identity it must satisfy — P·A = L·U, rank plus nullity, trace and determinant of the eigenvalue set, and the reconstruction P·D·P−1. The count of passing checks is printed under the canvas. No figure in this lesson was copied from an answer key without being recomputed.
