Topic and objective

TOPIC: Boolean algebra and minimisation — Boolean functions and their truth tables; the identities and the principle of duality; algebraic minimisation by absorption and consensus; minterms, maxterms and the two canonical forms; the Karnaugh map with its Gray-code labels, wrapping edges and power-of-two blocks; don't-care conditions; prime implicants, essential prime implicants and the minimal cover; minimisation of the complement to obtain a product of sums; and the tabular (Quine–McCluskey) method.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a Boolean function as a truth table, as a minterm list, as a maxterm list, as an expression or as a Karnaugh map, produce its minimal sum of products and its minimal product of sums, say how many prime implicants it has and which of them are essential, and justify every step by a rule rather than by pattern-matching. In particular: count prime implicants when the question asks only for the count, use a don't-care as an optional one, and read a product of sums off the zeros.

I am treating this as the whole of the syllabus item "Boolean algebra and minimization — algebraic technique, Karnaugh map, tabular method", and as the algebra that the next two Digital Logic lessons (combinational circuits, sequential circuits) build on. It stops at the two-level minimisation problem: multiplexers, adders, flip-flops and counters belong to the circuits lesson, and number representation belongs to the one before it.

Prerequisites

  • Propositional logic: truth tables, negation, conjunction, disjunction, De Morgan's laws. Boolean algebra is the same algebra read as circuits instead of as propositions — and that is not a coincidence, it is the same lattice.
  • Binary numbers: reading a four-bit string as a number, and knowing that the leftmost bit is the most significant. Nothing beyond counting to sixteen.
  • Optional: partial orders and lattices. The set of Boolean functions of n variables, ordered by "implies", is a lattice, and the two operations are its meet and join. The lesson does not depend on this, but it explains why the identities are the ones they are.
  • No calculus, no matrices, no programming.

The five ideas

  1. A Boolean function is its truth table; an expression is one of many descriptions of it. Minimisation is a search over descriptions.
  2. Two cells that differ in one bit merge into one product with one literal fewer. Every rule in this topic is that sentence applied repeatedly.
  3. A prime implicant is maximal, not useful. A question that asks for the count wants maximality; a question that asks for the expression wants a cover.
  4. A don't-care may be used or ignored, so it can enlarge a block; but it can never make a block essential, because essentiality is about ones.
  5. Grouping the zeros and inverting gives the product of sums. The map is one method; the tabular method is the same method without a picture, and it is the one that scales.

Conventions used throughout

AND is written as a product (xy), OR as a sum (x + y), and NOT as a prime (x'). A literal is one variable plain or primed, so x and x' are two literals. Variables are ordered as written, left to right, with the leftmost as the most significant bit of the minterm index: for f(P,Q,R,S), minterm 5 is P'QRS'. A product term is a product of literals, a sum term is a sum of literals, SOP is a sum of products and POS is a product of sums. Σ m(…) lists the minterms where the function is 1; Π M(…) lists the maxterms where it is 0; d(…) lists don't-cares. "Minimal" always means: fewest product terms first, then fewest literals.

1. Intuition — one column, many descriptions

Think of a lift that must open its doors. Four sensors feed a controller, and the controller's job is written down as a truth table: for each of the sixteen sensor patterns, doors open or doors stay shut. The table is the specification and it is not negotiable. What is negotiable is the circuit. One engineer builds it from the table directly and needs sixteen terms; another notices that the same column can be produced by three terms. Both circuits behave identically on all sixteen patterns — but one is cheaper, faster and cooler.

Educational interpretation The lift is an analogy for orientation. Everything after this card is about the table and the expressions, and no result depends on the analogy.

2. Formal definition

f : {0,1}n → {0,1}A Boolean function of n variables. The domain has 2n elements — one for each assignment of the inputs — and the codomain has two, so there are exactly 2(2n) different functions of n variables.
x + 0 = x    x · 1 = x    x + x' = 1    x · x' = 0The four identities that make the algebra Boolean. The others — commutative, associative, distributive, absorption, De Morgan — are consequences of these and of the definitions of the operations by truth table.

Verified fact The operations are defined by their tables, not by a formula: x · y = 1 exactly when both are 1, and x + y = 1 exactly when at least one is 1. This is why every identity can be settled by writing four rows.

3. Visual understanding — one merge, one literal

Two cells that differ in exactly one variable can be merged, and the merged product drops that variable. xy'z + xyz = xz: the pair is 1 whenever x and z are 1, whichever way y goes, so y is irrelevant and disappears.

2k cells ⟹ n − k literalsA block of 2k cells that is a full sub-cube keeps n − k literals. One cell keeps n, a pair keeps n − 1, a quad keeps n − 2, an octet keeps n − 3.

Every minimisation method in this lesson — algebraic, map, tabular — is a search for the largest blocks of this kind.

4a. Boundaries — what the methods assume

  • Two levels. "Minimal" here means minimal among two-level AND-OR (or OR-AND) circuits. A multi-level circuit can use fewer gates; that is a different problem, and GATE's minimisation questions mean two levels.
  • Product terms. Every method assumes the answer is a sum of products. That is not a restriction: every Boolean function can be written that way, which is what canonical SOP proves.
  • The map is limited by the eye. It is drawn for up to four variables comfortably, and for five or six with two or four maps. Beyond that, only the tabular method works — and it is the reason the 2027 syllabus names it.
  • Cost model. Fewest terms, then fewest literals. A question that says "minimum number of gates" is counting something slightly different, and the count includes the inverter stage only if the question says complements are not available.

5. Worked example in one line each, with the reason

Canonical, then minimal. For f(a,b,c) = Σ m(1,3,4,5,6) the canonical sum of products is a'b'c + a'bc + ab'c' + ab'c + abc' — five terms, thirteen literals. The minimal form is a'c + ac' + b'c — three terms, six literals. Why the second is legitimate: every one of the five minterms is inside one of the three terms, and no term contains a zero-row.

Count prime implicants, not blocks. For f(w,x,y,z) = Σ(0,2,4,5,6,10) there are three prime implicants: w'z', w'xy', x'yz'. The pairs (0,2), (0,4), (2,6), (4,6) all exist as blocks and none of them is prime, because the quad w'z' already contains them. Why the distinction earns marks: GATE 2015 Set 3 Q43 asks for exactly this count, and its key is 3.

A don't-care that pays. For the 2018 function, minimising with the five don't-cares available gives three terms and seven literals; ignoring them gives three terms and nine literals. Why: the don't-cares at 8 and 10 enlarge Q'S' from a pair into a quad, and the one at 3 enlarges nothing but costs nothing.

6. Edge cases that decide marks

  • Variable order. Σ m indices depend on the order the variables are written. Read f(P,Q,R,S) with P as the most significant bit; reversing the order permutes every index and turns a correct answer into a wrong one.
  • A function that is all ones. Its canonical POS is the empty product, written 1; its minimal SOP is the single literal-free term 1. There are no maxterms to list, because there are no zero-rows.
  • A function that is all zeros. Symmetrically, the canonical SOP is the empty sum, 0, and the minimal POS is 0.
  • Cyclic maps. Some functions have no essential prime implicant at all — for example Σ m(0,1,2,5,6,7) in three variables has six prime implicants, none essential, and every minimal cover needs three of them. "Take the essentials" produces nothing here.
  • Two minimal answers. A minimal expression is not always unique. Two different covers can tie on terms and on literals; both are correct, and a multiple-select question may list both.
  • Don't-cares in the answer. A don't-care you covered is a 1 in your circuit; one you did not cover is a 0. Both are correct, but the two circuits differ on those rows, and a question that asks what the circuit outputs on a don't-care row is asking about your expression, not about the specification.

7. The one-sentence summary of levels 1–6

A Boolean function is a truth table; every expression for it is a choice of blocks of 2k cells; the map and the table are two ways of finding the maximal blocks, the essentials are the ones forced by a one that nobody else covers, and the answer is the cheapest cover — with don't-cares free to use and never able to force anything.

Example 1 — from an expression to its minterms, then to a minimum

Question. For f(a,b,c) = a'c + ac' + b'c, list the minterms, count the prime implicants, and name the essential ones.

Minterms. a'c is 1 where a=0, c=1, which is minterms 1 (001) and 3 (011). ac' is 1 where a=1, c=0: minterms 4 (100) and 6 (110). b'c is 1 where b=0, c=1: minterms 1 and 5 (101). The union is {1,3,4,5,6}.

Prime implicants. Four: a'c over {1,3}, ac' over {4,6}, b'c over {1,5}, ab' over {4,5}. None of the four can be enlarged, because minterms 0, 2 and 7 are zeros, and 7 blocks every quad.

Essential. Minterm 3 is covered only by a'c, and minterm 6 only by ac'. So the essentials are a'c and ac', and minterm 5 is covered by both b'c and ab' — a choice, not a necessity. The page computes all four prime implicants and both essentials in code.

Example 2 — 2015 Set 3 Q43, counting prime implicants

Question. (GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q43, NAT 2 marks) The total number of prime implicants of f(w,x,y,z) = Σ(0,2,4,5,6,10) is ______.

  1. Write the minterms in binary: 0000, 0010, 0100, 0101, 0110, 1010.The tabular method needs them in this form; the map needs them as cells.
  2. 0, 2, 4, 6 differ only in the middle two bits, so they form a quad: w'z'.Four cells, so two literals stay.
  3. 4 (0100) and 5 (0101) differ only in z, so they form the pair w'xy'.
  4. 2 (0010) and 10 (1010) differ only in w, so they form the pair x'yz'.
  5. Answer 3. The pairs (0,2), (0,4), (2,6), (4,6) exist but sit inside the quad, so they are not prime.Official key: 3.

Example 3 — 2018 Q49, essentials with don't-cares

Question. (GATE CSE 2018, Q49, NAT 2 marks) F(P,Q,R,S) = Σ m(0,2,5,7,9,11) + d(3,8,10,12,14). The number of essential prime implicants is ______.

All five prime implicants. Q'S' (quad 0,2,8,10), Q'R (pair 2,11), P'QS (pair 5,7), PQ' (pair 9,11), P'RS (pair 3,7).

Which own a one. Q'S' owns 0; P'QS owns 5; PQ' owns 9. Those three are essential. Q'R covers 2 and 11, and minterm 2 is also covered by Q'S', minterm 11 by PQ', so it owns nothing. P'RS covers 3 (a don't-care) and 7, but 7 is also covered by P'QS.

Answer 3. Note what the don't-cares did: they turned a pair into a quad, which reduced the literal count from nine to seven, but they created no essential implicant. The page computes the count and the literal counts both ways.

Example 4 — 2017 Set 2 Q28, a product of sums

Question. (GATE CSE 2017 Set 2, Q28, MCQ 2 marks) Given f(w,x,y,z) = Σm(0,1,2,3,7,8,10) + Σd(5,6,11,15), which is a minimum product-of-sums form?

Zeros. {4,9,12,13,14} — everything that is neither a one nor a don't-care.

Group the zeros. {4,6,12,14} has x=1, z=0 with w and y free: xz'. {9,11,13,15} has w=1, z=1: wz. So f̅ = xz' + wz.

Invert. f = (x' + z)(w' + z') — option A, and the published key.

Kill the others in one row each. Option B, (w'+z)(x+z), is 0 at minterm 0, where x=0 and z=0; option C, (w+z)(x'+z), is 0 at minterm 0 as well, where w=0 and z=0; option D, (w+z')(x'+z), is 0 at minterm 1, where w=0 and z=1. Each is a row where the specification says 1, so one substitution eliminates the option.

Example 5 — the cyclic map, where nothing is essential

Question (original practice). For g(a,b,c) = Σ m(0,1,2,5,6,7), how many essential prime implicants are there, and how many terms does a minimum cover need?

The six prime implicants are a'c' {0,2}, a'b' {0,1}, b'c {1,5}, bc' {2,6}, ac {5,7} and ab {6,7}. Every minterm sits in exactly two of them, so no prime implicant is essential: zero.

A cover must still exist. Three pairs suffice — for example a'c', b'c and ab — giving six literals, and no two pairs can cover six minterms, so three terms is the minimum.

Why it matters. "Take all essential prime implicants, then the rest" is a complete algorithm only when at least one essential exists. On a cyclic map the second phase is a real search, and the page runs it by enumerating covers in order of term count.

Example 6 — the tabular method on the same function, to show it agrees

For f(w,x,y,z) = Σ(0,2,4,5,6,10), list the minterms by the number of ones: zero ones: 0000; one one: 0010, 0100; two ones: 0101, 0110, 1010.

Round one: 0000 with 0010 gives 00-0; 0000 with 0100 gives 0-00; 0010 with 0110 gives 0-10; 0010 with 1010 gives -010; 0100 with 0101 gives 010-; 0100 with 0110 gives 01-0. Round two: 00-0 with 0-00 gives 0--0; 00-0 with 01-0 gives 0--0; 0-00 with 01-0 gives 0--0; 0-10 with -010 cannot combine, because the dashes are in different places.

Survivors: 0--0 (that is w'z'), 010- (w'xy'), -010 (x'yz'). Three, exactly as the map said. Derivation The page implements this procedure in code and compares its prime-implicant list with the one the map-based reasoning gives; a mismatch fails a check.

Edge cases that change the answer

Each of these is a place where a correct-looking method gives a wrong answer because an assumption quietly failed.

1. Variable order decides every index

Σ m numbers rows by reading the assignment as a binary number, leftmost variable most significant. For f(w,x,y,z), minterm 6 is wxyz = 0110, that is w=0, x=1, y=1, z=0. If you read the same string as zyxw you get a different function.

Counterexample. f(w,x,y,z) = Σ m(1,2) with w most significant is w'x'y'z + w'x'yz'; with z most significant the same numbers give w'x'yz + w'xy'z'. Both are legal functions; only one is the function the question asked about. Fix the order in the first line of your answer.

2. The all-ones and all-zeros functions

If f = 1 for every row, there are no zero-rows, so Π M is empty. The canonical POS is the empty product, which is 1 by convention. The minimal SOP is the term 1, with no literals.

Trap. Writing Π M(0,1,…,15) for the all-ones function inverts the answer. The list of maxterms is exactly the list of zero-rows, and an all-ones function has none. Symmetrically, the all-zeros function has empty Σ m and canonical SOP 0.

3. A block may never contain a zero

A block of four cells that includes a zero is not a block at all: it corresponds to a product term that is 1 on a row where the function is 0, which changes the function.

How it happens. Reading the map in counting order instead of Gray-code order makes two non-adjacent cells look adjacent, and the "block" you draw spans a zero in between. If your answer fails on one row of the truth table, this is the first thing to check.

4. A don't-care is optional in both directions

You may cover a don't-care, and you may leave it uncovered. What you may not do is treat it as a one that must be covered, or as a zero that blocks a block.

Consequence for answers. Two students can produce different correct circuits for the same specification: they differ only on don't-care rows. A question that asks "what does the circuit output at minterm 12" is asking about a particular expression, not about the function. The 2018 question avoids this ambiguity by asking for a count, which is well defined.

5. Cyclic maps have no essential implicant

Σ m(0,1,2,5,6,7) in three variables: six prime implicants, each minterm in exactly two of them, zero essentials.

What goes wrong. The recipe "take the essentials, then cover what is left" terminates immediately with an empty set, and the answer becomes "nothing", which is wrong. On a cyclic map the minimum requires comparing covers, and ties are possible: for this function several three-term covers exist, all with six literals.

6. Fewest terms is not always fewest literals

Two covers can have the same number of terms and different literal counts, and — less often — a cover with more terms can tie on total literal count while using more gates.

What GATE means. "Minimum" in GATE's minimisation questions means fewest product terms, then fewest literals. When the question says "minimum number of two-input NAND gates" it is counting a different cost, and the answer can differ from the minimal SOP, because a multi-input AND is not one gate.

7. Five variables need two maps, and the mirror cells are adjacent

A five-variable map is drawn as two four-variable maps, one for v=0 and one for v=1. A cell in one map is adjacent to the cell in the same position in the other map, because those two assignments differ only in v.

Consequence. Blocks of 8 and 16 can straddle the two maps, and a student who treats the two maps as unrelated loses them. The page checks that the mirror relation holds for all sixteen positions.

8. The complement is not the dual

The dual of an expression swaps AND with OR and 0 with 1, and leaves the variables alone. The complement swaps AND with OR and complements every variable, which is De Morgan.

Closely confused pair. The dual of x + y' is x · y'; the complement is x' · y. Only the complement is guaranteed to be the opposite function. The dual of an expression is generally a different function, and the duality principle applies to identities, not to a single expression you are simplifying.

Seven beliefs that cost marks, and what replaces them

Each row states the belief in the words a student would use, says what is actually true, and gives the smallest case that shows the difference.

Misconception 1 — "the largest number of groups gives the minimum"

What is true. The minimum is the cheapest cover, not the set of all blocks. Covering a one twice adds a term that is not needed; the goal is to cover every one at least once with as few, and as large, blocks as possible.

Smallest counterexample. f = a in two variables. The blocks are the quad {0,1,2,3} (that is the term 1), the pair {2,3} (a), and the pair {0,1} (a'). Using all of them gives 1 + a + a' = 1, which is correct but not minimal in the sense of cost — the minimal cover is the single term a, two literals fewer. Counting blocks, not covers, gives the wrong answer to "how many terms".

Misconception 2 — "a don't-care is a one"

What is true. A don't-care may be treated as a one if that helps a block, and as a zero otherwise. It never has to be covered.

Smallest counterexample. f = Σ m(0) + d(15) in four variables. Treating 15 as a one that must be covered suggests keeping a term for it — but no block contains both 0 and 15, so that term is pure cost. The minimal answer is w'x'y'z', and 15 is left uncovered.

Misconception 3 — "a don't-care can make an implicant essential"

What is true. Essentiality is defined by a one that only this prime implicant covers. Don't-cares do not need covering, so covering one exclusively proves nothing.

Smallest counterexample. In the 2018 question, P'RS covers the don't-care at 3 exclusively — no other prime implicant contains 3 — and it is still not essential, because its only one, minterm 7, is also covered by P'QS.

Misconception 4 — "prime implicants are the answer"

What is true. A prime implicant is a maximal block. An answer is a cover: a subset of the prime implicants whose union contains every one. Some prime implicants appear in no minimal cover at all.

Smallest counterexample. In the 2018 question there are five prime implicants and the minimum uses three. Listing all five as the answer would be wrong twice over: too many terms, and one of them (P'RS) has a single literal count of three for a single minterm.

Misconception 5 — "grouping zeros gives the same kind of answer as grouping ones"

What is true. Grouping ones gives a sum of products directly. Grouping zeros gives a sum of products for the complement, and the answer needs De Morgan: every product becomes a sum and every literal flips.

Smallest counterexample. For the 2017 function the zero-blocks give xz' + wz. Copying that as (x + z')(w + z) flips nothing and is the wrong function: it is 0 at minterm 0, where f is 1. The correct product of sums is (x'+z)(w'+z').

Misconception 6 — "if I cannot simplify further, it is minimal"

What is true. Algebraic simplification is a sequence of local rewrites; where you stop depends on the order you chose. Minimality is a global property, and only an exhaustive method — the map, or the tabular search — can certify it.

Smallest counterexample. Starting from a'c + ac' + b'c, absorption and consensus remove nothing: the expression is already minimal at six literals. Starting from the canonical five-term form of the same function and applying identities in an unlucky order, you can easily stop at four terms and eight literals, which is not minimal and cannot be simplified by the three rules you happened to try.

Misconception 7 — "the dual and the complement are the same operation"

What is true. Dual: swap the operators and the constants, leave variables alone. Complement: swap the operators and complement every variable. Only the second produces the opposite function.

Smallest counterexample. Take x + y. Its dual is xy, which is 0 at (1,0) while x+y is 1 — so the dual is not the complement. The complement is x'y', which is 0 exactly where x+y is 1. Duality is a rule about identities: it says the dual of a true identity is true, not that the dual of a function is its complement.

How this topic is actually asked

Five patterns cover the previous-year record. Each is a different skill, and each has a procedure that finishes in under two minutes once you have practised it.

Pattern 1 — count the prime implicants

A numerical answer question: given a minterm list, how many prime implicants? 2015 Set 3 Q43 is the canonical example, key 3.

Procedure. Draw the map, find every maximal block, and stop. Do not build a cover, and do not count sub-blocks. Sanity check: every one must lie in at least one prime implicant, and no prime implicant may be contained in another.

Pattern 2 — count the essential prime implicants

Same shape, one step further: 2018 Q49, key 3. The difference is that essentials are defined by ones.

Procedure. List the prime implicants, then for each one ask whether it owns a one. A one is owned if no other prime implicant contains it. Count only those. Don't-cares never count towards ownership.

Pattern 3 — pick the minimum form from four options

Multiple choice with a product-of-sums or sum-of-products answer: 2017 Set 2 Q28, key A.

Procedure. Do not expand the options. Minimise yourself, then — if your form is not among them — test each option on one row where you know the answer. One disagreement eliminates an option, and the rows with the most zeros in the specification are the fastest to test.

Pattern 4 — identify the essential prime implicants of a given expression

The 2004 question gives f = a'c + ac' + b'c and asks which are essential; key: a'c and ac'.

Procedure. Convert to minterms, find all prime implicants (the given terms may not all be prime), then test ownership. Here b'c covers {1,5} and minterm 5 is also covered by ab', so it is not essential.

Pattern 5 — an algebraic identity under a constraint

2016 Set 2 Q33 gives x_1 ⊕ x_2 ⊕ x_3 ⊕ x_4 = 0 and asks which statement must hold; key C.

Procedure. Substitute a witness. For a "must be true" question, one row that satisfies the hypothesis and falsifies the option kills it: x_1=x_2=x_3=x_4=1 kills A and D at once. What survives is the answer — and here it survives because a̅ ⊕ b̅ = a ⊕ b, so C restates the hypothesis.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q43 2 marks

The total number of prime implicants of the function f(w,x,y,z) = Σ(0,2,4,5,6,10) is ______.

Answer: 3. The quad {0,2,4,6} gives w'z'; the pair {4,5} gives w'xy'; the pair {2,10} gives x'yz'. Each is maximal: 7 and 14 are zeros, which prevents any of them from growing.

Why the others fail. Counting every block gives seven or more, because (0,2), (0,4), (2,6) and (4,6) all exist. Counting only the blocks you would use in the answer also gives three here, which is a coincidence — the two counts differ in general.

Trap. The question asks for prime implicants, not essential ones. Here all three happen to be essential, so the two counts coincide; in the 2018 question they do not.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2018, Q49 2 marks

Consider F(P,Q,R,S) = Σ m(0,2,5,7,9,11) + d(3,8,10,12,14), where m denotes a minterm and d a don't-care. The number of essential prime implicants of F is ______.

Answer: 3. The five prime implicants are Q'S' (0,2,8,10), Q'R (2,11), P'QS (5,7), PQ' (9,11) and P'RS (3,7). The essentials are Q'S' (owns 0), P'QS (owns 5) and PQ' (owns 9).

Why 5 is the wrong answer. Five is the number of prime implicants. The question asks how many are essential, and two of the five own no one: Q'R shares 2 with Q'S' and 11 with PQ', and P'RS shares 7 with P'QS.

Why 4 is tempting. P'RS covers the don't-care at 3 exclusively, and it is easy to read "covers something nobody else covers" as essential. Don't-cares do not need covering, so they cannot create an essential implicant.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2017 Set 2, Q28 2 marks

Given f(w,x,y,z) = Σm(0,1,2,3,7,8,10) + Σd(5,6,11,15), which one of the following is a minimum product-of-sums form of f?

(A) (w' + z')(x' + z)   (B) (w' + z)(x + z)   (C) (w + z)(x' + z)   (D) (w + z')(x' + z)

Answer: (A). The zeros are 4, 9, 12, 13, 14. Grouping {4,6,12,14} gives xz' and {9,11,13,15} gives wz, so f̅ = xz' + wz and f = (x'+z)(w'+z'). The published key is A.

Why the others fail, one row each. (B) is 0 at minterm 0, where x=0 and z=0, and f must be 1 there. (C) is 0 at minterm 0 as well, where w=0 and z=0. (D) is 0 at minterm 1, where w=0 and z=1. Each option dies on a row of the specification.

The trap. Forgetting to invert the zero-blocks gives (x+z')(w+z), which is not an option — but a student who inverts one factor and not the other lands on (B) or (D). Invert both, or apply De Morgan to the whole expression at once.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2004 1 mark

Which are the essential prime implicants of f(a,b,c) = a'c + ac' + b'c?

(A) a'c and ac'   (B) a'c and b'c   (C) a'c only   (D) ac' and bc'

Answer: (A). The minterms are {1,3,4,5,6}. a'c covers {1,3} and owns 3. ac' covers {4,6} and owns 6. So both are essential.

Why (B) and (C) fail. b'c covers {1,5}, and minterm 5 is also covered by ab', so b'c owns nothing. (C) omits ac', which owns minterm 6 — no other prime implicant contains 6.

Why (D) fails. bc' is not even an implicant: it is 1 at minterm 2, where f=0. An option can be eliminated for not being a term of the function at all.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2016 Set 2, Q33 2 marks

Let x_1 ⊕ x_2 ⊕ x_3 ⊕ x_4 = 0, where x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4 are Boolean variables and ⊕ is XOR. Which one of the following must always be TRUE?

(A) x_1x_2x_3x_4 = 0   (B) x_1x_3 + x_2 = 0   (C) x_1' ⊕ x_3' = x_2' ⊕ x_4'   (D) x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 0

Answer: (C). Complementing both inputs leaves XOR unchanged, so x_1' ⊕ x_3' = x_1 ⊕ x_3. From x_1 ⊕ x_2 ⊕ x_3 ⊕ x_4 = 0 we get x_1 ⊕ x_3 = x_2 ⊕ x_4, so (C) restates the hypothesis. Published key: C.

Witnesses that kill the rest. (1,1,1,1) satisfies the hypothesis, and makes A's product 1 and D's sum 1, so A and D both fail. (1,0,1,0) satisfies the hypothesis and gives x_1x_3 + x_2 = 1, so B fails.

The trap. "Must always be true" is falsified by one witness. Students often try to prove each option instead of looking for a single row, which takes far longer and invites errors.

NAT Original practice no official marks

How many essential prime implicants does g(a,b,c) = Σ m(0,1,2,5,6,7) have?

Answer: 0. The six prime implicants are a'c'{0,2}, a'b'{0,1}, b'c{1,5}, bc'{2,6}, ac{5,7}, ab{6,7}. Every minterm lies in exactly two of them, so no prime implicant owns a minterm.

What to do next. A minimum cover still exists — three pairs, six literals — but it must be found by comparing covers. This is the cyclic map, and it is the case where "take the essentials" gives an empty starting set.

MSQ Original practice no official marks

Which of the following are true of a don't-care term d(i) in a Karnaugh map for a sum-of-products answer?

(A) It may be included in a block. (B) It must be included in some block. (C) It may be left out of every block. (D) Including it can reduce the number of literals.

Answer: (A), (C), (D). A don't-care is a row whose output is unspecified, so either value is correct and the minimiser chooses whichever is cheaper.

Why (B) fails. Nothing forces a don't-care to be covered. In the 2018 function the don't-care at 12 is used, and a different function may leave all its don't-cares out.

Why (D) holds, with an example. In the 2018 function, using the don't-cares at 8 and 10 turns Q'S' from a pair into a quad, dropping one literal; the total falls from nine literals to seven.

MCQ Original practice no official marks

Which of these is the dual of x' + yz?

(A) x'(y + z)   (B) x(y + z)   (C) x + y'z'   (D) xy + xz

Answer: (A). Duality swaps AND with OR and 0 with 1, and leaves every literal untouched — including a primed one. So x' + yz becomes x' · (y + z).

Why (B) is the trap. (B) is the complement, not the dual: De Morgan complements the variables as well. The complement of x' + yz is x · (y' + z'), which is not (B) either — (B) is what you get by dropping the primes, a third, meaningless operation.

Why (C) and (D) fail. (C) changes the variables without changing the structure; (D) is an expansion of x(y+z), not a dual. Only (A) applies the swap to the operators alone.

NAT Original practice no official marks

A four-variable function is 1 on all sixteen rows. How many literals does its minimal sum-of-products form contain?

Answer: 0. The whole map is one block of sixteen, which drops all four variables. The term is the constant 1.

Why it is not 4 or 16. The canonical form has sixteen minterms of four literals each — sixty-four literals. The minimal form has one term and no literals at all, and it is a legitimate two-level circuit: the output is wired to the constant.

NAT Original practice no official marks

For f(P,Q,R,S) = Σ m(0,2,5,7,9,11) + d(3,8,10,12,14) (the 2018 function), how many literals does a minimal sum-of-products form contain?

Answer: 7. The three essential implicants are Q'S' (two literals), P'QS (three) and PQ' (two): 2 + 3 + 2 = 7.

Why 9 is the tempting wrong answer. Treating the don't-cares as zeros gives P'Q'S' + P'QS + PQ'S, which is three terms and nine literals. The two don't-cares at 8 and 10 are what let the first term shrink from three literals to two, and the one at 3 lets nothing. The page computes both counts.

MCQ Original practice no official marks

Which statement about the tabular (Quine–McCluskey) method is correct?

(A) It finds a minimal cover in one pass, without a cover chart. (B) A term is a prime implicant exactly when it was never ticked as combined. (C) It requires the variables to be ordered so that the map wraps correctly. (D) It cannot use don't-care terms.

Answer: (B). Terms that combine are ticked; when a round produces nothing new, the unticked terms are precisely the maximal sub-cubes, that is the prime implicants.

Why (A) fails. The prime-implicant list is only the first half. Choosing among them is the second half, and it needs the cover chart or an exhaustive search — the cyclic map shows why a single pass cannot always decide.

Why (C) fails. The method never draws a map, so no wrapping convention is needed; that is its advantage for five or more variables. (D) fails because don't-cares are simply included in the initial list of terms, exactly as they are placed on the map.

Note. The 2027 syllabus names the tabular method explicitly, so this distinction is now examinable even though the previous papers barely touched it.

Decision procedure for any minimisation question

  1. Fix the variable order and the minterm numbering.Every index depends on it; get it wrong and nothing later can be right.
  2. Write the specification: Σ m of the ones, Π M or the explicit zero list, and d of the don't-cares.
  3. Ask what is being counted: a form, a number of prime implicants, a number of essentials, a number of gates, or a number of literals.Four of those five questions have different answers for the same function.
  4. Find the prime implicants — by map for up to four variables, by the tabular method beyond that.
  5. If essentials are asked for: for each prime implicant, ask whether it owns a one. Count.
  6. If a form is asked for: take the essentials, then cover the rest with the largest remaining blocks; for POS, do all of it on the zeros and invert.
  7. Verify on two rows — one where the function is 1 and one where it is 0 — and check the literal count.

The whole topic in five sentences

A Boolean function is its truth table, and an expression is a choice of blocks of 2k cells, each block dropping k literals. The canonical forms are the two extremes: every row, every literal, unique and never minimal. The map is the table folded so that adjacent cells differ in one bit, with the edges wrapped; the tabular method is the same search written as dashes and a cover chart. Prime implicants are maximal blocks, essential ones own a one that nobody else covers, and a minimal answer is a cover — not a list. Don't-cares are free to use and free to ignore, and they never create an essential implicant.
