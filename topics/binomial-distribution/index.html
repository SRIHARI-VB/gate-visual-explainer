Topic and objective

TOPIC: The binomial distribution — the count of successes in n independent trials with one constant probability p. The mass function and the number of sequences behind it, the ratio recursion that walks the table, the peak at floor((n+1)p), the mean np and variance npq, the sum of two binomials, the Poisson limit, the waiting counts that are not binomial, and the cases where sampling without replacement changes the answer.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide from a problem statement whether a binomial model is justified, and say which of the three conditions fails when it is not. Compute P(X = k) from the coefficient and the two powers, or from the ratio to the neighbouring mass. Find the peak without drawing anything. Quote np and npq and know which of them independence is needed for. Add two binomials only when p matches, and say what the sum is otherwise. Use the Poisson when np is fixed and p is small, and the normal when np and nq are both large. Never confuse successes in a fixed number of trials with trials until a fixed number of successes.

I am reading this microtopic as one habit: name the fixed quantity first, then check the three conditions, then compute. Every scene and every question on the page is that habit applied to different words.

Prerequisites

The sentence to keep

A binomial is a model with three assumptions — fixed n, constant p, independent trials — and every question on this topic is really a question about whether those three hold and which quantity the words held fixed. The mean np survives even when independence fails; the variance npq does not.

Level 1 — Intuition, and Level 2 — the formal definition

Educational interpretation A factory line stamps 10 parts a day and each part is defective with probability 0.3, independently. Nobody can tell you the chance of exactly 3 defectives by watching the line, because the answer depends on the order as well as the count — and the order is not what you asked about. The binomial is the distribution that forgets the order and keeps the count.

Formal definition. Let n be a positive integer and p a number in [0, 1]. Let X1, …, Xn be independent variables, each taking 1 with probability p and 0 with probability q = 1 − p. Set X = X1 + … + Xn. Then X is a binomial random variable with parameters n and p, written X ~ Bin(n, p), and for each integer k from 0 to n,

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pk · qn−k, and P(X = k) = 0 for every other k.

Read the coefficient aloud: "the number of ways to choose which k of the n trials are the successes". Read the two powers: "the probability of any one such pattern". Their product is the mass at k, and the binomial theorem is why the masses sum to one: (p + q)n = 1n = 1.

Level 3 — Visual understanding: the coefficient is a count of sequences

At n = 10 and p = 0.3 the bar at k = 3 is built in two pieces that the animation shows separately. There are C(10,3) = 120 sequences with exactly three successes — the coefficient, which is pure counting and does not know what p is. Each such sequence has probability 0.33 × 0.77 = 0.002224. The bar is 120 × 0.002224 = 0.266828.

That split is the whole picture: how many patterns times how likely one pattern is. It also explains why p near 0.5 gives a hump in the middle — the coefficient is largest at k = n/2 — and why p near 0 or 1 drags the hump to the ends, where the coefficient is small but the power term is enormous.

Level 4 — Worked example, one line at a time

A fair coin is tossed 4 times. P(two heads) = C(4,2)(1/2)2(1/2)2 = 6 × (1/16) = 6/16 = 3/8 = 0.375. This is GATE CSE 2004's one-mark item, and it is answered by the definition and nothing else.

A die shows a six with probability 1/6. In three throws, P(exactly two sixes) = C(3,2)(1/6)2(5/6) = 3 × 5/216 = 15/216 = 0.069444. Note that the base of each power is the probability of that trial's outcome, not a count.

Working backwards: a binomial has mean 4 and variance 3. Then np = 4 and npq = 3, so q = 3/4, p = 1/4 and n = 16. Two equations, two unknowns — and a third piece of information would make the system over-determined, which is why such questions only ever give two.

Level 5 — Boundaries: the three conditions, and what each one buys

Fixed n. The number of trials is decided before the experiment, not after. If you keep tossing until something happens, n is not fixed and the count is not binomial — it is geometric or negative binomial (scene 9).

Constant p. Every trial has the same success probability. Sampling without replacement changes p on each draw and gives the hypergeometric distribution instead (scene 10).

Independence. This is the one that only affects the second moment. The mean E[X] = np holds by linearity alone and survives dependence; the variance npq does not. Shared causes leave the mean untouched and inflate the variance.

Level 6 — Edge cases and a counterexample

n = 1 is the Bernoulli distribution itself: masses q and p at 0 and 1.

p = 0 or p = 1 is degenerate: all mass sits at k = 0 or at k = n, the variance is 0, and the "distribution" has one outcome.

n = 0 gives a point mass at 0; the mean and variance are both 0. No GATE item needs it, but the formulas do not break.

Symmetry. If X ~ Bin(n, p) then n − X ~ Bin(n, 1 − p). At p = 0.5 the mass function is symmetric about n/2, so P(X = k) = P(X = n − k).

Counterexample to "the mean is the most likely value". At n = 5, p = 0.3 the mean is 1.5, and the peak is at k = 1 with P = 0.360150 while P(2) = 0.308700. A mean that is not an integer is never a value of the variable at all.

Level 7 — GATE application

The exam rarely asks for the definition. It asks you to find the binomial inside a question that does not use the word — two random bit strings, a set of 2n elements with a coin each, a packet with a bit-error rate — and then to produce one of four things: a coefficient and two powers, an exact fraction, a mean and variance, or a peak.

The two most common answer shapes in past papers are an expression in n, such as C(n,d)/2n or C(2n,n)/4n, and an exact fraction such as 3/8 or 7/8. When the options contain a variable, the answer is a formula; when they are numbers, substitute and compute.

Example 1 — the definition, used directly (GATE CSE 2004, 1 mark)

"If a fair coin is tossed four times, what is the probability that two heads and two tails will result?"

Answer: 3/8 = 0.375. Four independent fair trials, n = 4 and p = 1/2. C(4,2) = 6, and every sequence of four tosses has probability (1/2)4 = 1/16. So the answer is 6/16 = 3/8.

The trap that is not here. "Two heads and two tails" fixes the counts, not the order, which is exactly why the coefficient appears. Had the question said "heads, heads, tails, tails in that order", the answer would be 1/16 and the coefficient would be 1.

Example 2 — finding the binomial in a bit string (GATE CSE 2004, 2 marks)

"Two n bit binary strings, S1 and S2, are chosen randomly with uniform probability. The probability that the Hamming distance between these strings (the number of bit positions where the two strings differ) is equal to d is …" Options: C(n,d)/2n, C(n,d)/2d, d/2n, 1/2d.

Answer: C(n,d)/2n, the first option. Each position differs with probability 1/2, independently, so the distance is a binomial count with n trials and p = 1/2. Then P(D = d) = C(n,d)(1/2)d(1/2)n−d = C(n,d)/2n.

The page checks the answer a second way: 2n choices for S1, then C(n,d) choices of which positions differ, so 2nC(n,d) pairs out of 22n. At n = 8 and d = 3 that is 14336/65536 = 0.218750, and the bar chart gives the same number. Why option 2 fails: it divides by 2d and forgets that the n − d agreeing positions each still cost a factor of 1/2.

Example 3 — the trial count is 2n, not n (GATE CSE 2006, Q21, 2 marks)

"For each element in a set of size 2n, an unbiased coin is tossed. The 2n coin tosses are independent. An element is chosen if the corresponding coin toss was a head. The probability that exactly n elements are chosen is …" Options: C(2n,n)/4n, C(2n,n)/2n, 1/C(2n,n), 1/2.

Answer: C(2n,n)/4n, the first option. There are 2n trials at p = 1/2, so the count is Bin(2n, 1/2) and P(X = n) = C(2n,n)(1/2)n(1/2)n = C(2n,n)/22n = C(2n,n)/4n.

Why the other three fail. C(2n,n)/2n keeps only one of the two half-power factors — the commonest slip in the topic. 1/C(2n,n) treats a count of patterns as though it were a probability, and is larger than 1 for every n above 1. And 1/2 is the value the expression approaches as n grows, not its value: the page computes 0.5 at n = 1, 0.375 at n = 2 and 0.112275 at n = 25, which is within half a per cent of 1/root(pi n).

Example 4 — two strings that must differ (GATE CSE 2005, 2 marks)

"A random bit string of length n is constructed by tossing a fair coin n times and setting a bit to 0 or 1 depending on outcomes head and tail, respectively. The probability that two such randomly generated strings are not identical is …" Options: 1/2n, 1 − 1/n, 1/n!, 1 − 1/2n.

Answer: 1 − 1/2n. The two strings agree in a given position with probability 1/2, so they agree everywhere with probability (1/2)n. The complement is the answer.

This is the binomial summed over k from 1 to n: the number of differing positions is Bin(n, 1/2), and summing every mass except k = 0 gives the same 1 − 1/2n. When the question asks for "at least one", the complement is nearly always shorter than the sum.

Example 5 — at least one of each (GATE CSE 2002)

"Four fair coins are tossed simultaneously. The probability that at least one head and one tail turn up is …" Options: 1/16, 1/8, 7/8, 15/16.

Answer: 7/8. The only outcomes that fail are all four heads and all four tails, each with probability 1/16. So the answer is 1 − 2/16 = 7/8.

In binomial language: P(1 ≤ X ≤ 3) with X ~ Bin(4, 1/2) is 1 − P(0) − P(4) = 1 − 1/16 − 1/16. Why 15/16 is offered: it is the answer to "at least one head", which excludes only the all-tails string — one event instead of two.

Example 6 — working backwards from the moments

A binomial variable has mean 4 and variance 3. Find n and p, then P(X = 0).

Divide: variance over mean = npq over np = q = 3/4. So p = 1/4, and n = 4/p = 16. Then P(X = 0) = qn = (3/4)16 = 0.010023.

The division is the whole method, and it works because both moments are linear in n. Note the sanity check available for free: a variance of 3 against a mean of 4 is legal, since npq ≤ np. A variance of 5 against a mean of 4 would be impossible, and that impossibility is a legitimate way to eliminate a wrong model in a multiple-choice question.

The mean is not the peak

At n = 5 and p = 0.3 the mean is 1.5 and the peak is at k = 1: P(1) = 0.360150, P(2) = 0.308700. The peak is floor((n+1)p) = floor(1.8) = 1. Rounding the mean gives 2 and the wrong bar.

When (n+1)p is a whole number the peak is two neighbouring bars of equal height: at n = 9 and p = 0.5 both k = 4 and k = 5 are 0.246094. A question that says "the mode" and gives one integer is then testing whether you noticed the tie.

Variance above the mean is impossible

For any binomial, npq ≤ np because q ≤ 1. So a model claiming mean 6 and variance 9 cannot be binomial, whatever the other numbers say.

The reverse is not a proof of binomiality: a mean of 6 and a variance of 3 is consistent with many distributions. This is the same warning the normal-distribution lesson makes — two moments never determine a distribution, so consistency is necessary and never sufficient.

Degenerate parameters

p = 0: every trial fails, all mass at k = 0, mean 0, variance 0. p = 1: all mass at k = n. n = 0: a point mass at 0.

In all three the mass function still sums to 1 and the formulas np and npq still give the right answer, so no special case is needed — but the ratio recursion P(k+1)/P(k) divides by p/q and breaks at p = 0 or 1, which is why it is stated for 0 < p < 1.

k outside 0 to n

P(X = k) = 0 for k below 0 or above n, and this is a real distinction from the Poisson, where every k ≥ 0 carries some mass. It is what makes "P(X at most 10) = 1" true at n = 10 and false for a Poisson with the same mean.

It also means a question asking for P(X > n) has the answer 0 without any arithmetic — and questions do occasionally test exactly that.

The symmetry you can use instead of computing

If X ~ Bin(n, p) then n − X ~ Bin(n, 1 − p). At p = 1/2 the mass function is symmetric, so P(X = k) = P(X = n − k) and P(X ≤ k) = P(X ≥ n − k).

So at n = 8, P(X ≥ 6) = P(X ≤ 2), and the page's bar chart at scene 5 is symmetric about 4. Using symmetry turns a right tail into a left tail, which is one addition instead of several.

Small n: the normal approximation is simply wrong

At n = 10, p = 0.3 the binomial mass at k = 0 is 0.028248; the Poisson at the same mean gives 0.049787. Neither the Poisson nor the normal is a substitute for the exact coefficient when n is small and the question asks for an exact fraction.

GATE items that print C(2n,n)/4n or C(n,d)/2n are asking for the exact expression, so an approximation is not merely unnecessary — it cannot be an option.

"The most likely count is np"

Wrong. The peak is floor((n+1)p). At n = 5, p = 0.3, np = 1.5 and the peak is at k = 1, not 2.

What is true: the peak is within 1 of np, which is why the two agree whenever np is an integer and often when it is not. The counterexample is small enough to check by hand.

"Var(X) = (np)2 q, or maybe root(np) times q"

Wrong. Var(X) = npq and the standard deviation is root(npq). There is no square on np, and the root goes over the whole product.

Why the error is common: the normal distribution's variance is sigma2, so a variance written with a square somewhere looks familiar. The binomial's variance is linear in n.

"Successes in 5 trials and the 3rd success on trial 5 are the same question"

Wrong. They are 0.132300 and 0.079380 at p = 0.3. The waiting question forces the last trial to be a success, so only four trials are free.

The test: ask what is fixed. If the number of trials is fixed, it is binomial. If the number of successes is fixed and the trials vary, it is geometric or negative binomial.

"Two binomials always add to a binomial"

Wrong. Only when p is the same. Bin(2,0.2) + Bin(3,0.8) differs from Bin(5,0.5) by as much as 0.1419, even though both have mean 2.8 and variance 0.8.

What survives: the means and the variances always add. Matching moments is a check, never an identification.

"A binomial is fine for sampling without replacement if the sample is small"

Careful. It is an approximation whose error depends on the sampling fraction. At N = 10, K = 3, n = 4, the exact hypergeometric gives P(1) = 0.5 against the binomial's 0.4116 — a difference far larger than any option spacing.

The correction: the variance is multiplied by (N−n)/(N−1), here 0.666667. When that factor is close to 1 the binomial is a fair approximation; here it is not.

"Independence is needed for E[X] = np"

Wrong. The mean is a linearity result and holds for any dependence structure. What independence buys is Var(X) = npq, because variances only add when the variables are uncorrelated.

Why it matters in the exam: a question that describes dependent trials can still ask for the expected count, and np is still correct. It cannot ask for the variance with that formula.

The recognition procedure, in the order you should run it

1. Is there a fixed number of trials? If the experiment stops when something happens, the answer is not binomial. 2. Is there one constant probability? If items are drawn without replacement, it is hypergeometric. 3. Are the trials independent? If not, the mean is still np and the variance is not npq. 4. Which quantity is fixed — n or the number of successes? Then write the coefficient, the two powers, and simplify.

If the answer options contain n, the expected answer is a closed expression and you should simplify the powers before comparing. If the options are numbers, compute the fraction exactly and reduce it — 6/16 becomes 3/8 and only one option will be 3/8.

The four answer shapes in past papers

A coefficient over a power of 2 — C(n,d)/2n, C(2n,n)/4n, 1 − 1/2n. These come from counting bit strings or coin sequences.

An exact fraction — 3/8, 7/8, 15/216. These come from small n where the arithmetic fits on one line.

A moment — np, npq, or a pair of them to be solved backwards for n and p.

A peak — floor((n+1)p), sometimes disguised as "the most probable number of successes".

Four traps, each with the sentence that avoids it

Trap 1 — dropping a factor of 1/2. In any answer of the form C(n,k)/2n, count the positions that agree as well as the ones that differ. Trap 2 — rounding np for the peak. Write (n+1)p and floor it; if it is a whole number, say there are two modes. Trap 3 — reading "until" as "among". "Until" means the last trial is forced, so the coefficient is over the earlier trials. Trap 4 — using the Poisson when np is large. The Poisson needs p small; with np = 30 and p = 0.5 the normal is the right approximation and the Poisson is not.

Each trap is a different way of losing the same point: the model was chosen from the shape of the words rather than from the three conditions.

MCQGATE CSE 2004, 2 marks

Two n bit binary strings, S1 and S2, are chosen randomly with uniform probability. The probability that the Hamming distance between these strings is equal to d is

  1. C(n,d)/2n
  2. C(n,d)/2d
  3. d/2n
  4. 1/2d

Answer: A — C(n,d)/2n

Each bit position differs with probability 1/2 and the positions are independent, so the distance is Bin(n, 1/2). Then P(D = d) = C(n,d)(1/2)d(1/2)n−d = C(n,d)/2n. Checked a second way: 2n × C(n,d) pairs out of 22n, which is the same expression. At n = 8, d = 3 the model gives 56/256 = 0.218750.

Why B fails: the n − d agreeing positions each contribute a factor of 1/2, so the denominator is 2n and not 2d. Why C fails: it counts positions instead of subsets of positions. Why D fails: it is the probability of one specified pattern of d differences, without the coefficient.

MCQGATE CSE 2006, Q21

For each element in a set of size 2n, an unbiased coin is tossed. The 2n coin tosses are independent. An element is chosen if the corresponding coin toss was a head. The probability that exactly n elements are chosen is

  1. C(2n,n)/4n
  2. C(2n,n)/2n
  3. 1/C(2n,n)
  4. 1/2

Answer: A — C(2n,n)/4n

The count of chosen elements is Bin(2n, 1/2). So P(X = n) = C(2n,n)(1/2)n(1/2)n = C(2n,n)/22n = C(2n,n)/4n.

Why B fails: it keeps only one of the two half-power factors — the most common slip in this topic. Why C fails: a coefficient is a count of patterns, not a probability, and 1/C(2n,n) exceeds 1 for n above 1. Why D fails: 1/2 is the large-n limit. The page computes 0.5 at n = 1, 0.375 at n = 2 and 0.112275 at n = 25, against 1/root(pi n) = 0.112838.

MCQGATE CSE 2005, 2 marks

A random bit string of length n is constructed by tossing a fair coin n times and setting a bit to 0 or 1 depending on outcomes head and tail, respectively. The probability that two such randomly generated strings are not identical is

  1. 1/2n
  2. 1 − 1/n
  3. 1/n!
  4. 1 − 1/2n

Answer: D — 1 − 1/2n

In each position the two strings agree with probability 1/2, independently, so they agree everywhere with probability (1/2)n. The complement is the answer. Equivalently, sum the binomial masses for k = 1 to n with X ~ Bin(n, 1/2), which is 1 − P(X = 0).

Why A fails: it is the probability that the strings are identical. Why B and C fail: neither is a probability that equals 1 at n = 1, which the true answer must.

MCQGATE CSE 2002

Four fair coins are tossed simultaneously. The probability that at least one head and one tail turn up is

  1. 1/16
  2. 1/8
  3. 7/8
  4. 15/16

Answer: C — 7/8

With X ~ Bin(4, 1/2), the event is 1 ≤ X ≤ 3, so the probability is 1 − P(X = 0) − P(X = 4) = 1 − 1/16 − 1/16 = 7/8 = 0.875.

Why D fails: 15/16 is the answer to "at least one head", which excludes only the all-tails string — one event instead of two. Why A and B fail: both are far below the mass of the four middle counts, which is 14/16 in total.

NATOriginal practice question

A binomial random variable has mean 6 and variance 2.4. What is n?

Answer: 10

Divide the variance by the mean: npq / np = q = 2.4/6 = 0.4, so p = 0.6 and n = 6/0.6 = 10. The model confirms np = 6 and npq = 2.4 at (10, 0.6).

The trap: dividing the other way gives p = 0.4 and then n = 15, which also satisfies np = 6 but gives a variance of 3.6, not 2.4. Always check the second moment after solving.

MCQOriginal practice question

X ~ Bin(9, 0.5). Which statement about the most probable value of X is correct?

  1. 4 is the unique mode
  2. 5 is the unique mode
  3. 4 and 5 are both modes, each with probability 0.246094
  4. 4.5 is the mode

Answer: C — two modes, 4 and 5, each 0.246094

(n+1)p = 10 × 0.5 = 5 is a whole number, so the two neighbouring bars tie. The page computes both masses as 0.24609375 and finds exactly two k that attain the maximum.

Why A and B fail: each names one bar, and the bars at 4 and 5 are equal to twelve decimal places. Why D fails: 4.5 is the mean; a mode must be a value the variable can take, and X takes only integers.

MSQOriginal practice question

X ~ Bin(20, 0.4) and Y ~ Bin(30, 0.4) are independent. Which of the following are true?

  1. X + Y ~ Bin(50, 0.4)
  2. E[X + Y] = 20
  3. Var(X + Y) = 12
  4. X + Y ~ Bin(50, 0.8)

Answer: A and B and C

A is true because p matches: the trials add to 50, each still with p = 0.4. B is true because means add: 20 × 0.4 + 30 × 0.4 = 8 + 12 = 20. C is true because variances add: 20 × 0.4 × 0.6 + 30 × 0.4 × 0.6 = 4.8 + 7.2 = 12, which also equals 50 × 0.4 × 0.6.

Why D fails: p is a per-trial probability and does not add. Averaging 0.4 over more trials leaves it at 0.4; a sum with p = 0.8 would have mean 40, not 20.

NATOriginal practice question

A fair die is rolled repeatedly. What is the probability that the third six appears on exactly the fifth roll? Give the answer to four decimal places.

Answer: 0.0193

The fifth roll is forced to be a six, so among the first four rolls exactly two must be sixes: C(4,2)(1/6)2(5/6)2 = 6 x (1/36) x (25/36) = 150/1296 = 0.115741. Multiply by 1/6 for the fifth roll: 150/7776 = 0.019290, which is 0.0193 to four places.

In negative-binomial form the mass is C(n-1, k-1) pk qn-k with n = 5 and k = 3, which is the same product.

The trap: C(5,3)(1/6)3(5/6)2 = 250/7776 = 0.032150 answers "exactly three sixes among five rolls", which also counts runs where the third six arrived on roll 3 or 4.

The mass function

P(X = k) = C(n,k) p^k q^(n-k), for k = 0 to n, and 0 elsewhere.

C(n,k) counts the success patterns; pkqn−k is the probability of one pattern.

P(0) = qn · P(n) = pn · masses sum to 1 by (p+q)n.

At n = 10, p = 0.3: P(0) = 0.028248, P(3) = 0.266828, P(10) = 0.000006.

The ratio recursion

P(k+1)/P(k) = ((n-k)/(k+1)) · (p/q)

Start at qn and multiply across; no factorial is needed.

At n = 10, p = 0.3 the ratios are 4.285714, 1.928571, 1.142857, 0.75, 0.514286, …

The mass rises while the ratio exceeds 1 and falls afterwards, so the peak is where it crosses.

The peak

mode = floor((n+1)p), and two modes when (n+1)p is a whole number.

n = 10, p = 0.3: (n+1)p = 3.3, peak at 3. n = 9, p = 0.5: peak at 4 and 5, both 0.246094.

Not round(np): at n = 5, p = 0.3 the peak is 1 while np = 1.5.

The two moments

E[X] = np · Var(X) = npq · sd = root(npq)

The mean needs only linearity. The variance needs independence.

Always npq ≤ np, so a variance above the mean rules out a binomial.

With n fixed, npq is largest at p = 1/2. With the mean fixed instead, the variance is 6(1−p): 5.4 at p = 0.1 and 0.6 at p = 0.9.

Combining and limits

Equal p: Bin(n1,p) + Bin(n2,p) = Bin(n1+n2,p). Unequal p: not binomial.

n − X ~ Bin(n, 1−p); at p = 1/2 the mass function is symmetric.

Poisson: np fixed, p small — gap 0.04279 at n = 10, 3.368e-4 at n = 1000, 3.361e-6 at n = 100000.

Normal: np and nq both large, with the half-step continuity correction.

The four-step procedure

  1. Fixed n? No fixed trial count, no binomial.
  2. Constant p? Without replacement changes p; that is hypergeometric, with variance times (N−n)/(N−1).
  3. Independent? If not, keep np and abandon npq.
  4. Which count? Successes in n trials, or trials until k successes — 0.132300 against 0.079380 at n = 5, p = 0.3.

What the page computes for itself

Nothing on the canvas is copied from a table. The mass function is built from a Lanczos log-gamma, and the eleven bars at n = 10 sum to 1.000000000000; the mean and variance are summed directly over k and compared with np and npq at four parameter pairs; the ratio recursion is checked against the measured ratio of neighbouring bars at all ten values of k; the mode is found by a scan and compared with floor((n+1)p), including the tied case at n = 9 and the counterexample at n = 5; the two binomials are convolved and compared with the binomial of the summed trial count, once with matching p and once with unequal p; the Poisson gap is measured at five values of n up to 100000; the geometric and negative binomial masses are summed and their means compared with 1/p and k/p; the hypergeometric is compared with the binomial and its variance with npq(N−n)/(N−1); and the four previous-year answers are recomputed from their question text. 57 checks re-derive the lot a second way.
