Topic and objective

TOPIC: Hashing — hash functions, separate chaining, open addressing with linear, quadratic and double hashing, the load factor, clustering, deletion with tombstones, and the expected-probe formulas.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a hash function, a table size and a list of keys in an insertion order, trace the table exactly and report any slot, chain length or probe count asked for. Given only $$n$$, $$m$$ and $$\alpha$$, choose the right expected-cost formula for the scheme and for the kind of search. Given a proposed scheme, decide whether it is even guaranteed to terminate, and know which condition makes it so.

I read this topic as one skill — following a probe walk — applied to three schemes, plus one arithmetic skill: substituting into a formula. The worst-case analysis of a search is shared with the binary search and asymptotic complexity lessons and is not re-derived here; the probability machinery behind the formulas is in the counting lesson.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus, by name.

Section 5 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus (IIT Madras) reads: "Searching, sorting, hashing. Asymptotic worst case time and space complexity. Algorithm design techniques: greedy, dynamic programming and divide-and-conquer. Graph traversals, minimum spanning trees, shortest paths."

Hashing is one of three words in the first sentence, so a hashing question is inside the declared boundary. It is also named in Section 4 under Programming and Data Structures only indirectly — the words "hashing" appears once, in Section 5.

Class A does not mean a question is guaranteed, and it says nothing about marks. The previous-year items quoted here are observations about old papers, not a forecast for 2027.

Prerequisites

Four published lessons carry what this one uses:

  • Arrays, Stacks and Queues — a hash table is an array, and a chained slot is a list. Index arithmetic and wrap-around are the whole of a probe walk.
  • Linked Lists — the chain at a slot, and why unlinking is cheap once you hold the node.
  • Asymptotic Complexity — what "average" and "worst case" mean, and why an average needs a distribution.
  • Counting and Combinatorics — the pigeonhole principle, which is the reason collisions cannot be avoided, and the product rule behind the 97-over-100 cube.

One more thing is assumed and it is worth stating plainly: modulo means the remainder on division, so $$17 \bmod 7 = 3$$, and in this lesson a remainder is always taken into the range 0 to $$m-1$$ even for a negative key. The greatest common divisor of two numbers is the largest integer dividing both; $$\gcd(4,6)=2$$ and $$\gcd(4,7)=1$$.

Visualization plan

Ten scenes, each one argument:

  1. Direct addressing. The ideal — one array access — and the assumption it makes about the key universe that a real key set breaks.
  2. The hash function. Division and multiplication methods, and the bit-level reason a power-of-two table size throws information away.
  3. The adversary. One published function, six hostile keys, one chain of six. Then universal hashing and the 1-over-m bound, checked exhaustively.
  4. Chaining. Nine keys in seven slots, $$\alpha = 1.29$$, and the two search costs.
  5. Open addressing. Linear probing on eleven slots, with the two walks that wrap.
  6. Clustering. A run of occupied slots absorbing every key that hashes into it, and the squared denominator in Knuth's formula.
  7. Quadratic and double hashing. The two probe rules, and the exact condition each needs before it may promise a free slot.
  8. Deletion. A present key becoming unreachable, then the tombstone that rescues it.
  9. The costs. Four formulas at four load factors, measured beside the formula.
  10. The decision procedure. Four questions that turn any hashing item into a trace or a substitution.

The five ideas, in one breath

  1. Collisions are unavoidable — pigeonhole — so the design question is resolution, never avoidance.
  2. Chaining stores lists outside the table and tolerates $$\alpha > 1$$; open addressing stores keys inside the table and needs $$\alpha < 1$$.
  3. A probe sequence must be a permutation of the slots, or a key can be lost while a free slot exists. That is what the coprimality and primality conditions buy.
  4. Deletion in open addressing needs a third state, the tombstone, because search stops at the first empty slot.
  5. "Hashing is $$O(1)$$" is a statement about an average under uniform hashing. The worst case is $$\Theta(n)$$, and both statements are true of different things.

Level 1 — Intuition: the cloakroom

A cloakroom has 100 hooks and a thousand possible coats. If you number the hooks by coat, you need a thousand hooks and you will use a hundred. If instead the attendant applies a rule — "a coat goes to the hook numbered by the last two digits of its tag" — you need a hundred hooks, and finding a coat is one glance at one hook. Two coats with tags ending 47 want the same hook. That is the whole idea, and the last sentence is the whole problem.

Educational interpretation The analogy carries the trade honestly: a smaller table, a computed position, and collisions you must resolve. It does not carry the cost analysis, because a cloakroom attendant can see all the coats at once and a computer cannot.

Level 2 — Formal definition

A hash table is an array $$T[0 \ldots m-1]$$ of $$m$$ slots. A hash function is a total function $$h$$ from the key universe $$U$$ onto the index set $$\{0, \ldots, m-1\}$$. For a key $$k$$, the value $$h(k)$$ is its home slot. A collision is a pair of distinct keys $$k_1 \ne k_2$$ with $$h(k_1) = h(k_2)$$. The load factor is $$\alpha = n/m$$, where $$n$$ is the number of keys currently stored. Every symbol in words: $$m$$ is how many slots the table has, $$n$$ is how many keys you have put in it, and $$\alpha$$ is the average number of keys per slot.

Separate chaining keeps, at each slot, a list of the keys that hashed there, so the table holds $$m$$ list heads and the keys live outside it. Open addressing — also called closed hashing — stores the keys themselves in the slots, so a colliding key must go somewhere else in the same array. It does so by following a probe sequence: a function $$h(k, i)$$ giving the slot tried at probe number $$i$$, for $$i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$, with $$h(k,0)$$ the home slot.

The simple uniform hashing assumption (SUHA): each key is equally likely to hash into any of the $$m$$ slots, independently of every other key. Every expected-cost formula in this lesson is conditional on it. It is an assumption about the key set, not a theorem about a function.

Level 2a — The three probe rules, side by side

Write $$h'(k)$$ for the ordinary hash of $$k$$. Then:

linear: $$h(k,i) = (h'(k) + i) \bmod m$$
quadratic: $$h(k,i) = (h'(k) + c_1 i + c_2 i^2) \bmod m$$
double: $$h(k,i) = (h_1(k) + i \cdot h_2(k)) \bmod m$$

Linear probing steps by one, so it walks a cluster cell by cell. Quadratic probing steps by a growing amount, so it jumps out of a cluster but two keys with the same home slot still follow the same path. Double hashing steps by an amount that depends on the key, so those two keys separate at once. Only double hashing removes both kinds of clustering, and it is the only one whose expected cost matches the uniform-hashing bound.

Level 2b — What the load factor is, and is not

$$\alpha = n/m$$ is a ratio, so it can be any non-negative real. With chaining, $$\alpha = 80$$ is a perfectly ordinary state of affairs: 2000 keys in 25 slots. With open addressing, $$\alpha$$ can never reach 1, because the table has only $$m$$ cells and each key occupies one.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2015 Set 3 asked for the load factor of a table with 25 slots storing 2000 elements, and the keyed answer was 80. A load factor is not a percentage: writing 0.8 or "80 percent" is a different quantity from the one the item asked for.

Level 2c — Notation used in this lesson

  • $$m$$ — number of slots in the table (the table size).
  • $$n$$ — number of keys stored (the count).
  • $$\alpha$$ — load factor, $$n/m$$.
  • $$h(k)$$ — the home slot of key $$k$$.
  • $$h(k,i)$$ — the slot tried at probe number $$i$$; probe 0 is the home slot.
  • $$i$$ — the probe index, counting from 0.
  • $$\gcd(a,b)$$ — greatest common divisor; 1 means coprime.
  • $$\Theta(1)$$ — bounded above and below by constants, i.e. independent of $$n$$.

Level 3 — Visual understanding

Three pictures carry this topic and nothing else does.

The chain picture shows why chaining's cost is the list length: a search walks nodes, one per comparison, and stops at the key or at the end.

The probe-walk picture shows why open addressing's cost is the walk length: the walk is a path through the array, and every step is one comparison. Because the walk must be able to reach any slot, it must be a permutation of the slots — that single requirement generates the primality and coprimality conditions.

The cluster picture shows why linear probing degrades: an occupied run behaves as one big slot, so the probability of a long walk rises faster than linearly. The animation draws all three.

Example 1 — Chaining, nine keys into nine slots

GATE CSE 2014 Set 1. Insert 5, 28, 19, 15, 20, 33, 12, 17, 10 into a table of 9 slots with $$h(k) = k \bmod 9$$, resolving by chaining. Report the maximum, minimum and average chain lengths.

  1. Reduce every key: 5→5, 28→1, 19→1, 15→6, 20→2, 33→6, 12→3, 17→8, 10→1.
  2. Read the chain lengths off the slots: slot 1 holds 28, 19, 10 (three); slot 6 holds 15, 33 (two); slots 2, 3, 5, 8 hold one each; slots 0, 4, 7 hold none.
  3. Maximum 3, minimum 0, average = nine keys over nine slots = 1.

Answer: 3, 0, 1. The minimum is 0 only because three slots are empty. Note that the average is fixed by $$n$$ and $$m$$ alone: it is 1 for any arrangement of nine keys in nine slots. The page recomputes all three.

Example 2 — Linear probing with a shifted home slot

GATE CSE 2026 Set 1. Table $$P[0 \ldots 10]$$, open addressing with linear probing, $$h(x) = (x+7) \bmod 11$$. Insert 1, 13, 22, 15, 11, 24 in that order. Which positions are empty afterwards?

  1. 1 → $$8 \bmod 11 = 8$$, free. 13 → $$20 \bmod 11 = 9$$, free. 22 → $$29 \bmod 11 = 7$$, free. 15 → $$22 \bmod 11 = 0$$, free.
  2. 11 → $$18 \bmod 11 = 7$$, taken by 22. Walk 8 (1), 9 (13), 10 free → slot 10.
  3. 24 → $$31 \bmod 11 = 9$$, taken by 13. Walk 10 (11), then wrap to 0 (15), then 1 free → slot 1.
  4. Occupied: 0, 1, 7, 8, 9, 10. Empty: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

Answer: only slot 2 among the four options offered. Two things make this item fair — the keys and the order are given, so it is a trace, and the shift by 7 is applied before probing, not during it.

Example 3 — Double hashing, one probe index

GATE CSE 2020. $$h_1(k) = k \bmod 23$$, $$h_2(k) = 1 + (k \bmod 19)$$, table size 23. Give the address returned by probe 1, counting the probe sequence from probe 0, for $$k = 90$$.

  1. $$h_1(90) = 90 \bmod 23 = 21$$, because $$23 \times 3 = 69$$ and $$90 - 69 = 21$$.
  2. $$h_2(90) = 1 + (90 \bmod 19) = 1 + 14 = 15$$, because $$19 \times 4 = 76$$ and $$90 - 76 = 14$$.
  3. Probe 1 means $$i = 1$$: $$(21 + 1 \times 15) \bmod 23 = 36 \bmod 23 = 13$$.

Answer: 13. Nothing is inserted here, so no table is traced — the whole item is one substitution. The trap is reading "probe 1" as the first probe, which would give the home slot 21.

Example 4 — A full insertion trace under double hashing

GATE CSE 2025 Set 1. $$h_1(k) = k \bmod 11$$, $$h_2(k) = 1 + (k \bmod 7)$$, $$m = 11$$, probe $$i$$ is $$[h_1(k) + i\,h_2(k)] \bmod m$$. Insert 63, 50, 25, 79, 67, 24 in that order. Where does 24 land?

  1. 63 → 8, 50 → 6, 25 → 3, 79 → 2, 67 → 1. All five are free on probe 0, so each takes its home slot.
  2. 24: $$h_1 = 2$$, $$h_2 = 1 + 3 = 4$$. Probe 0 → slot 2, taken by 79. Probe 1 → $$(2+4) \bmod 11 = 6$$, taken by 50. Probe 2 → $$(2+8) \bmod 11 = 10$$, free.

Answer: slot 10. The step of 4 skips straight past the occupied run, which is exactly the behaviour that distinguishes double hashing from linear probing. The page runs the trace and checks the slot.

Example 5 — A probability item with no table at all

GATE CSE 2014 Set 3. A table of 100 slots, collisions resolved by chaining, simple uniform hashing. What is the probability that the first three slots are unfilled after the first three insertions?

  1. Each insertion lands in a uniformly random slot, independently. The probability a given insertion avoids slots 0, 1, 2 is 97/100.
  2. Three independent insertions must all avoid them, so multiply: $$(97/100)^3$$.
  3. That is $$912673 / 10^6 \approx 0.9127$$.

Answer: $$(97 \times 97 \times 97)/100^3$$. The tempting wrong answer is the falling product $$(99 \times 98 \times 97)/100^3$$, which is the probability that three insertions land in distinct slots avoiding the first three — a different question. Chaining is what makes the three insertions independent; under open addressing they are not.

Example 6 — Which function spreads best

GATE CSE 2015 Set 2. Over $$i = 0$$ to 2020, which of $$i^2 \bmod 10$$, $$i^3 \bmod 10$$, $$(11i^2) \bmod 10$$, $$(12i^2) \bmod 10$$ distributes keys most uniformly over ten buckets?

  1. Reducing a product modulo 10 depends only on the last digit, so only the residues 0 to 9 matter.
  2. Squares of 0 to 9 end in 0, 1, 4, 9, 6, 5, 6, 9, 4, 1 — six distinct values, and 0, 1, 4, 9 are each hit twice. Four buckets are never touched.
  3. Cubes of 0 to 9 end in 0, 1, 8, 7, 4, 5, 6, 3, 2, 9 — ten distinct values, one each. The map is a bijection on the last digit.
  4. $$11i^2 \equiv i^2$$ and $$12i^2 \equiv 2i^2 \pmod{10}$$, so both are at best as good as the square, and the second is worse.

Answer: $$i^3 \bmod 10$$. The page counts the distinct residues for both and reports 10 against 6. This is the practical face of "a good hash function must mix all the bits": cubing is invertible on the last digit, squaring is not.

Counterexample 1 — quadratic probing that cannot finish

Take $$m = 8$$ and the rule $$h(k,i) = (h'(k) + i^2) \bmod 8$$. The offsets for $$i = 0,1,2,\ldots$$ are 0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, which reduce modulo 8 to 0, 1, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 1. Only three residues ever appear: 0, 1 and 4.

So a key whose home slot is 0, 1 or 4 can only ever look at slots congruent to 0, 1 or 4 modulo 8 — that is, at slots 0, 1, 4 and their partners 8, 9, 12, which do not exist. If those three slots are occupied, the insertion fails even though five slots are empty.

Mathematical derivation The page computes the reachable residue set for $$m = 8$$ and reports 3 of 8, then for $$m = 11$$ and $$m = 13$$ and reports 6 and 7, which are $$(m+1)/2$$. Condition violated: the theorem that guarantees a free slot needs $$m$$ prime and $$\alpha \le 1/2$$. With $$m = 8$$ neither holds.

Counterexample 2 — double hashing with a step that shares a factor

Take $$m = 8$$ and a secondary hash that always returns 2. The probe sequence from home slot 0 is 0, 2, 4, 6, 0, 2, 4, 6 — four slots, then it repeats. Slots 1, 3, 5, 7 are unreachable.

In general the walk visits exactly $$m / \gcd(h_2(k), m)$$ distinct slots, so full coverage needs $$\gcd(h_2(k), m) = 1$$ for every key.

Mathematical derivation The page checks that with $$m = 23$$ prime, every value of $$1 + (k \bmod 19)$$ from 1 to 19 is coprime to 23, so the 2020 item's scheme always covers all 23 slots. It also counts the reachable set for $$m = 8$$ with step 2 and reports 4 of 8. Condition violated: the step and the table size must be coprime. Choosing a prime $$m$$ is the standard way to make that automatic.

Counterexample 3 — deleting a key makes another key vanish

Table of 7 slots, $$h(k) = k \bmod 7$$, linear probing. Insert 10, 3, 17, 24 in that order: 10 → slot 3; 3 → slot 3 taken, so slot 4; 17 → 3, 4 taken, so slot 5; 24 → 3, 4, 5 taken, so slot 6.

Now delete key 3 by writing an empty marker in slot 4. Search for 24: home slot 3 holds 10, slot 4 is empty, so the search stops and reports 24 absent. But 24 is sitting in slot 6.

Mathematical derivation The page performs both deletions and reports the outcome: the naive one loses key 24, the tombstone one finds it at probe 4. Condition violated: "empty" is being used both as "this slot was never used" and as "this slot is free now". Those are different states and open addressing needs both.

Boundary — $$\alpha$$ approaching 1

Every open-addressing formula has $$(1-\alpha)$$ in a denominator, so the cost is infinite at $$\alpha = 1$$ and large before it. At $$\alpha = 0.99$$ an unsuccessful linear-probing search averages 5000.5 probes and a double-hashing search 100 probes.

A question that asks for the cost at $$\alpha = 1$$ has no finite answer; the correct response is that the table is full and no unsuccessful search can terminate before scanning all $$m$$ slots. Practical tables rehash at $$\alpha$$ between about 0.5 and 0.75 for this reason. Educational interpretation The 0.5 to 0.75 range is the usual engineering advice, not a syllabus rule and not a theorem.

Boundary — an empty table and a single key

With $$n = 0$$, $$\alpha = 0$$ and every formula gives 1 probe: the home slot is empty, so the search ends immediately. With $$n = m - 1$$ in open addressing, exactly one slot is free and the unsuccessful cost is the whole walk, up to $$m$$ probes.

Two boundary facts that items exploit: an unsuccessful search under open addressing needs at most $$m$$ probes, because the walk is a permutation and would repeat; and a successful search needs at most $$m$$ too. Under chaining neither bound holds — a chain can be longer than any constant, up to $$n$$.

Boundary — negative and non-integer keys

$$-17 \bmod 7$$ in mathematics is 4, but in C it is -3, because C truncates toward zero. A hash function must map into $$0 \ldots m-1$$, so the implementation needs $$((k \bmod m) + m) \bmod m$$.

Strings are the other standard case: a hash over characters folds them, for example $$h = 31h + c$$ per character with the arithmetic done modulo the table size. Educational interpretation The multiplier 31 is a widespread convention, not a syllabus requirement; the requirement is only that the fold depends on every character and on its position.

1. "Hash table search is $$O(1)$$"

Why it tempts: it is said constantly, and it is true often enough that it feels like a fact about the data structure.

Minimal counterexample: a table of 1000 slots holding 1000 keys that all hash to slot 0. Every search walks a chain of length 1000. The worst case is $$\Theta(n)$$, and an adversary can force it, which is why GATE CSE 2023's answer was universal hashing.

Correct rule: the expected cost is $$\Theta(1+\alpha)$$ under uniform hashing; the worst case is $$\Theta(n)$$. Say which one you mean.

2. "Open addressing and closed hashing are different schemes"

Why it tempts: the words point the wrong way. "Open" sounds like lists hanging off the table; "closed" sounds like a sealed array.

Minimal counterexample: a stem that says "closed hashing" and gives a probe function. There are no lists anywhere; the keys are in the array.

Correct rule: open addressing = closed hashing = probing. Chaining = open hashing. Read the stem for the word probe or list, not for the word open.

3. "A good hash function should have no collisions"

Why it tempts: collisions are described as a problem, so avoiding them sounds like the goal.

Minimal counterexample: the pigeonhole principle. A function from a set larger than $$m$$ into $$m$$ slots must collide. GATE CSE 2022's scheme used two functions, one for odd keys and one for even, and still had $$n/m$$ expected keys per slot.

Correct rule: the goal is even spread, and a resolution scheme for what spread cannot prevent.

4. "Quadratic probing always finds a free slot if one exists"

Why it tempts: it is strictly better than linear probing at everything the eye can see — no trails, no clusters.

Minimal counterexample: $$m = 8$$ with offsets $$i^2$$ reaches only slots 0, 1 and 4 modulo 8. Five slots empty, insertion impossible. The page reports 3 of 8 reachable.

Correct rule: the guarantee needs $$m$$ prime and $$\alpha \le 1/2$$, giving at least $$(m+1)/2$$ distinct probes. Without those conditions, "cannot be inserted" is a legitimate answer.

5. "Deleting in open addressing means marking the slot empty"

Why it tempts: the key is gone, the slot is free, and the code is one assignment.

Minimal counterexample: the four-key table above. Marking slot 4 empty makes key 24 in slot 6 unreachable, and the search reports it absent.

Correct rule: a third state, the tombstone — search walks over it, insert may reuse it, and the table is rehashed when tombstones accumulate.

6. "The load factor is a percentage, so it is at most 1"

Why it tempts: the word "factor" and the symbol $$\alpha$$ both suggest a fraction of a whole.

Minimal counterexample: GATE CSE 2015 Set 3 — 25 slots, 2000 elements, answer 80. Under chaining, $$\alpha$$ has no upper bound.

Correct rule: $$\alpha = n/m$$ is a ratio of counts. It is bounded by 1 only for open addressing, and only because each key needs its own cell.

7. "Successful and unsuccessful search cost the same"

Why it tempts: both are "a search", and both formulas start with 1 plus something.

Minimal counterexample: linear probing at $$\alpha = 0.9$$. Successful 5.5 probes, unsuccessful 50.5 — a factor of nine.

Correct rule: a successful search stops at its key; an unsuccessful one runs to the end of the walk. Use the formula for the kind of search the stem names.

8. "Linear probing and quadratic probing cluster the same way"

Why it tempts: both are open addressing, both are deterministic walks, both degrade at high load.

Minimal counterexample: two keys with home slot 5. Under linear probing both walk 5, 6, 7, … together, so the run they create is shared. Under quadratic probing both walk 5, 6, 9, 14, … — still the same path as each other, but no longer a contiguous run.

Correct rule: linear probing suffers primary clustering (contiguous runs merge). Quadratic probing removes primary but keeps secondary clustering (same home slot, same path). Only double hashing removes both.

Pattern A — trace the table, then answer a part

The stem gives a function, a table size and an insertion order, and asks for one slot, one chain length, or which positions are empty. This is the commonest hashing item and it is pure bookkeeping.

Procedure: draw the table as a row of $$m$$ boxes. Take the keys one at a time. Write the home slot above each key. For a collision, write the walk out slot by slot, wrapping with modulo. Only then answer the question asked.

Common wrong approach: computing all the home slots first and assuming each key takes its home slot, forgetting that a key inserted earlier has already moved.

Pattern B — one probe index, no table

The stem gives two hash functions and a probe number and asks for one address. Seen in 2020 and again in 2025 Set 1 as a full trace.

Procedure: evaluate both hashes as plain arithmetic, then substitute into $$(h_1 + i h_2) \bmod m$$, reducing once at the end. Check the answer lies in $$0 \ldots m-1$$.

Common wrong approach: using $$i = 0$$ for "probe 1", or reducing modulo the wrong modulus — the second hash is computed modulo the table size, not modulo its own range.

Pattern C — a count from a distribution

"Maximum, minimum and average chain length", "the load factor", "the probability the first three slots are unfilled". No trace is needed; these follow from $$n$$, $$m$$ and independence.

Procedure: the average chain length is always $$n/m$$. The minimum is 0 exactly when some slot is empty. For a probability, decide whether the trials are independent — chaining makes them so, open addressing does not — then multiply.

Common wrong approach: using a falling product $$(m-1)(m-2)\ldots/m^k$$ for an item about specified slots, when the slots are fixed and the insertions are independent.

Pattern D — scheme choice and worst case

"Which strategy best counteracts an adversary", "the advantage of chaining over open addressing", "the worst-case search time". These are conceptual and the answer is usually one sentence.

Procedure: name the property being asked about — adversarial keys, deletion, space, cache locality — and match it to the scheme. Universal hashing answers adversaries; chaining answers deletion; open addressing answers space.

Common wrong approach: choosing "division method with a prime" for an adversary item. A prime modulus spreads a random key set well; it does not defeat a key set chosen after the function is known.

Pattern E — design a hash function

"Which of these functions distributes keys most uniformly", or a stem that fixes $$m$$ and asks what goes wrong. Seen in 2015 Set 2.

Procedure: reduce the function to its action on the last digit or last $$p$$ bits. Count how many distinct residues it produces. A function that is a bijection on those residues is uniform; one that is not, is not.

Common wrong approach: testing the four options on a handful of values. Sampling cannot distinguish a bijection from a near-bijection reliably; count the residues instead.

The assumption behind every formula

The four expected-probe formulas assume simple uniform hashing. If a stem says the keys are adversarial, or the function is fixed and published, or the key set is a run of consecutive integers with a bad modulus, the formulas do not apply and only the trace does.

This is the single assumption that separates a correct answer from a plausible one on this topic. When a stem gives concrete keys, assume nothing and trace.

NATEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2026 Set 2

The keys 5, 28, 19, 15, 26, 33, 12, 17, 10 are inserted into a hash table using $$h(k) = k \bmod 9$$. Collisions are resolved by chaining. After all the keys are inserted, the length of the longest chain is ______.

Answer: 3

Residues modulo 9: 5→5, 28→1, 19→1, 15→6, 26→8, 33→6, 12→3, 17→8, 10→1.

Slot 1 receives 28, 19 and 10 — three keys. Slot 6 receives 15 and 33. Slot 8 receives 26 and 17. Slots 3 and 5 get one each. The longest chain is 3.

The average chain length is 9/9 = 1 and the minimum is 0 (slots 0, 2, 4, 7 are empty), so an item that asked for the average would want 1, not 3. The page computes all three.

MSQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2026 Set 1

Consider a hash table $$P[0,1,\ldots,10]$$ that is initially empty, maintained using open addressing with linear probing. The hash function is $$h(x) = (x+7) \bmod 11$$. The sequence of insertions is 1, 13, 22, 15, 11, 24. Which of the following positions is/are empty after these insertions?

  1. 0
  2. 10
  3. 2
  4. 1

Answer: C only — position 2

Home slots: 1→8, 13→9, 22→7, 15→0, all free. Then 11 hashes to 7, which is taken, so it walks 8, 9 and stops at 10. Then 24 hashes to 9, taken; walks 10 (taken), wraps to 0 (taken), then 1, free.

Final table: slot 0 holds 15, 1 holds 24, 7 holds 22, 8 holds 1, 9 holds 13, 10 holds 11. Slots 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 are empty.

So only position 2 is empty among the four offered. 0 holds 15, 10 holds 11, 1 holds 24. The trap is dropping the modulo wrap for key 24, which would stop the walk at slot 10 and leave slot 1 free.

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2025 Set 1

In a double hashing scheme, $$h_1(k) = k \bmod 11$$ and $$h_2(k) = 1 + (k \bmod 7)$$ are the auxiliary hash functions, and the table size $$m$$ is 11. The hash function for the $$i$$-th probe is $$[h_1(k) + i\,h_2(k)] \bmod m$$. The keys 63, 50, 25, 79, 67, 24 are inserted in that order. The slot at which key 24 gets stored is ______.

Answer: 10

The first five keys take their home slots with no collision: 63→8, 50→6, 25→3, 79→2, 67→1.

For 24: $$h_1 = 24 \bmod 11 = 2$$ and $$h_2 = 1 + (24 \bmod 7) = 1 + 3 = 4$$. Probe 0 gives slot 2 (taken by 79). Probe 1 gives $$(2+4) \bmod 11 = 6$$ (taken by 50). Probe 2 gives $$(2+8) \bmod 11 = 10$$, which is free.

The trap is answering 11 because slot 10 is the eleventh position. Slots are numbered from 0, so the eleventh slot has index 10.

NATEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2020

Consider a double hashing scheme in which the primary hash function is $$h_1(k) = k \bmod 23$$ and the secondary hash function is $$h_2(k) = 1 + (k \bmod 19)$$. The table size is 23. The address returned by probe 1 in the probe sequence, assuming the probe sequence begins at probe 0, for key value $$k = 90$$ is ______.

Answer: 13

$$h_1(90) = 90 \bmod 23 = 21$$. $$h_2(90) = 1 + (90 \bmod 19) = 1 + 14 = 15$$.

Probe 1 means $$i=1$$: $$(21 + 15) \bmod 23 = 36 \bmod 23 = 13$$.

The page also checks that with $$m = 23$$ prime, every possible step from 1 to 19 is coprime to 23, so the walk always covers all 23 slots. That is why this scheme cannot get stuck.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2014 Set 3

Consider a hash table with 100 slots. Collisions are resolved using chaining. Assuming simple uniform hashing, what is the probability that the first 3 slots are unfilled after the first 3 insertions?

  1. $$(97 \times 97 \times 97)/100^3$$
  2. $$(99 \times 98 \times 97)/100^3$$
  3. $$(97 \times 96 \times 95)/100^3$$
  4. $$(97 \times 96 \times 95)/(3! \times 100^3)$$

Answer: A — $$(97/100)^3$$

Slots 0, 1 and 2 must stay empty, so every one of the three insertions must land in one of the other 97 slots. Each insertion does that with probability 97/100, and under chaining the three insertions are independent, so the probabilities multiply.

B is the probability that three insertions land in distinct slots avoiding 0, 1, 2 — a different question, because here the insertions may share a slot. C additionally forbids repeats. D divides by 3!, which would matter only if the order of the insertions were irrelevant.

The value is 0.912673, and the page computes it. Note that under open addressing the insertions would not be independent, and this product would be wrong.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2015 Set 2

Which one of the following hash functions on integers will distribute keys most uniformly over 10 buckets numbered 0 to 9, for $$i$$ ranging from 0 to 2020?

  1. $$h(i) = i^2 \bmod 10$$
  2. $$h(i) = i^3 \bmod 10$$
  3. $$h(i) = (11 i^2) \bmod 10$$
  4. $$h(i) = (12 i^2) \bmod 10$$

Answer: B — $$i^3 \bmod 10$$

Only the last digit matters. The squares of 0 to 9 end in 0, 1, 4, 9, 6, 5, 6, 9, 4, 1: six distinct residues, with 0, 1, 4 and 9 each produced twice. Buckets 2, 3, 7 and 8 are never used.

The cubes of 0 to 9 end in 0, 1, 8, 7, 4, 5, 6, 3, 2, 9: ten distinct residues, one each. Cubing is a bijection on the last digit; squaring is not.

C reduces to $$i^2$$ and D to $$2i^2$$ modulo 10, so both are no better than A. The page counts 10 distinct residues for the cube and 6 for the square.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 1996

An advantage of a chained hash table (external hashing) over the open addressing scheme is:

  1. Worst case complexity of search operations is less
  2. Space used is less
  3. Deletion is easier
  4. None of the above

Answer: C — deletion is easier

In a chained table a slot's list is independent of every other slot, so unlinking a node cannot disturb any other key's search. In open addressing, emptying a slot breaks every probe walk that passed through it, which is why a tombstone is needed.

A is false: the worst case for chaining is a chain of length $$n$$, while open addressing is bounded by $$m$$ probes. B is false: chaining stores $$n$$ nodes plus $$m$$ pointers, open addressing stores exactly $$m$$ cells. D is therefore wrong.

This is the one place where chaining is strictly better, and it is a correctness advantage rather than a speed one.

MSQHardOriginal practice

A table of size $$m = 8$$ uses open addressing with the probe rule $$h(k,i) = (h'(k) + i^2) \bmod 8$$. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. Every slot of the table is reachable by some probe.
  2. Only three of the eight slots are reachable by the probe sequence.
  3. A key whose reachable slots are all occupied cannot be inserted even though free slots exist.
  4. With $$m = 11$$ instead, the first six probes are guaranteed to be distinct.

Answer: B, C and D

The offsets $$i^2 \bmod 8$$ for $$i = 0,1,2,\ldots$$ are 0, 1, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 1, so only residues 0, 1 and 4 ever appear. B is true and A is false.

Because only three residues are reachable, a key can be locked out while five slots are empty. C is true, and it is the counterexample that defeats "quadratic probing always finds a free slot".

For $$m = 11$$ prime, $$i^2 \equiv j^2$$ only when $$i \equiv j$$ or $$i \equiv -j$$, so $$i = 0 \ldots 5$$ give six distinct residues. D is true, and that is exactly the guarantee that makes prime $$m$$ with $$\alpha \le 1/2$$ safe. The page computes the reachable sets: 3 of 8, and 6 of 11.

MCQHardOriginal practice

A table of 7 slots uses $$h(k) = k \bmod 7$$ with linear probing. Keys 10, 3, 17, 24 are inserted in that order, so the table becomes slot 3: 10, slot 4: 3, slot 5: 17, slot 6: 24. Key 3 is then removed by writing the empty marker in slot 4. What happens when key 24 is searched for?

  1. Found in slot 6 after four probes
  2. Reported absent after two probes
  3. Found in slot 6 after two probes
  4. The search loops for ever

Answer: B — reported absent after two probes

Search for 24 begins at home slot 3. Slot 3 holds 10, not 24, so it continues to slot 4. Slot 4 is now empty, and an empty slot is the signal that the key is not present, so the search stops: two probes, answer absent.

But 24 is sitting in slot 6. A describes the table before the deletion. C is impossible — the search has already stopped. D confuses this with a non-terminating probe sequence, which is a different failure.

The fix is a tombstone: mark slot 4 as deleted rather than empty, so search walks over it and finds 24 at probe 4. The page performs both deletions and reports the two outcomes.

NATMediumOriginal practice

A hash table of 1000 slots holds 500 keys under open addressing with double hashing, and simple uniform hashing applies. Using the standard expected-cost formula, the expected number of probes for an unsuccessful search is ______. Give your answer to one decimal place.

Answer: 2.0

$$\alpha = n/m = 500/1000 = 0.5$$. For double hashing the expected unsuccessful cost is $$1/(1-\alpha) = 1/0.5 = 2.0$$.

Under linear probing the same load factor would give $$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + 1/(1-\alpha)^2\right) = 2.5$$, and under chaining $$1 + \alpha = 1.5$$. The scheme changes the answer, so read which one the stem names. The page measures 2.50 for linear probing at this load factor against a formula of 2.50.

MCQEasyOriginal practice

Which statement about the load factor $$\alpha$$ is correct?

  1. Under chaining $$\alpha$$ can exceed 1; under open addressing it cannot reach 1.
  2. $$\alpha$$ is always at most 1 because it is a fraction.
  3. $$\alpha = m/n$$ is the number of slots per key.
  4. $$\alpha$$ must be an integer.

Answer: A

$$\alpha = n/m$$. Chaining puts keys outside the table, so $$n$$ may be far larger than $$m$$: GATE CSE 2015 Set 3's table of 25 slots holding 2000 elements has $$\alpha = 80$$. Open addressing puts each key in its own cell, so $$n \le m$$ always.

B is the misconception. C inverts the ratio. D confuses a ratio with a count — 9 keys in 7 slots gives $$\alpha = 9/7$$, not an integer.

This is also the closest confused pair in the topic: $$\alpha$$ is a ratio of counts, while $$m$$ and $$n$$ are counts. Reading one for the other is the commonest arithmetic slip here.

MCQMediumOriginal practice

A hash table stores $$n$$ keys chosen by an adversary who knows the hash function, in $$m$$ slots with $$n > m$$. Which single change gives the best guarantee against that adversary?

  1. Use the division method with $$m$$ prime.
  2. Use the multiplication method with the golden-ratio constant.
  3. Choose the hash function at random from a universal family, after the keys are fixed.
  4. Increase $$m$$ until it exceeds $$n$$.

Answer: C — universal hashing

A fixed function, however good, can be inverted: the adversary computes keys that share a slot and submits them. Randomising the choice of function removes that power, because the adversary must commit to the keys first. The guarantee is that for any two distinct keys at most a $$1/m$$ fraction of the family collides on them, so the expected chain length is $$n/m$$ whatever the keys.

A and B are the answers to a different question — spreading a random key set — and both are still deterministic. D avoids the stated condition $$n > m$$ rather than answering it.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2023 asked this with a malicious key generator, and the keyed answer was universal hashing. The page checks the $$1/m$$ bound exhaustively for $$p = 7$$, $$m = 3$$: the worst pair collides on 10 of 42 functions, against a bound of 14.

The revision sheet

Everything below is on one screen. If you remember only this tab, you can attempt most hashing items.

Definitions

  • Home slot: $$h(k)$$.
  • Collision: distinct keys, same home slot.
  • Load factor: $$\alpha = n/m$$. A ratio, not a percentage.
  • Chaining = open hashing. Open addressing = closed hashing = probing.
  • Tombstone: a third slot state, between occupied and empty.

Probe rules

  • Linear: $$(h'(k)+i) \bmod m$$. Primary clustering.
  • Quadratic: $$(h'(k)+c_1 i + c_2 i^2) \bmod m$$. Secondary clustering.
  • Double: $$(h_1(k)+i\,h_2(k)) \bmod m$$. No clustering.
  • Probe 0 is the home slot.

The four expected costs

  • Chaining, unsuccessful: $$1+\alpha$$.
  • Chaining, successful: $$1+\alpha/2$$.
  • Linear, unsuccessful: $$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2}\right)$$.
  • Linear, successful: $$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)$$.
  • Double, unsuccessful: $$\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$$.
  • Double, successful: $$\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$$.

The three conditions

  • Open addressing needs $$\alpha < 1$$.
  • Quadratic probing needs $$m$$ prime and $$\alpha \le 1/2$$ to guarantee a free slot.
  • Double hashing needs $$\gcd(h_2(k), m) = 1$$; a prime $$m$$ gives it for free.

Decision procedure

  1. Which scheme? Read for list or probe.
  2. Count or estimate? Keys given → trace. Only $$n, m, \alpha$$ → formula.
  3. A probe index? Substitute $$i$$, with probe 0 first.
  4. Successful or unsuccessful search? Different formula, different trace.

Three sentences to keep

Collisions are inevitable, so resolution is the design question. A probe walk must cover the table or keys are lost while space exists. Hashing is $$O(1)$$ on average and $$\Theta(n)$$ in the worst case, and every average formula is conditional on uniform hashing.

Numbers worth memorising

At $$\alpha = 0.5$$: linear 2.5 probes unsuccessful, 1.5 successful; double 2.0 and 1.39; chaining 1.5 and 1.25. At $$\alpha = 0.9$$: linear 50.5 and 5.5; double 10 and 2.56; chaining 1.9 and 1.45. Linear probing is usable below about $$\alpha = 0.5$$ and expensive above 0.9.
