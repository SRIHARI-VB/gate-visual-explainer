Topic and objective

TOPIC: The comparison lower bound for sorting — the decision tree, the leaf count, the ceiling of $$n!$$, and the exact number of comparisons for small $$n$$.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Turn any comparison-based sorting algorithm into a binary decision tree, argue that a correct algorithm needs at least $$n!$$ leaves, deduce that a tree of height $$h$$ has at most $$2^h$$ leaves, and conclude $$h \ge \lceil \log_2(n!) \rceil$$. Compute that ceiling exactly in whole numbers for $$n = 1$$ to $$22$$, say why the ceiling and the base are what they are, and apply Stirling to get $$\Theta(n\log n)$$. Then state precisely when the ceiling is achieved ($$n \le 11$$) and when it is not (from $$n = 12$$, where the floor is $$29$$ and the proven minimum is $$30$$).

GATE RELEVANCE: Class A — Section 5 of the syllabus names “Searching, sorting, hashing. Asymptotic worst case time and space complexity.” This lesson is the standard content of that second sentence for sorting: the worst-case asymptotic complexity of the sorting problem, not of one algorithm. The words “decision tree” and “lower bound” are not in the syllabus; the clause they belong to is, and three previous-year items ask it directly (1991, 2004, 1990).

PREREQUISITES: factorials and how fast they grow; binary trees and their height (trees); what $$\Theta$$, $$\Omega$$ and “worst case” mean (asymptotic complexity); having seen merge sort and insertion sort (sorting algorithms). No probability beyond “all orders equally likely”, and no calculus except one logarithm.

VISUALIZATION PLAN: eleven scenes. I read this topic as the lower bound and its exact arithmetic, not as a general course in sorting. The neighbouring lessons own the algorithms themselves and the exact comparison counts for selection problems; this one owns the decision-tree bound.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • $$n!$$ is the product $$1 \cdot 2 \cdots n$$ and counts the orderings of $$n$$ distinct items: $$5! = 120$$.
  • A binary tree has at most two children per node; its height is the number of edges on the longest path down from the root.
  • $$\log_2$$ asks “two to what power”: $$\log_2 120 \approx 6.907$$ because $$2^{6.9} \approx 120$$.
  • A ceiling rounds up to the next whole number: $$\lceil 6.907 \rceil = 7$$.

Helpful, not required: having seen that merge sort does $$n\lceil \log_2 n\rceil$$-ish comparisons and insertion sort does about $$n^2/2$$ in the worst case, so that the floor has something to be compared with.

Approximate learning time: 40–55 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. The algorithm is a tree. Each comparison is an internal node with two exits; each run is a root-to-leaf path; the worst case is the height.
  2. Leaves are orders. A correct algorithm must print a different answer for each of the $$n!$$ input orders, so it needs $$n!$$ leaves.
  3. Doubling caps the leaves. A binary tree of height $$h$$ has at most $$2^h$$ leaves, so $$2^h \ge n!$$ and $$h \ge \lceil \log_2(n!)\rceil$$.
  4. The floor is not always the answer. It is achieved for $$n \le 11$$; at $$n = 12$$ the floor is $$29$$ and the proven minimum is $$30$$.
  5. The model is conditional. Comparisons only. Counting sort and radix sort make none, so no decision tree exists and the floor does not bind them.

Intuition: twenty questions, with the answers only ever yes or no

Someone has hidden a shuffled deck of three cards and will only answer yes-or-no questions. How many questions before you know the order? There are six possible orders. One yes-or-no question splits them into two groups, so after $$k$$ questions you have at most $$2^k$$ distinguishable outcomes. Six orders therefore need at least three questions, because $$2^2 = 4 < 6 \le 8 = 2^3$$.

A comparison sort is exactly that game. The hidden thing is the input order, the questions are comparisons, and the answers are “the first is smaller” or “the second is smaller”. The number of questions in the worst case is the depth you must reach to separate all six orders.

That is the entire lower bound. Everything else in this lesson is the arithmetic of it, and one honest correction: the counting gives a floor, and a floor is not always reachable.

Educational interpretation The analogy is exact about the counting and silent about cost: a comparison here is assumed to cost one unit, whatever is being compared. If comparing two items is itself expensive, the bound still holds on the number of comparisons, but not on running time.

Formal definition

Fix an algorithm that sorts $$n$$ distinct items using only comparisons. Its decision tree is the binary tree built as follows: the root is the first comparison the algorithm makes; each internal node is a comparison; the two children of a node are the two possible answers; a leaf is a point at which the algorithm stops and prints its answer.

$$\text{height}(T) = \text{the worst-case number of comparisons made by the algorithm}$$

Read: the longest root-to-leaf path counts the comparisons of the unluckiest input, so the height is the worst case. The average of the leaf depths is the average case, over equally likely orders.

$$L(T) \ge n! \quad\text{and}\quad h \ge \lceil \log_2(n!) \rceil$$

Read: the tree needs at least one leaf per input order; a tree of height $$h$$ has at most $$2^h$$ leaves; so $$2^h \ge n!$$, and since $$h$$ is a whole number it is at least the ceiling.

The lower bound is the statement that every comparison-based algorithm has height at least $$\lceil \log_2(n!)\rceil$$. It is a statement about the problem, not about any one algorithm.

Verified fact This is the standard treatment: Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, volume 3, section 5.3.1, defines the minimum $$S(n)$$ and calls $$\lceil \lg n! \rceil$$ the information-theoretic lower bound. The derivation below is worked out here rather than quoted.

Derivation 1 — every comparison algorithm has a tree, and the tree is all that matters

Run the algorithm on a fixed input. At each step it either stops or makes one comparison, whose outcome is determined by the input. So the run is a path, and the set of all runs is a tree: two inputs that make the same comparisons in the same order with the same answers follow the same path.

The tree is finite because the algorithm must stop on every input. Its height is the largest number of comparisons any input costs. Nothing about the algorithm survives this translation except the tree, which is why the argument works for algorithms nobody has written yet.

$$\text{worst case of the algorithm} = \text{height of its tree}$$

Read: one number, read off one picture. If the tree is shallow, every input is cheap; if some path is deep, that input is expensive.

Derivation The page builds the tree for $$n = 3$$ and $$n = 4$$ and walks every input order through it, confirming that each order reaches its own leaf and that the deepest path is the length claimed.

Derivation 2 — a correct algorithm needs one leaf per input order

There are $$n!$$ orders in which $$n$$ distinct items can arrive, and the sorted answer reveals which order it was: given the sorted list and the original items, you can reconstruct the input order uniquely. So two different orders must end at two different leaves, because a leaf carries one printed answer.

$$L(T) \ge n!$$

Read: the number of leaves is at least the number of orders. If two orders shared a leaf, the algorithm would print one answer for both, and one of them would be wrong.

For $$n = 3$$ that is 6 leaves, and the drawn tree has exactly 6. For $$n = 4$$ it is 24. Nothing here uses the comparison model yet — only correctness.

Derivation Checked on the page: the built $$n = 3$$ tree has 6 distinct leaves and the built $$n = 4$$ tree has 24, with every one of the $$n!$$ orders reaching its own leaf.

Derivation 3 — a tree of height h holds at most 2 to the h leaves

Count by level. The root is one node. Each node has at most two children, so level 1 holds at most 2, level 2 at most 4, and level $$k$$ at most $$2^k$$. A tree of height $$h$$ has levels $$0$$ to $$h$$, and the leaves are a subset of the nodes, so the total number of leaves is at most the total number of nodes at the deepest level, which is $$2^h$$.

$$L(T) \le 2^{h}$$

Read: doubling at every level is the fastest a tree can widen, so no shape beats the full tree. A lopsided tree uses fewer leaves, never more.

Putting Derivations 2 and 3 together: $$n! \le L(T) \le 2^h$$, hence $$2^h \ge n!$$, hence $$h \ge \log_2(n!)$$, and since a height is an integer, $$h \ge \lceil \log_2(n!) \rceil$$.

Derivation The page counts the leaves of every binary tree shape up to height 6 and confirms the maximum is exactly $$2^h$$ each time — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.

Derivation 4 — why the ceiling, and why base two

Ceiling: a height is a count of comparisons, so it is a whole number. If $$\log_2(n!) = 6.907$$ then no tree of height 6 works, and the smallest usable height is 7. Rounding down is not a rounding error: a height-6 tree holds 64 leaves and $$5! = 120$$ orders, so 56 orders would have no leaf.

Base two: a comparison of two distinct items has exactly two possible outcomes. It has three possible relations — smaller, equal, larger — but “equal” cannot happen for distinct items, so a correct algorithm cannot rely on that branch, and a branch that is never taken contributes no leaves.

$$h \ge \big\lceil \log_2(n!) \big\rceil, \qquad \text{not } \big\lceil \log_3(n!) \big\rceil$$

Read: base three would give $$\log_3 120 = 4.36$$, suggesting five comparisons for five items, when seven are necessary. The base is the number of usable outcomes, and that number is two.

Derivation Both refutations are arithmetic on the page: $$2^6 = 64 < 120$$ and $$120 \le 128 = 2^7$$; and $$\lceil \log_3 120\rceil = 5 < 7$$.

Derivation 5 — Stirling turns the integer into Theta of n log n

The exact ceiling is what an exam asks for at small $$n$$. For the asymptotic statement, replace the factorial by Stirling's approximation, converted to base two:

$$\log_2(n!) = n\log_2 n - \frac{n}{\ln 2} + \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi n) + o(1)$$

Read: the leading term is $$n\log_2 n$$; the next is a constant times $$n$$ (since $$1/\ln 2 = 1.4427$$); the rest grows like $$\log n$$. So the floor grows like $$n\log n$$, up to a constant factor.

Because merge sort achieves $$n\lceil\log_2 n\rceil - 2^{\lceil \log_2 n\rceil} + 1$$ comparisons, which is the same rate, the floor is not merely $$\Omega(n\log n)$$ but $$\Theta(n\log n)$$ for the problem. That is the whole asymptotic content of the topic, and it is why the exam can ask for “the tightest lower bound” and expect $$n\log n$$.

Derivation The page checks Stirling against the exact logarithm at $$n = 8, 16, 32, 64$$ (agreement to two decimals) and checks the merge sort closed form against a simulated merge sort on every order up to $$n = 8$$.

Derivation 6 — the floor is not always achievable

Counting gives a lower bound. Whether an algorithm reaches it is a separate, much harder question, and the answer is known only for small $$n$$.

$$\lceil \log_2(n!)\rceil \;\le\; S(n) \;\le\; \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big\lceil \log_2 \tfrac{3k}{4} \Big\rceil$$

Read: $$S(n)$$ is the true minimum. The left side is what counting forces; the right side is what merge insertion (Ford–Johnson) achieves. Where the two meet, $$S(n)$$ is settled.

They meet for every $$n$$ from 1 to 11. At $$n = 12$$ the floor is 29, merge insertion spends 30, and exhaustive search proved that 29 is impossible. The gap is not monotone: it is 1 from 12 to 19, 0 again at 20 and 21, and 1 again at 22.

Verified fact The proven minima are OEIS A036604: 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 26, 30, 34, 38, 42, 46, 50, 54, 58, 62, 66, 71. The $$n = 12$$ value was settled by exhaustive search (Wells, 1965); 13, 14 and 22 were proved later by Peczarski, and 16, 17, 18 by Stober and Weiss. Derivation Every gap on the page is recomputed against the exact ceiling, and merge insertion's count is computed by the page, not copied.

Derivation 7 — the average case, from the same tree

Average the leaf depths instead of taking the maximum. For a binary tree with $$L$$ leaves, the average depth is at least $$\log_2 L$$, because the balanced tree is the shape that minimises the average and the balanced tree with $$L$$ leaves has average depth about $$\log_2 L$$.

$$\text{average comparisons} \;\ge\; \log_2(n!)$$

Read: substitute $$L \ge n!$$. So the average case has the same $$n\log n$$ floor as the worst case, with a smaller constant — and a lopsided tree pays for its short paths with very long ones.

The best case is not bounded this way. Insertion sort sorts an already sorted array in $$n - 1$$ comparisons, which is below the floor for every $$n \ge 3$$. A floor on the worst case says nothing about the best case.

Derivation Measured on the page: the optimal $$n = 4$$ tree averages 4.667 comparisons over its 24 paths against a floor of 4.585; insertion sort averages 4.917 over the 24 orders; and its best case is 3.

Page 1 of 2

n = 0 and n = 1: no comparison at all

An empty list and a one-element list are already sorted. The tree is a single leaf, its height is 0, and the formula gives $$\lceil \log_2 1\rceil = 0$$. It agrees.

$$n = 2$$ gives $$\lceil\log_2 2\rceil = 1$$, and one comparison decides the order — so here the floor is met, with no slack at all: $$2^1 = 2 = 2!$$.

Verified fact The page's table starts at $$n = 1$$ for this reason: those two rows are the ones an examiner can check by hand, and a bound that failed there would be suspect.

Equal keys: the model changes, and so does the count

Everything here assumes distinct items. If keys may repeat, two different input orders can have the same sorted output, so those orders may share a leaf and the leaf count is no longer $$n!$$. The correct count then uses the number of distinct multiset permutations, which is smaller.

The usual exam convention is that a comparison sort of $$n$$ distinct keys is being asked about, because that is the case where the bound is sharp. If a question says “may contain duplicates”, the $$n!$$ count is not the right one.

Educational interpretation A stable sort is required to keep equal keys in their input order, which restores the need for distinct leaves — that is one reason stability is a real constraint and not decoration.

Worst case, average case, best case: three different questions

The floor of $$\lceil\log_2(n!)\rceil$$ is a worst-case statement, and the same counting gives an average-case floor of $$\log_2(n!)$$ without the ceiling. It says nothing about the best case.

Insertion sort on eight items: best 7, average about 15.7, worst 28. The floor is 16. The best case is below it and the worst is above it, and both are correct statements.

Derivation All three insertion sort figures are simulated on the page over every order of eight items; the page also checks that $$n - 1$$ is below the floor for every $$n$$ from 3 to 12.

Three-way comparisons change the base

A machine that answers “smaller, equal, larger” in one instruction gives a ternary decision tree, and then the floor becomes $$\lceil\log_3(n!)\rceil$$ — smaller, because each node has three exits. At $$n = 5$$ that is 5 instead of 7.

This is not a loophole either: it costs a more powerful comparison. GATE's standard model is the two-outcome comparison, and a question that offers $$\log_3$$ as an option is testing whether you know that.

Educational interpretation The rule to carry: the base of the logarithm is the number of outcomes the model actually allows, and for two distinct keys under a normal comparison that number is two.

Non-comparison sorts are outside the model entirely

Counting sort, radix sort and bucket sort learn about the keys by reading their values, not by comparing pairs. There is no decision tree, so there is nothing for the counting argument to count, and they can beat $$n\log n$$.

They pay for it with assumptions the comparison model does not make: keys are integers (or short strings) in a range that is known and small. Counting sort's cost is $$n + k$$ for a range of size $$k$$, so a range of size $$n^2$$ makes it quadratic.

Verified fact The page runs a counting sort on six keys and counts its comparisons: zero, with the output correctly sorted. That is the escape route, measured rather than asserted.

Each comparison is one unit, and that is an assumption

The bound counts comparisons, not seconds. It assumes every comparison costs the same and that everything else the algorithm does is at most proportional to the comparisons.

That is why the bound transfers to running time for sorting of machine words, and why it does not transfer directly to sorting long strings, where one comparison may scan many characters. There, the right measure is comparisons times the cost of a comparison.

Educational interpretation This assumption is the reason the phrase “comparison-based” is in every statement of the theorem. Without it the theorem is not even false — it is meaningless.

1. “The bound is $$\lfloor \log_2(n!)\rfloor$$”

Wrong. Smallest counterexample: $$n = 5$$. $$\log_2 120 = 6.907$$, so rounding down gives 6, and a height-6 tree has $$2^6 = 64$$ leaves. There are 120 orders, so 56 of them would share a leaf with another and one of each pair would print the wrong answer.

The height is an integer, so it is at least the ceiling. There is no case where rounding down is safe.

Derivation The page checks $$2^h \ge n!$$ and $$2^{h-1} < n!$$ for every $$n$$ from 1 to 22, which is exactly the statement that $$h$$ is the ceiling and nothing smaller works.

2. “A comparison has three outcomes, so the base is three”

Wrong. Smallest counterexample: $$n = 5$$. $$\lceil \log_3 120\rceil = 5$$, but five comparisons cannot sort five items — the page's exhaustive search returns 7, and the 1991 paper's answer is 7.

The third outcome is “equal”, which cannot occur for distinct keys. A branch that can never be taken adds no leaves, so the tree is binary and the base is two.

Derivation Any ternary branch taken by an algorithm on distinct keys would need an input with equal keys to be useful; for distinct keys it is dead code, and dead code cannot reduce the height.

3. “The bound is the minimum number of comparisons”

Wrong from $$n = 12$$ onward. Smallest counterexample: $$n = 12$$. The ceiling is 29 ($$2^{28} < 12! \le 2^{29}$$), but the proven minimum is 30. Twenty-nine comparisons are provably impossible, even though the counting does not rule them out.

The counting argument is necessary, not sufficient: it says a shallow tree would have too few leaves, and it cannot see the subtler obstructions that make a 29-comparison tree impossible.

Verified fact $$S(12) = 30$$ is recorded in OEIS A036604 and was settled by exhaustive search. Derivation The gap is 0 for $$n \le 11$$ and irregular afterwards, which the page's strip recomputes entry by entry.

4. “So no algorithm can ever sort faster than n log n”

Wrong as stated. Counterexample: counting sort. It sorts six small integers with zero comparisons, and it is linear in $$n$$ plus the key range. The floor binds only methods that learn by comparing.

The repaired statement is the one to memorise: no comparison-based algorithm sorts in fewer than $$\lceil\log_2(n!)\rceil$$ comparisons in the worst case.

Derivation The page counts the comparisons a counting sort makes: zero, on a real array, with the output verified sorted.

5. “The floor bounds the best case too”

Wrong. Smallest counterexample: $$n = 3$$. The floor is 3 comparisons, and insertion sort sorts the already-sorted order $$a < b < c$$ in 2 comparisons. At $$n = 4$$ the floor is 5 and the best case is 3.

The floor is a statement about the worst case (and, with the ceiling removed, the average). A single lucky input can always be cheap; the tree only forces one deep path to exist.

Derivation The page checks $$n - 1 < \lceil\log_2(n!)\rceil$$ for every $$n$$ from 3 to 12, and simulates the best case of insertion sort at $$n = 4$$: three comparisons.

6. “n log n means exactly $$n\log_2 n$$ comparisons”

Wrong. Counterexample: $$n = 8$$. $$n\log_2 n = 24$$, the floor is 16, and merge sort spends 17. Three different numbers, all of them described by the same $$\Theta(n\log n)$$.

The exact floor is $$n\log_2 n - 1.4427n + \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi n)$$; at $$n = 8$$ that is about 15.3, and its ceiling is 16. The leading term alone overshoots by roughly $$1.44n$$, which is why the exact count is always a little below $$n\log_2 n$$.

Derivation Stirling is compared with the exact logarithm at $$n = 8, 16, 32, 64$$, and the difference is checked to stay small.

Pattern A — GATE CSE 1991, Q.1(vii): the exact count for a small n

The paper asks, as a fill in the blank worth two marks: “The minimum number of comparisons required to sort 5 elements is ______.”

The answer recorded in the archives is 7, and it is the ceiling of $$\log_2 120 = 6.907$$. Here the floor and the minimum coincide, because $$n = 5$$ is one of the settled values: the page's exhaustive minimax over comparison strategies confirms that no strategy sorts five items in six comparisons, and merge insertion does it in seven.

The reasoning to write down is two lines: five items have $$5! = 120$$ possible orders; a comparison has two outcomes, so $$k$$ comparisons separate at most $$2^k$$ orders; $$2^6 = 64 < 120 \le 128 = 2^7$$, so seven.

Verified fact Wording and key (7) agree across three independent archives of the 1991 paper. The official paper was not opened this run. Derivation The verdict is recomputed here: the exact minimum for five items is found by search, not copied from the key.

Pattern B — GATE CSE 2004: the tightest lower bound, asymptotically

The item asks: “The tightest lower bound on the number of comparisons, in the worst case, for comparison-based sorting is of the order of” with options $$n$$, $$n^2$$, $$n\log n$$ and $$n\log^2 n$$. The key is $$n\log n$$.

The reason is that the floor is $$\Theta(n\log n)$$, not merely $$\Omega(n\log n)$$: Stirling gives the leading term $$n\log_2 n$$, and merge sort matches it, so no smaller order is tight. $$n\log^2 n$$ is an order too large to be “tightest”.

Verified fact Year and wording agree across two independent archives; they disagree on the question number (29 in one, 35 in the other), so only the year is quoted here. Derivation The page checks that $$n\log_2 n$$ stays within a constant factor of the exact floor from $$n = 8$$ to $$4096$$, and that merge sort stays within a constant factor above it up to $$n = 64$$.

Pattern C — GATE CSE 1990, Q.3(v): the same fact, asked as a multiple select

The item asks for the complexity of comparison-based sorting algorithms, with options $$\Theta(n\log n)$$, $$\Theta(n)$$, $$\Theta(n^2)$$ and $$\Theta(n\sqrt n)$$. The key is $$\Theta(n\log n)$$.

The reasoning is the same two halves: a floor of $$\Omega(n\log n)$$ from the decision tree, and an algorithm — merge sort or heapsort — achieving $$O(n\log n)$$. Theta needs both, and the paper is asking for both.

Note the trap in the wording: $$\Theta$$ of the problem, not of a named algorithm. A question about quicksort alone could legitimately answer $$\Theta(n^2)$$.

Verified fact Wording and key agree across two independent archives of the 1990 paper. Educational interpretation The distinction between a bound on the problem and a bound on one algorithm is the single most common source of lost marks in this topic.

“minimum number of comparisons”
the ceiling, and check the value is a settled one
“lower bound on comparisons”
the ceiling, stated as $$\Omega(n\log n)$$ if asked asymptotically
“tightest lower bound, order of”
$$n\log n$$, because merge sort matches it

NAT original

Q1 — six items

The minimum number of comparisons required to sort 6 distinct elements in the worst case is ______.

Answer: 10

$$6! = 720$$, and $$2^9 = 512 < 720 \le 1024 = 2^{10}$$, so the ceiling is 10. It is also achievable: the page's exhaustive search returns 10, and merge insertion spends 10.

The trap is 9 (rounding down) or 12 (from $$n\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$$). The floor at six items is 10, and this is one of the settled values.

MCQ original

Q2 — why the leaves

A correct comparison sort of $$n$$ distinct items must have at least $$n!$$ leaves in its decision tree because:

(a) each comparison creates two leaves   (b) different input orders must produce different outputs, and a leaf carries one output   (c) the tree must be balanced   (d) there are $$n!$$ internal nodes

Answer: (b)

The sorted output determines the input order for distinct items, so two orders sharing a leaf would get the same printed answer, and one would be wrong.

(a) is false: comparisons are internal nodes, not leaves. (c) is false: the bound does not require balance, it only holds more strongly when the tree is unbalanced. (d) counts the wrong thing: the internal nodes are comparisons, and there are far fewer of them than $$n!$$ in any useful tree.

NAT original

Q3 — twelve items

The value of $$\lceil \log_2(12!) \rceil$$ is ______.

Answer: 29

$$12! = 479{,}001{,}600$$, and $$2^{28} = 268{,}435{,}456 < 479{,}001{,}600 \le 536{,}870{,}912 = 2^{29}$$. So the ceiling is 29.

Careful with the next step: the minimum for twelve items is 30, not 29. This question asks for the bound, and the bound is 29.

MSQ original

Q4 — which statements are true

(a) a binary tree of height $$h$$ has at most $$2^h$$ leaves   (b) the worst case of a comparison sort equals the height of its decision tree   (c) every comparison sort makes at least $$\lceil\log_2(n!)\rceil$$ comparisons on every input   (d) merge sort attains the floor for every $$n$$

Answer: (a), (b)

(a) is the doubling count, checked on every tree shape up to height 6. (b) is the definition of height as the longest root-to-leaf path.

(c) fails on the best case: insertion sort sorts eight sorted items in 7 comparisons, below 16. (d) fails at $$n = 8$$, where merge sort spends 17 against a floor of 16, and at $$n = 3$$, where it spends 3 against a floor of 3 — met there, missed here, so “every $$n$$” is false.

MCQ original

Q5 — the escape route

Which statement is true?

(a) the $$\Omega(n\log n)$$ lower bound applies to every sorting algorithm   (b) counting sort is faster than the bound allows, so the bound is wrong   (c) the bound applies only to algorithms that learn by comparing pairs   (d) radix sort also needs $$\Omega(n\log n)$$ comparisons

Answer: (c)

The bound is derived from a decision tree of comparisons. Counting sort and radix sort have no such tree, so the counting argument says nothing about them.

(a) and (d) both overreach the model: radix sort makes zero comparisons. (b) misreads a conditional theorem as a universal one — the bound is not wrong, it is scoped.

NAT original

Q6 — the slack

For $$n = 7$$, how many leaves does a decision tree of height $$\lceil\log_2(7!)\rceil$$ have left unused? ______

Answer: 3152

$$7! = 5040$$ and $$2^{12} = 4096 < 5040 \le 8192 = 2^{13}$$, so the height is 13 and there are $$8192 - 5040 = 3152$$ unused slots.

The trap is stopping at $$2^{12}$$: that power is too small, so subtracting it gives a meaningless 944. Work the two inequalities first, find the power at the ceiling, and only then subtract the factorial.

MSQ original

Q7 — averages and best cases

(a) some comparison sort averages fewer than $$\log_2(n!)$$ comparisons   (b) insertion sort averages 4.917 comparisons on four items   (c) the floor bounds the best case from below   (d) any binary tree with 24 leaves has average depth at least 4.585

Answer: (b), (d)

(b) is measured on the page over all 24 orders. (d) is the average-depth theorem applied to $$L = 24$$.

(a) contradicts (d), since $$n!$$ leaves force an average of at least $$\log_2(n!)$$. (c) is the trap from scene ten: the best case of insertion sort on four items is 3, well under the floor of 5.

MCQ original

Q8 — the closest pair of ideas

A paper asks: “What is the minimum number of comparisons needed to sort 12 elements?” Which is the correct answer and why?

(a) 29, the ceiling   (b) 30, the proven minimum   (c) 66, from $$n\log_2 n$$   (d) 29 or 30, undetermined

Answer: (b)

The ceiling is 29, but 29 comparisons are provably insufficient; 30 is the settled minimum, recorded in OEIS A036604. The wording “minimum” asks for the achievable value, not the bound.

This is the closest confusion in the whole topic, and it is why the lesson keeps the two words apart: lower bound gives 29, minimum gives 30. Option (d) would be right for $$n = 23$$ and beyond, where the exact value is unknown — but at $$n = 12$$ it is known.

The argument in four lines

Model
each comparison is an internal node; each run is a root-to-leaf path
Leaves
at least $$n!$$, one per input order
Capacity
a tree of height $$h$$ has at most $$2^h$$ leaves
Conclusion
$$h \ge \lceil \log_2(n!)\rceil = \Theta(n\log n)$$

Four lines, and every question in the topic is one of them being asked about.

Formulas, and when each one applies

exact floor
$$\lceil \log_2(n!)\rceil$$ — small $$n$$, integer answer
asymptotic
$$\Theta(n\log n)$$ — the order, when the question says “order of”
Stirling
$$n\log_2 n - 1.4427n + \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi n)$$
average floor
$$\log_2(n!)$$ — no ceiling, since it is not a count of comparisons
merge insertion
$$\sum_{k\le n}\lceil\log_2 \tfrac{3k}{4}\rceil$$ — the upper bound
merge sort worst
$$n\lceil\log_2 n\rceil - 2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil} + 1$$

The first two are what GATE asks. The rest are what makes the first two defensible.

Four traps, one line each

  • Rounding down: 6 instead of 7 at $$n = 5$$. The height is an integer, so take the ceiling.
  • Base three: 5 instead of 7 at $$n = 5$$. The third outcome cannot happen for distinct keys.
  • Floor as minimum: 29 instead of 30 at $$n = 12$$. Read the question's wording.
  • Floor as best case: the floor says nothing about a lucky input; insertion sort beats it on sorted data.

Educational interpretation These four cover almost every wrong option the archives show being chosen on this topic.

The five-step procedure

  1. What is counted? Worst case, average, or one named algorithm on one input.
  2. Write $$2^h \ge n!$$ and find the smallest power of two that covers $$n!$$.
  3. Floor or minimum? They agree to $$n = 11$$ and differ from $$n = 12$$.
  4. Name the model. Comparisons only, so non-comparison sorts are outside.
  5. Check the base. Two, because the third outcome is unavailable.

Sixty seconds, and it answers every variant the archives contain.

If you remember one thing

A sorting algorithm is a binary tree of comparisons, and a tree of height $$h$$ cannot have more than $$2^h$$ leaves. Since correctness demands $$n!$$ leaves, the height cannot be smaller than $$\lceil\log_2(n!)\rceil$$ — and that single sentence is the floor, the $$\Theta(n\log n)$$, and the reason counting sort is allowed to ignore both.

Everything else in the lesson is the arithmetic of that sentence, including the one honest correction: the floor is achievable up to $$n = 11$$ and not at $$n = 12$$.
