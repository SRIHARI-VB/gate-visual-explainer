Topic and objective

TOPIC: Relations and their properties — a relation as a set of ordered pairs, its matrix and arrow pictures, the six properties (reflexive, irreflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, asymmetric, transitive), closure, equivalence relations and partitions, composition and inverse, and the counting formulas that follow from the matrix.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a relation described by a rule, a pair list, a matrix or a digraph, decide each of the six properties by a test you can defend rather than by recall; find the smallest counterexample when a property fails; compute the reflexive, symmetric and transitive closures; turn an equivalence relation into its classes and back; and count relations of a stated property on an n-element set from the free-cell argument.

I read "relations and their properties" as the syllabus item "Sets, relations, functions, partial orders and lattices" cut at relations — properties, closure, equivalence relations and the counting that goes with them. Partial orders, Hasse diagrams and lattices are the next lesson and are only previewed here. It uses Sets and Set Operations for the Cartesian product and subsets, and First-Order Logic for reading a definition as a quantified statement.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus.

Section 1 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus (IIT Madras) contains the sentence: "Discrete Mathematics: Propositional and first order logic. Sets, relations, functions, partial orders and lattices. Monoids, Groups. Graphs: connectivity, matching, colouring. Combinatorics: counting, recurrence relations, generating functions."

Relations are named directly, and they are also the object that equivalence relations (used in counting by orbits and in modular arithmetic) and partial orders (used in scheduling and lattices) are built from.

Prerequisites

  • Sets and set operations: subset, the Cartesian product A × A, and why |A × A| = n². A relation is a subset of that product, so this is the whole vocabulary.
  • First-order logic: reading "for every a, the pair (a, a) is in R" as a universal statement, and knowing that a universal claim dies at one counterexample.
  • Multiplying independent choices (the product rule) — needed for every counting formula here.
  • Nothing about partial orders, lattices, groups or graph algorithms is assumed.

The five ideas

  1. A relation is a set of pairs, so counting relations is counting subsets of the n² cells.
  2. The matrix is the whole relation: the diagonal carries reflexivity, the mirror pairs carry symmetry and antisymmetry.
  3. Antisymmetric is not the negation of symmetric; a relation can have both, neither, or exactly one.
  4. Closure is the smallest repair of one named property — never "the" repair in general.
  5. Equivalence relations and partitions are the same thing counted twice.

Intuition first: the seating chart

A school has a rule about who may sit next to whom. The rule is recorded as a list of allowed pairs: Asha may sit beside Bala, Bala may sit beside Chandan, and so on. The list says nothing about Asha and Chandan unless they appear together in it. That list is a relation: a set of ordered pairs drawn from the set of students.

Educational interpretation A symmetric rule is a friendship: if Asha may sit beside Bala, then Bala may sit beside Asha. An antisymmetric rule is seniority: if the senior may sit beside the junior, the junior may not claim the same privilege — unless they are the same person. A transitive rule says a chain transfers: Asha with Bala and Bala with Chandan forces Asha with Chandan. An equivalence rule is a house colour: everyone has one, colours are mutual, and a chain of "same house" stays in one house — which is why the houses partition the school.

Definition 1 — binary relation

A binary relation R on a set A is any subset of A × A. We write a R b when (a, b) ∈ R.

Derivation |A × A| = n² when |A| = n, and a set with n² elements has 2n² subsets, so there are exactly 2n² relations on A. For n = 3 that is 512, enumerated on this page.

Definition 2 — domain, range, inverse

The domain of R is the set of first coordinates that occur; the range is the set of second coordinates. The inverse R−1 holds (b, a) exactly when R holds (a, b).

Derivation The matrix of R−1 is the transpose of the matrix of R, so R is symmetric exactly when R = R−1. Checked here on all 512 relations of a three-element set.

Definition 3 — reflexive, irreflexive

Reflexive: for every a ∈ A, (a, a) ∈ R. Irreflexive: for every a ∈ A, (a, a) ∉ R.

Exam trap These are not negations of each other. The negation of "all diagonal cells are 1" is "at least one diagonal cell is 0". A relation with (1,1) and (2,2) but no (3,3) is neither reflexive nor irreflexive.

Definition 4 — symmetric, antisymmetric, asymmetric

Symmetric: (a, b) ∈ R implies (b, a) ∈ R. Antisymmetric: (a, b) ∈ R and (b, a) ∈ R together imply a = b. Asymmetric: (a, b) ∈ R implies (b, a) ∉ R.

Derivation Asymmetric = irreflexive + antisymmetric, because asymmetry forbids self-loops as well as two-way pairs. Every asymmetric relation is antisymmetric; the converse fails at the identity relation.

Definition 5 — transitive

(a, b) ∈ R and (b, c) ∈ R together imply (a, c) ∈ R, for all a, b, c.

Derivation In matrix terms: if row a has a 1 in column b and row b has a 1 in column c, then row a must have a 1 in column c. In arrow terms: every two-step path needs a one-step shortcut.

Definition 6 — the three closures

The reflexive closure is R ∪ {(a, a) : a ∈ A}. The symmetric closure is R ∪ R−1. The transitive closure R+ is the set of pairs (a, c) for which some path of length at least one runs from a to c.

Derivation Each is the smallest relation containing R with its named property, and each is unique: the intersection of all relations with the property that contain R also has the property and contains R. Verified for transitivity by listing every transitive superset of every relation on a three-element set.

Definition 7 — composition

S ∘ R holds (a, c) when some b has (a, b) ∈ R and (b, c) ∈ S. Read right to left: apply R first.

Derivation Composition is associative, and (S ∘ R)−1 = R−1 ∘ S−1 — the order reverses, exactly as with inverse functions. Both identities were checked on all pairs of relations here.

Definition 8 — equivalence relation

R is an equivalence relation when it is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The class of a is [a] = { b : (a, b) ∈ R }.

Derivation The classes are non-empty, pairwise disjoint and cover A, so they form a partition. Conversely every partition comes from exactly one equivalence relation. Verified by enumerating all 512 relations and all 5 partitions of a three-element set.

The one line worth memorising

Draw the matrix: the diagonal answers reflexivity, the mirror pairs answer symmetry and antisymmetry, and a two-step path answers transitivity. Every definition in this topic is a sentence about one of those three places.

Worked example 1 — one relation, all six verdicts

On A = {1, 2, 3} let R = {(1,1), (1,2), (2,3)}. Decide each property, and say which cell or pair decides it.

Step 1 — draw the matrix before anything else

1 2 3
1 [ 1 1 0 ]
2 [ 0 0 1 ]
3 [ 0 0 0 ]

Derivation Row a, column b is 1 exactly when (a, b) ∈ R. Three cells are on the diagonal: (1,1) = 1, (2,2) = 0, (3,3) = 0. Three mirror pairs: {1,2} holds only (1,2); {1,3} holds neither; {2,3} holds only (2,3).

Step 2 — the verdicts, each with its witness

  • Not reflexive. (2,2) is missing.
  • Not irreflexive. (1,1) is present.
  • Not symmetric. (2,3) is present and (3,2) is not.
  • Antisymmetric — yes. No two different elements point at each other.
  • Not asymmetric. (1,1) is a self-loop.
  • Not transitive. (1,2) and (2,3) are present, (1,3) is not.

Step 3 — read the trap in verdict four

This relation is antisymmetric and not symmetric. That is a coincidence of this example, not a law: the identity relation on the same set is both.

Exam trap Answering "antisymmetric" because "not symmetric" was checked. The two properties are independent; each needs its own test.

Step 4 — repair it three ways

Add (2,2) and (3,3) for the reflexive closure. Add (2,1) and (3,2) for the symmetric closure. Add (1,3) — and then (1,3) with (2,3) already there forces nothing new — for the transitive closure. Each repair is minimal for its own property and changes the others, which is exactly why a question must name the property.

Worked example 2 — an equivalence relation, both directions

On A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} let a R b mean a and b have the same parity. List the classes, check the partition laws, then go backwards from a partition to its relation.

Step 1 — the three properties

Reflexive: every number has its own parity. Symmetric: "same parity" reads the same either way. Transitive: if a and b agree and b and c agree, then a and c agree.

Derivation Parity is a function p(n) = n mod 2, and the relation is "p(a) = p(b)". Any relation defined by equality of a function's values is an equivalence relation, for the same three reasons.

Step 2 — the classes and the partition

[1] = [3] = [5] = {1, 3, 5}
[2] = [4] = [6] = {2, 4, 6}

Derivation Non-empty: each contains its own element. Disjoint: no number has two parities. Covering: every number has one. Two classes, and 2 + 4 = 6 elements accounted for.

Step 3 — backwards, from a partition

Take the partition {{1, 4}, {2}, {3, 5, 6}}. The relation it induces is the union of the blocks squared: {1,4} × {1,4}, then {2} × {2}, then {3,5,6} × {3,5,6}.

Derivation That gives 4 + 1 + 9 = 14 pairs. In general a block of size k contributes k² pairs, so the relation has Σ k² pairs, and it is reflexive because each k² includes the k diagonal cells.

Step 4 — the count on three elements

Partitions of {1,2,3}: one block; three ways to split 2 + 1; three singletons. That is 5. Enumerating all 512 relations and testing the three properties also gives 5.

Exam trap "Number of equivalence relations containing the pair (1,2)" is a different question from "number of equivalence relations". Containing (1,2) forces 1 and 2 into one block, which leaves only 2 of the 5.

Worked example 3 — closures on a chain, and the smallest counterexample

On A = {1, 2, 3, 4} let R = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,4)}. Compute R+, then examine the claim "symmetric and transitive implies reflexive".

Step 1 — the transitive closure of the chain

Two-step paths: 1→2→3 gives (1,3); 2→3→4 gives (2,4). Three-step path: 1→2→3→4 gives (1,4). Nothing else, so R+ = R ∪ {(1,3), (2,4), (1,4)}, six pairs.

Derivation This is reachability: (a, c) ∈ R+ exactly when a path of length ≥ 1 runs from a to c. Six of the sixteen possible pairs survive; the pairs (2,1), (3,2), (4,3) and the four diagonal cells never appear.

Step 2 — the reflexive closure is not free

The reflexive closure of R+ adds (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4), giving ten pairs. The transitive closure of the reflexive closure is the same ten pairs.

Derivation Note the order matters as a description, not as a result: adding diagonal cells cannot create a new two-step path whose shortcut is missing, because every diagonal pair is already a shortcut to itself.

Step 3 — the claim, and the one-cell refutation

Claim: a symmetric and transitive relation is reflexive. Argument offered: a R b gives b R a by symmetry, hence a R a by transitivity. That argument needs a b to exist.

Exam trap On A = {1, 2}, R = {(1,1)} is symmetric (its only pair is its own mirror), transitive (no two-step path exists), and not reflexive ((2,2) is missing). The gap in the argument is an element with no arrow at all.

Step 4 — what the counterexample teaches

A universal claim is refuted by one instance, and the smallest instance is the one to remember. Here it is a single self-loop on a two-element set. If the relation is symmetric, transitive and every element is related to something, then it is reflexive on those elements — which is the correct version of the claim, with its extra assumption stated.

Boundaries that change the answer

Every property in this topic is a universal statement, so the empty set, the empty relation and a single element are the places where intuition and definition part company. Each case below is computed on the Animation tab rather than asserted.

The empty relation on a non-empty set

∅ ⊆ A × A is a relation. It is irreflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, asymmetric and transitive. It is not reflexive when A is non-empty.

Derivation Symmetric and transitive both hold vacuously: no pair exists to start the implication. Reflexive fails at the first element, since (a, a) ∉ ∅.

The empty set as the base set

On A = ∅ there is exactly one relation, the empty one, and it is reflexive — because "for every a ∈ A" has no witness to fail.

Derivation 20² = 1 relation, 20−0 = 1 reflexive relation, and the transitive counts start at 1 for n = 0. Every formula on this page agrees at n = 0 only if you read the diagonal argument as "zero cells forced".

A one-element set

On A = {1} there are 2 relations: ∅ and {(1,1)}. The second is reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, transitive and an equivalence relation. The first is everything except reflexive.

Derivation Symmetry and antisymmetry cannot conflict on one element, since antisymmetry only forbids two-way pairs between different elements. This is the smallest case where both properties hold at once.

Reflexive is relative to a stated set

"Divides" is reflexive on the positive integers, and also reflexive on {1, 2, 3}. But the relation {(1,1), (1,2), (2,2)} is reflexive on {1, 2} and not reflexive on {1, 2, 3}.

Exam trap The set is part of the question. A relation lifted to a bigger set can lose reflexivity without gaining or losing a single pair.

Composition order

S ∘ R applies R first. With R = {(1,2)} and S = {(2,3)} on {1,2,3}, S ∘ R = {(1,3)} while R ∘ S = ∅.

Exam trap Convention varies between textbooks. GATE questions normally say which order they mean, or write the relation as a matrix product; check the wording before computing.

A property that is not inherited

A subset of a transitive relation need not be transitive: on {1,2,3}, {(1,2), (2,3), (1,3)} is transitive, and its subset {(1,2), (2,3)} is not. Symmetry and antisymmetry, by contrast, are inherited by subsets, because they are conditions that forbid pairs rather than require them. Derivation Removing pairs can only break a requirement, never a prohibition — which is why reflexive and transitive are not inherited and irreflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric and asymmetric are.

Five beliefs that cost marks

Each is stated the way a learner states it, with the smallest structure that refutes it and the rule to use instead. The counterexamples are computed on this page, so they can be checked rather than trusted.

1. "Antisymmetric means not symmetric"

Why it seems right: the words look like opposites, and most textbook examples of one are not examples of the other.

Why it is wrong: antisymmetry forbids two-way arrows between different elements. Symmetry requires two-way arrows wherever there is a one-way arrow. A relation with no off-diagonal arrows at all satisfies both.

Minimal counterexample: the identity relation on {1}, or on any set — symmetric and antisymmetric. The empty relation is also both.

Rule: test each property separately; "both" and "neither" are both possible. Derivation All 512 relations on three elements were classified here: 8 are both, and many are neither.

2. "Symmetric plus transitive gives reflexive"

Why it seems right: the argument a R b ⇒ b R a ⇒ a R a is valid, and it is easy to stop there.

Why it is wrong: the argument needs an element b related to a. An element with no arrow at all is never forced to relate to itself.

Minimal counterexample: on {1, 2}, R = {(1,1)}. Symmetric, transitive, not reflexive.

Rule: symmetric + transitive + every element related to something ⇒ reflexive. State the third condition when you use the claim. Verified fact A claim of exactly this form has been examined in GATE papers.

3. "Not reflexive means irreflexive"

Why it seems right: the prefix "ir-" suggests the negation, as it does in "irregular".

Why it is wrong: the negation of a universal statement is an existential one. Not reflexive means some element fails to relate to itself; irreflexive means every element does.

Minimal counterexample: on {1, 2}, R = {(1,1)}: neither reflexive (2 is missing) nor irreflexive (1 is present).

Rule: count the diagonal 1s. All of them: reflexive. None: irreflexive. Otherwise: neither.

4. "The closure is the closure"

Why it seems right: the phrase is used loosely in lectures, as though there were one repair per relation.

Why it is wrong: the reflexive, symmetric and transitive closures are three different relations, and each is minimal only for its own property.

Minimal counterexample: on {1,2,3} with R = {(1,2)}, the transitive closure is R itself, the symmetric closure is {(1,2), (2,1)}, and the reflexive closure adds three diagonal cells.

Rule: name the property before computing. The equivalence closure (reflexive + symmetric + transitive) is the composite of all three, and is the smallest equivalence relation containing R.

5. "Transitive is inherited, so a subset of a transitive relation is transitive"

Why it seems right: symmetry is inherited by subsets, so it is tempting to assume every property is.

Why it is wrong: transitivity requires pairs to exist; deleting pairs can delete a required shortcut while leaving the two-step path intact. On {1,2,3}, {(1,2), (2,3), (1,3)} is transitive and {(1,2), (2,3)} is not.

Rule: properties that only forbid — irreflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, asymmetric — pass to subsets. Properties that require — reflexive, transitive — do not. Community observation This distinction is a reported difficulty in learner discussions of relation properties; it was not measured on a GATE cohort.

What GATE actually asks about relations

Five patterns cover the relation questions in the papers: a property verdict on a relation defined by an algebraic rule, a counting question on one named property, a claim about two properties interacting, the size or contents of a closure or of the smallest equivalence relation containing a given set of pairs, and an equivalence-class or partition count. The first two are the most common.

Pattern 1 — verdict on a rule-defined relation

Asks: a relation on pairs, integers or strings is given by an equation or a condition, then "which of the following is true" about reflexive, symmetric, transitive.

Procedure: substitute x = y into the rule for reflexivity; swap the two variables for symmetry; chain two instances for transitivity, and try the smallest numbers that break the chain. Verified fact This is the form of GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q41, whose rule is p − s = q − r on pairs of positive integers.

Pattern 2 — count relations with one property

Asks: "the number of reflexive relations on a set of n elements", "the number of symmetric relations", or a numerical version with n given.

Procedure: draw the n × n matrix, mark the cells the property forces, and raise 2 to the number of free cells — or to the number of free decisions, when a mirror pair is one decision. Verified fact The count of all binary relations, 2n², is GATE CSE 1999 Q1.2, and the same question appears in the 1987 paper.

Pattern 3 — two properties interacting

Asks: "if R is symmetric and transitive, is it reflexive?", or which combinations are possible.

Procedure: hunt for the smallest counterexample rather than proving. A two-element set with one self-loop kills the popular claim; the identity relation kills "antisymmetric means not symmetric". Common wrong approach: accepting the plausible chain of implications without checking that every element appears in some pair.

Pattern 4 — closures and smallest containing relations

Asks: the number of pairs in the smallest equivalence relation containing a given set, or the contents of a transitive closure.

Procedure: build the reflexive, symmetric and transitive closures in that order, and note that the transitive closure of the result is reachability in the arrow diagram. Count pairs, not classes, when the question asks for pairs. Derivation Worked on a chain of four elements in Example 3: six pairs in the transitive closure, ten after the reflexive closure.

Pattern 5 — classes, partitions and the Bell numbers

Asks: the number of equivalence relations on a small set, the largest possible number of classes, or the classes of a relation given by a rule such as congruence.

Procedure: for n ≤ 4 list the partitions; the counts are 1, 2, 5, 15. For a rule, the classes are the sets on which the rule's invariant agrees, so compute the invariant for each element and group. Common wrong approach: counting ordered partitions or forgetting the one-block partition.

The three-step habit for any relation question

Write the base set and the relation in pair form for the smallest interesting case. Draw the matrix and mark the diagonal and the mirror pairs. Then answer the exact question asked — a verdict, a count of free cells, a closure, or a class list — and check it against one more example of a different size. The definitions are short; the marks are lost by testing the wrong thing.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 1999, Q1.2 1 mark

The number of binary relations on a set with n elements is:

(A) n²   (B) 2n   (C) 2n²   (D) None of the above

Answer: (C) 2n². A relation is a subset of A × A, and |A × A| = n². A set with n² elements has 2n² subsets, so there are that many relations.

Why the others are wrong. (A) is the number of pairs, not of relations. (B) is the number of subsets of A, that is the size of the power set. (D) would only be right if none of the others matched, and (C) does.

Exam trap Reading the question as "relations from A to B" changes the answer to 2|A|·|B|. Here the relation is on one set, so the product is A × A.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2015 Set 3, Q41 2 marks

Let R be a relation on the set of ordered pairs of positive integers such that ((p, q), (r, s)) ∈ R if and only if p − s = q − r. Which one of the following is true about R?

(A) Both reflexive and symmetric
(B) Reflexive but not symmetric
(C) Not reflexive but symmetric
(D) Neither reflexive nor symmetric

Answer: (C). Rewrite the rule as p + r = q + s: the sum of the first coordinates equals the sum of the second coordinates.

Reflexivity. Put (r, s) = (p, q). The rule demands p + p = q + q, that is p = q. So a pair relates to itself only when its two entries are equal, and (1,2) is a counterexample. Not reflexive.

Symmetry. If p + r = q + s then r + p = s + q by commutativity of addition, which is exactly the rule for ((r,s), (p,q)). Symmetric.

Why the others are wrong. (A) and (B) both claim reflexivity, which fails at (1,2). (D) claims symmetry fails, but the swap is a pure commutativity step.

Exam trap The elements of the base set are themselves pairs, so reflexivity must be tested at ((p,q), (p,q)) — not at (p,p). Reading the outer pair as an inner one is the intended error.

NAT Original GATE-style easy

The number of symmetric relations on a set of four elements is _______. (Enter an integer.)

Answer: 1024. For n = 4 the free decisions are the 4 diagonal cells and the C(4,2) = 6 unordered pairs, so 10 decisions: 210 = 1024.

Common wrong answer: 256. That is 28, which comes from treating each mirror pair as two free cells and then halving — the arithmetic of "half of 16 cells" instead of "one decision per mirror pair".

Derivation The exponent is n(n+1)/2 = 4·5/2 = 10. This page confirms 210 = 1024 by counting the symmetric relations among all 65 536 relations on a four-element set.

NAT Original GATE-style medium

The number of antisymmetric relations on the set {1, 2, 3} is _______.

Answer: 216. Three diagonal cells are free (2³ = 8), and each of the three unordered pairs {1,2}, {1,3}, {2,3} has three options — no arrow, one direction only, the other direction only (3³ = 27). The product is 216.

Common wrong answer: 64. That is 26, which treats each mirror pair as a single yes-or-no decision. There are three decisions per pair, not two, because the two directions are distinguishable.

Derivation Enumerating all 512 relations on {1,2,3} and testing antisymmetry gives exactly 216. The asymmetric count is the same with the diagonal forced off: 27.

MSQ Original GATE-style medium

On A = {1, 2, 3} let R = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,1), (2,3), (3,2)}. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

(A) R is reflexive
(B) R is symmetric
(C) R is transitive
(D) R is antisymmetric

Answer: (A) and (B). All three diagonal pairs are present, so R is reflexive. Every off-diagonal pair comes with its mirror — (1,2) with (2,1), (2,3) with (3,2) — so R is symmetric.

Why (C) is false. (1,2) and (2,3) are both present, so transitivity demands (1,3), which is absent. One missing shortcut is enough.

Why (D) is false. (1,2) and (2,1) are both present with 1 ≠ 2, which antisymmetry forbids. Exam trap This relation is nearly an equivalence relation; the single missing pair (1,3) — and its mirror — is what stops it. Partial satisfaction is the trap in every MSQ of this shape.

NAT Original GATE-style medium

The number of equivalence relations on {1, 2, 3} that contain the pair (1, 2) is _______.

Answer: 2. An equivalence relation is a partition, and containing (1,2) means 1 and 2 lie in the same block. The partitions with that property are {{1,2},{3}} and {{1,2,3}}.

Common wrong answer: 3. That is the number of partitions in which the block containing 1 has size 2, forgetting that the one-block partition also contains the pair.

Derivation Of the 5 equivalence relations on three elements, 2 contain (1,2). Enumerated on this page: the five partitions are 123, 12|3, 13|2, 1|23, 1|2|3, and exactly the first two merge 1 with 2.

NAT Original GATE-style medium-hard

On A = {1, 2, 3}, let R = {(1, 2)}. The number of pairs in the smallest equivalence relation on A containing R is _______.

Answer: 5. Add (1,1), (2,2), (3,3) for reflexivity, and (2,1) for symmetry. The result {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,1)} is already transitive, since the only two-step chains it contains are 1→2→1 and 2→1→2, whose shortcuts (1,1) and (2,2) are present.

Common wrong answers: 2 (R plus its mirror, forgetting reflexivity) and 9 (collapsing everything into one block, which is not the smallest).

Derivation The classes are {1,2} and {3}, so the relation is the union of the blocks squared: 2² + 1² = 5 pairs. This is the general rule from Example 2.

MCQ Original GATE-style hard

Which one of the following statements is TRUE for every relation R on a non-empty set A?

(A) If R is symmetric and transitive then R is reflexive
(B) If R is symmetric then R is not antisymmetric
(C) If R is asymmetric then R is antisymmetric
(D) If R is irreflexive and symmetric then R is transitive

Answer: (C). Asymmetric means (a,b) ∈ R implies (b,a) ∉ R. If both (a,b) and (b,a) were in R, asymmetry would be violated, so the hypothesis of antisymmetry can never be met with a ≠ b. Hence antisymmetric.

(A) fails at R = {(1,1)} on {1,2}. (B) fails at the identity relation, which is both. (D) fails at R = {(1,2), (2,1)} on {1,2}: irreflexive and symmetric, but (1,1) is missing, so not transitive.

Derivation Asymmetric = irreflexive + antisymmetric, so (C) is one half of an identity between properties. All four options were tested against the 512 relations on a three-element set on this page.

NAT Original GATE-style medium

On A = {1, 2, 3, 4}, let R = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,4)}. The number of pairs in the transitive closure R+ is _______.

Answer: 6. R+ = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,4), (1,3), (2,4), (1,4)}: the three given pairs plus the three shortcuts forced by paths of length 2 and 3.

Common wrong answer: 7. Adding only (1,3) and (2,4) and then stopping misses (1,4), which the three-step path 1→2→3→4 forces. Repeat until nothing new appears.

Derivation R+ is reachability by a path of length ≥ 1. On a four-element chain the reachable pairs number 3 + 2 + 1 = 6, which is C(4,2). The reflexive closure would add four more, giving 10.

MSQ Original GATE-style hard

Let R and S be relations on a set A. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

(A) (R−1)−1 = R
(B) (S ∘ R)−1 = R−1 ∘ S−1
(C) If R and S are transitive then S ∘ R is transitive
(D) If R and S are equivalence relations then R ∩ S is an equivalence relation

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Inverting twice restores the original relation. The inverse of a composite reverses the order, exactly as with inverse functions: (S ∘ R)−1 = R−1 ∘ S−1.

(D) is true because each of the three properties is preserved by intersection: both relations have every diagonal pair, both are symmetric, and if (a,b) and (b,c) lie in the intersection then (a,c) lies in each, hence in both.

(C) fails. On A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, let R = {(1,2), (3,4)} and S = {(2,3), (4,5)}. Each is transitive on its own — neither has a two-step path. But S ∘ R = {(1,3), (3,5)} contains the chain (1,3), (3,5) and lacks (1,5), because the only route 1 → 2 → 5 needs (2,5) ∈ S, which is absent. So the composite is not transitive.

Exam trap Transitivity is not preserved by composition. Union is the other trap: the union of two equivalence relations need not be transitive, while the intersection always is. Derivation (A), (B) and the intersection closure were checked on all pairs of relations of a three-element set on this page.

Revision sheet — relations

Definitions in one line each, then the counts, then the decision procedure, then the traps with the counterexample that defuses each. Everything here is stated for a relation R on a finite set A with |A| = n ≥ 1 unless said otherwise.

Definitions

  • relation: R ⊆ A × A; there are 2n² of them.
  • reflexive: (a,a) ∈ R for all a.
  • irreflexive: (a,a) ∉ R for all a.
  • symmetric: (a,b) ∈ R ⇒ (b,a) ∈ R.
  • antisymmetric: (a,b), (b,a) ∈ R ⇒ a = b.
  • asymmetric: (a,b) ∈ R ⇒ (b,a) ∉ R.
  • transitive: (a,b), (b,c) ∈ R ⇒ (a,c) ∈ R.
  • equivalence: reflexive + symmetric + transitive.
  • class: [a] = { b : (a,b) ∈ R }.
  • partition: non-empty blocks, pairwise disjoint, covering A.

Counts, and when they hold

  • all: 2n²  ·  reflexive: 2n²−n  ·  irreflexive: 2n²−n
  • symmetric: 2n(n+1)/2  ·  antisymmetric: 2n3n(n−1)/2
  • asymmetric: 3n(n−1)/2  ·  both symmetric and antisymmetric: 2n
  • reflexive and symmetric: 2n(n−1)/2
  • transitive: no formula — 1, 2, 13, 171, 3994 for n = 0…4
  • equivalence: Bell numbers — 1, 2, 5, 15 for n = 0…3
  • assumption: n ≥ 1, and the count is of relations on A; a relation lifted to a bigger set changes its verdict.

Decision procedure

1. Write A and R as a pair list, small. 2. Draw the matrix; mark the diagonal and the mirror pairs. 3. Diagonal all 1 → reflexive; all 0 → irreflexive; mixed → neither. 4. Every mirror pair agrees → symmetric; every mirror pair has at most one 1 → antisymmetric; both are independent tests. 5. For each pair of 1s in a row-and-column chain, check the crossing cell; one miss → not transitive. 6. For an equivalence question, list the classes and verify the three partition laws.

Traps, each with its counterexample

  • "not symmetric ⇒ antisymmetric": fails at the identity relation (both).
  • "symmetric + transitive ⇒ reflexive": fails at {(1,1)} on {1,2}.
  • "not reflexive ⇒ irreflexive": fails at {(1,1)} on {1,2}.
  • "transitive is inherited by subsets": fails at {(1,2),(2,3)} inside {(1,2),(2,3),(1,3)}.
  • "closure is unique without naming the property": three different closures for one R.
  • "composition preserves transitivity": fails; union of two equivalence relations need not be transitive either.

Six quick questions, answers below

1. How many relations on a 2-element set?   2. How many of them are reflexive?   3. Is {(1,2),(2,1)} on {1,2} transitive?   4. How many equivalence relations on 4 elements?   5. Is the empty relation symmetric?   6. What is the transitive closure of a symmetric relation — symmetric?

1. 2⁴ = 16   2. 2² = 4   3. No — (1,1) and (2,2) are missing   4. 15   5. Yes, vacuously   6. Yes: a path can be reversed, so every forced pair has a forced mirror
