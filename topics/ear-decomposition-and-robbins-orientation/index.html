Topic and objective

TOPIC: Ear decomposition and Robbins’ theorem — building a map that has no bridge out of one cycle by adding paths one at a time, and using that build to give every street a direction so that every station still reaches every other.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Define an ear and an ear decomposition and check a proposed decomposition against all four clauses. Run the construction on a small map and say why the walk always comes home. Compute the number of ears from the map alone, as streets minus stations plus one, without building any decomposition. State Robbins’ theorem in both directions, and build a strong orientation two ways — by orienting the ears, and by a depth-first search. And separate the two questions that look identical: how many streets must fail to split the map, and how many stations must fail.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class C — a useful extension beyond the syllabus, not a syllabus item. Section 1 of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus says “Graphs: connectivity, matching, colouring”, and this lesson is one more way of asking what connectivity means: here, whether a map can be made one-way and survive. Two honest qualifications. First, neither “ear decomposition” nor “Robbins” appears in the syllabus, so no question here is claimed to be drawn from a named clause. Second, no previous-year GATE item on this specific theorem was located in the papers and archives searched for this lesson, so every question on the Practice tab is labelled original practice and none is presented as a previous-year question. What GATE does ask, verifiably, is strong connectivity of a given directed graph, bridges, and the number of edges that must be added to remove them — and each of those is a piece of this lesson, linked below.

PREREQUISITES: a bridge defined by deletion, and its equivalence with “lies on no cycle” (Articulation points, bridges and blocks); 2-edge-connected components and the bridge tree (2-edge-connected components); depth-first search with discovery and low numbers (Graph traversals); and reachability in a directed graph (Strongly connected components). Nothing else is assumed: the counting identity is one subtraction.

VISUALIZATION PLAN: twelve scenes, one worked map of eight stations and eleven streets, drawn the same way throughout. First the question itself, with an orientation that fails and one that works. Then the single obstruction — a bridge — which settles the answer with a no. Then the ear, in its three shapes: a multi-street path, one street, and a closed path that is a cycle. Then the definition, clause by clause, with each clause shown on the map. Then the construction, ear by ear, until no street is left. Then the argument that makes it work, counting unused streets at an unbuilt station, and the same walk dying at a bridge. Then the count: ears equal streets minus stations plus one. Then the orientation, built ear by ear, and Robbins’ theorem. Then the search that builds it in one pass, against the tempting variant that fails. Then the bowtie, which is bridgeless and still has a cut station. Then the four boundary cases. Then the five-step procedure.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A graph is a set of vertices (here, stations) and a set of edges (here, streets), each edge joining two vertices.
  • A bridge is an edge whose deletion raises the number of connected pieces. Equivalently, an edge that lies on no cycle. Both definitions are used here, and the second one is the one that explains the proof.
  • A cycle is a closed walk that repeats no vertex except its start.
  • Reachability in a directed graph: a vertex u reaches v when there is a directed path from u to v.
  • Depth-first search, and that every edge of an undirected graph is either a tree edge or joins a vertex to one of its ancestors.

Helpful, not required: the circuit rank of a connected graph, edges minus vertices plus one, met in the spanning trees lesson as the number of edges outside a spanning tree.

Approximate learning time: 40–55 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. An ear is a path through new ground. Both ends sit in the part already built; every inside station is new. One street long, or a whole cycle, both count.
  2. Build the map from one cycle, then add ears. Take any unbuilt street at a built station and follow unbuilt streets until you come home. You always can, when there is no bridge.
  3. The number of ears is fixed: streets − stations + 1. Each ear adds one edge more than it adds stations, so it raises that number by exactly one.
  4. Orient ear one round and every later ear end to end, and the whole map becomes strongly one-way. That construction is the proof of Robbins’ theorem.
  5. Bridgeless is not 2-vertex-connected. The bowtie has no bridge and has a cut station; it still has a strong orientation, but no open ear decomposition.

Intuition: a city that wants one-way streets

A city council wants every street to be one-way, to save space and to stop drivers turning across traffic. The rule they must keep is simple: from any corner you must still be able to drive to any other corner. The question is which maps allow that at all.

Two things make the question interesting. First, it is a question about some choice of directions, not about one particular choice: a map that fails for one assignment of directions may succeed for another. Second, there is an obstruction that no cleverness removes: a street that is the only way across a river. Point it one way and everyone on the far bank is cut off; point it the other way and the near bank is cut off.

The surprise is that this single obstruction is the only one. If no street is the only way across anything — that is, if every street lies on a round trip — then a one-way system always exists. The proof does not search over all assignments of directions, which would be hopeless; it builds the map up in pieces and orients each piece as it goes.

Educational interpretation The city is an aid for the vocabulary only. Nothing about the mathematics depends on it, and the formal statement below uses graphs.

Formal definitions

Let G be an undirected graph with vertex set V and edge set E. Write |E| for the number of edges and |V| for the number of vertices.

an orientation of G = a choice, for every edge, of one of its two directions

Read: each edge {u, v} becomes exactly one arc, either u to v or v to u. Every edge gets a direction; none is left two-way.

the orientation is strong ⇔ for all u, v: u reaches v

Read: every vertex reaches every other vertex along the arrows. It is not enough that one vertex reaches all others — the condition is every ordered pair, both ways round.

e is a bridge ⇔ deleting e raises the piece count

Read: e is the only edge joining two parts of the graph. Equivalently, e lies on no cycle. A graph is bridgeless when no edge is a bridge.

an ear = a path whose two ends are in B and whose inside vertices are all outside B

Read: B is the part of the graph already built. The ends of the path may be the same vertex, in which case the ear is closed and is a cycle. The inside vertices must be new, and no edge may be reused.

an ear decomposition of G = E written as a list of ears P₀, P₁, …, Pₖ

Read: the list covers every edge exactly once; P₀ is a cycle; and for each later ear, both ends lie in the union of the ears before it, while its inside vertices do not.

The one fact every step uses

If a connected graph has no bridge and at least two vertices, then every vertex has degree at least two.

The reason is short. A vertex of degree one has a single edge leaving it, and deleting that edge separates it from everything else, so that edge is a bridge. A vertex of degree zero is not in a connected graph with two or more vertices. So degree one and degree zero are both impossible, and degree at least two is what is left.

Mathematical derivation This is the whole content of the “walk cannot stall” argument later: a station that is not yet built has every one of its streets unused, and it has at least two of them, so a walk that arrives can always leave again.

Note the assumption: connected and no bridge. Drop connectivity and a vertex can have degree zero.

What one ear does to the numbers

Suppose an ear has k edges. Its two ends are already built, so all of its vertices except the ends are new: that is k − 1 new vertices, because a path with k edges has k + 1 vertices and two of them are old.

So one ear changes the difference (edges − vertices) by k − (k − 1) = 1. The first ear is the exception only in appearance: a cycle with k edges has k vertices, all new, so it also changes the difference by 1.

number of ears = |E| − |V| + 1 (connected graph)

Read: this is the circuit rank. Since every ear raises the difference by one, and the difference starts at 0 on a single vertex and must end at |E| − |V| + 1, the number of ears is forced. It is a property of the graph, not of the order in which ears are chosen.

Mathematical derivation The model on the Animation tab re-computes this on every decomposition it builds, on all 107 bridgeless connected graphs on four and five vertices, and finds no exception.

Why the walk always comes home

The construction is: keep a built set B, start it as one cycle, and then repeatedly take a built station that still has an unused street and follow unused streets until the walk arrives back at B. The only thing to prove is that the walk arrives.

  1. Every edge already used has both ends in B. So no used edge touches a station outside B. Justification: an ear joins two built stations and passes only through stations it then adds to B.
  2. So a station outside B has all of its streets unused. Justification: by the previous step.
  3. And it has at least two streets, because the graph is connected and has no bridge. Justification: the degree fact in the previous card.
  4. So a walk that arrives at a station outside B has an unused street to leave by, other than the one it arrived on. Justification: at most one of its streets was used to arrive.
  5. The graph is finite and each step uses a new street, so the walk cannot run forever; since it can never stall outside B, it must arrive back at B. Justification: finite edge set plus no dead ends.

Mathematical derivation This is the whole existence proof. Note where bridgelessness is used: step 3, and again in step 4 through the fact that no used edge touches an unbuilt station.

Robbins’ theorem, both directions

Theorem (Robbins, 1939). An undirected graph has a strong orientation if and only if it is connected and has no bridge.

⇒ a bridge kills it

If e is a bridge, deleting it splits the graph into two parts A and B. Whichever way e is directed, no directed path can go from the far side back, because every route between the parts uses e. So not strongly connected.

⇐ no bridge is enough, by the ears

Take an ear decomposition. Send ear one round as a directed cycle; point each later ear from one end to the other. Induct on the number of ears: if the graph built so far is strongly connected, adding a directed path between two of its vertices keeps it so. Any new station on the ear reaches the built part (follow the ear forward) and is reached from it (follow it back).

Verified fact The statement and the ear-based proof are standard; the lesson’s Sources tab names the material consulted, including a university course note that gives exactly this two-line induction.

The word connected in the theorem is not decoration: a disconnected graph has no strong orientation whatever its edges look like.

The orientation a search builds

The ear argument proves existence but is not how you would compute an orientation. One depth-first search does it, in time proportional to the size of the graph.

  1. Run a depth-first search from any station, and give every station a discovery number.
  2. For each tree edge, point it from the parent to the child — away from the root.
  3. For each non-tree edge, point it from the deeper end to the shallower end — from the descendant back up to its ancestor.
  4. Every station reaches the root, by following the tree edges up; and the root reaches every station, by following them down.

Step 3 is why bridgelessness is needed: with a bridge, the far side of it has no way back up, and the root never reaches across.

Mathematical derivation Step 4 is the argument: descendant-to-root by tree edges, root-to-descendant by tree edges, and any two stations join through the root. The model checks the result is strong on all 107 bridgeless connected maps it enumerates.

The variant that looks the same and is not

A very natural misreading of step 3 is: “point every edge from the station with the smaller discovery number to the one with the larger”. It sounds like the same rule, since tree edges do point from smaller to larger.

It is not the same rule, and the result is never strong. Every arc then goes from a smaller number to a larger one, so the numbers strictly increase along any directed path; a path can never return to where it started.

triangle a, b, c: a→b, b→c, a→c

Read: nothing points back to a. The graph is acyclic and cannot be strongly connected, although the triangle is bridgeless and does have strong orientations (two of them: the two cyclic ones).

Mathematical derivation The model checks that no graph on four stations has a strong orientation of this kind, which is the same statement: an acyclic orientation can never be strong when there is at least one edge.

Worked example — the map of eight stations and eleven streets

The map has stations A to H and eleven streets. Every number below is computed by the page’s model, and the animation draws exactly this decomposition.

Ear 1: A → B → C → D → A. Four streets, a cycle, and the four stations on it are now built.

Ear 2: B → E → F → D. Three streets; both ends built, and E and F are new.

Ear 3: C → G → H → D. Three streets; ends C and D built, G and H new.

Ear 4: C → H. One street; both ends already built, so there is no inside station at all.

Four ears cover 4 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 11 streets, which is all of them, and no street appears twice.

|E| − |V| + 1 = 11 − 8 + 1 = 4

Read: the circuit rank is four, and the decomposition has four ears. The count was known before any ear was found.

Verified fact The page’s model builds this decomposition, checks the four clauses on it, checks the ear count against the circuit rank, and reports the result in the model-checks panel on the Animation tab.

The orientation built from those ears

Send ear 1 round: A→B→C→D→A. Point ear 2 from B to D: B→E→F→D. Point ear 3 from C to D: C→G→H→D. Point ear 4 from C to H: C→H.

Now check two stations at random. E reaches A by E→F→D→A, and A reaches E by A→B→E. G reaches B by G→H→D→A→B, and B reaches G by B→C→G.

The general reason: each ear is a directed path whose two ends are already in a strongly connected part, so a station on the ear reaches the part by following the ear forward, and is reached from the part by following it back.

Mathematical derivation This is the induction of the theorem, run on one map. The model checks the resulting orientation is strong.

The same map, oriented by a search instead

Start the depth-first search at A. The tree edges, pointed away from A, and the non-tree edges, pointed back up, give a strong orientation with no ears involved.

On this map the search visits A, then B, then C, then D, and so on; the streets that are not in the search tree are D→A, E→B, F→D… each pointed from the deeper station to the shallower one.

Two different orientations of the same map, both strong, both built by rules you can run. The theorem promises at least one; it does not promise that a rule gives the only one.

The model checks both constructions on this map and on every bridgeless connected map on four and five stations.

Example 2 — the bowtie, where the second ear closes

Five stations: a shared station v, and two triangles v-a-b-v and v-c-d-v. Six streets.

Ear 1: v → a → b → v, a cycle. Ear 2: v → c → d → v — both ends are v. The ear is closed, and the definition permits that.

|E| − |V| + 1 = 6 − 5 + 1 = 2

Read: two ears, and the construction produces exactly two. No open ear decomposition exists, because an open one needs two distinct ends every time.

The bowtie has no bridge: every street lies on one of the two triangles. It does have a strong orientation — send each triangle round — and the model finds 4 of its 64 orientations are strong.

Verified fact All four claims are computed: bridgeless, two ears with the second closed, no cut-free decomposition, and 4 strong orientations by brute force.

Example 3 — two triangles joined by one street

Six stations, seven streets: two triangles and a street between them. Every station has degree at least two, so the degree condition alone does not rule it out.

But that street is a bridge. Point it one way and the far triangle cannot be left; point it the other way and the near triangle cannot be reached. The model brute-forces all 128 orientations and finds none that is strong.

The ear construction fails for the same reason: ear 1 is one triangle, and then the walk cannot reach the other triangle at all, so the streets of the far triangle end up in no ear and the decomposition cannot be completed.

Mathematical derivation This is the counterexample to “every vertex of degree two is enough”. The condition that matters is about edges, not degrees.

Example 4 — the complete graph on four stations

Four stations, all six streets present. |E| − |V| + 1 = 6 − 4 + 1 = 3, so any ear decomposition has three ears, and the model finds three.

Of its 2⁶ = 64 orientations, the model finds 24 that are strong. The 24 are the orientations that make it a strongly connected tournament, which is the same as containing a directed cycle through all four stations.

Verified fact 24 is a computed count, by trying all 64 orientations and checking reachability from every station.

Example 5 — a tree, and why nothing starts

Take four stations in a path: 1–2–3–4. Three streets, four stations, so |E| − |V| + 1 = 0.

Zero ears. An ear decomposition needs a first ear, and the first ear must be a cycle; a tree has none. The construction has nothing to start from, and the count agrees: zero ears is exactly what a map with no cycle should have.

And no strong orientation: point 1–2 from 1 to 2 and station 2 can never return to station 1.

Mathematical derivation The rank being zero is the same statement as the map having no cycle, which is the same statement as every street being a bridge.

One station, no streets

A graph with a single vertex and no edges is connected and has no bridge, so by Robbins’ theorem it has a strong orientation: the empty one. The requirement “every station reaches every other” is vacuous, because there is no other.

This is a convention, and it is worth knowing that it is one. Some texts define strong connectivity only for graphs with at least two vertices. Either way, the theorem is unaffected on every other graph, and the page states which convention it uses.

Two stations, one street

The smallest graph where the theorem says no. It is connected, but its single edge is a bridge, and one direction is always missing.

The circuit rank is 1 − 2 + 1 = 0, so even if a decomposition were attempted it would need zero ears while still having an edge to cover — a contradiction, which is the count telling you the map has no cycle.

Two stations, two parallel streets

Now there is no bridge: deleting either street leaves the stations joined by the other. Point one each way and the pair is strongly connected.

The circuit rank is 2 − 2 + 1 = 1, so one ear, and that ear is closed: it starts at one station and returns to it along the second street. This is why the definition of an ear speaks of a walk that repeats no edge, not of a simple cycle of distinct vertices.

A self-loop

A loop is never a bridge — deleting it changes nothing about who is connected to whom — so a loop never blocks a strong orientation. It also never helps: a loop gives no route to anywhere new.

So a graph with a loop and a bridge still has no strong orientation. Counting loops as ears is consistent with the circuit rank, which counts a loop as one.

Disconnected, with no bridge anywhere

Two separate triangles. No edge is a bridge, and every station has degree two. Yet no orientation is strong, because no direction crosses a gap where there is no edge at all.

This is the case that shows the word connected in the theorem cannot be dropped. The circuit rank formula also needs the connected case; for c pieces it becomes |E| − |V| + c.

Closed ears are allowed; open ones are a stronger demand

In the definition used here, an ear’s two ends may coincide. If every ear after the first is required to have two distinct ends, the decomposition is called open.

Open decompositions exist exactly for the 2-vertex-connected graphs — Whitney, 1932 — which is strictly fewer graphs than the bridgeless ones. The bowtie has an ear decomposition and no open one.

The count is fixed even when the decomposition is not

Different choices of first cycle give different decompositions, with different ears. What never changes is how many ears there are, because the count is |E| − |V| + 1.

So a proposed decomposition with the wrong number of ears is wrong, and you can say so without drawing anything.

Graphs that are not simple

Parallel edges and loops are fine for everything in this lesson, provided the ear is defined as a walk that repeats no edge rather than a path that repeats no vertex. The two definitions agree on simple graphs.

Where they differ is exactly the parallel-edge case above, which is also the case in which a two-station graph becomes strongly orientable.

“Bridgeless means 2-vertex-connected”

Why it tempts: on small graphs the two conditions very often hold together. The model confirms it: on four stations, all ten connected bridgeless graphs are also 2-vertex-connected, so every example you draw first agrees with the belief.

The minimal counterexample: the bowtie — five stations, six streets, no bridge, and one cut station. The model finds 97 connected bridgeless graphs on five stations of which only 90 are 2-vertex-connected.

The correct rule: bridgeless is about edges (how many must be deleted to split the map); 2-vertex-connected is about stations. Bridgeless is the weaker condition, and the bowtie is where they part.

“Point every edge from the earlier-discovered end to the later”

Why it tempts: it is almost the right rule. Tree edges do point from the earlier station to the later one, so the first few arrows look correct.

The minimal counterexample: a triangle. The search numbers the stations a, b, c, and every edge points a→b, b→c, a→c. Nothing points back to a.

The correct rule: tree edges away from the root, and every other edge from the descendant back up to the ancestor. The two rules differ on exactly the non-tree edges, and those are the ones that provide the way back.

“An ear decomposition is a list of cycles”

Why it tempts: the first ear is a cycle, and the word “ear” suggests a loop.

The minimal counterexample: the worked map’s fourth ear, C→H, is a single street; its second ear, B→E→F→D, is a path that is not closed.

The correct rule: only the first ear must be a cycle, because nothing is built yet. Every later ear is a path whose two ends are already built; it may be one edge long, and it may close, but it need not.

“Every vertex of degree at least two is enough”

Why it tempts: the degree fact is used in the proof, so it feels like the hypothesis. And on small maps it nearly works.

The minimal counterexample: two triangles joined by one street. All six stations have degree two or three, and there is no strong orientation.

The correct rule: the hypothesis is about edges, not degrees: no edge may be the only way across. Minimum degree two does not imply bridgeless.

“Strong means one station can reach all the others”

Why it tempts: that is the condition you naturally check first, and it is the one the first scene of the animation shows failing.

The minimal counterexample: the discovery-order orientation of the triangle: a reaches b and c, but neither reaches a. One-way reachability holds, strong connectivity does not.

The correct rule: strong means every station reaches every other, for all ordered pairs. One station reaching all is not enough, and neither is all stations reaching one.

“If a strong orientation exists, this construction must give one”

Why it tempts: the construction is presented as the proof, so it looks like the unique recipe.

The counterexample: the worked map has 54 strong orientations out of 2048, and the ear construction produces one of them; the search rule produces another.

The correct rule: the theorem promises existence. Any construction that provably yields a strong orientation is a valid answer, and no construction is claimed to yield all of them.

Pattern 1 — is this directed graph strongly connected?

The syllabus-adjacent form of this lesson’s question, and the one GATE actually asks. Given a directed graph, decide whether every vertex reaches every other.

What it demands: a traversal from one vertex, then a traversal of the reversed graph from the same vertex. Both reach everything → strong. Equivalently, the condensation has one component.

The common wrong approach: a single traversal from one vertex. That proves one-way reachability, not strong connectivity — the misconception above, in exam form.

Pattern 2 — which edges are bridges?

The structural question underneath the theorem. Two standard methods: delete each edge and recount components, or run the low-number test and report edges with low[child] > disc[parent].

What it demands: the strict inequality. Equality means the child can still reach the parent’s level by another route, so the edge is on a cycle and is not a bridge.

The common wrong approach: comparing low[child] ≥ disc[parent] and calling the equality case a bridge, which over-reports.

Pattern 3 — count the ears, or the independent cycles

Ask for the number of edges outside a spanning tree, the number of independent cycles, or the number of ears: all three are |E| − |V| + 1 for a connected graph.

What it demands: knowing that the number is forced, so no construction is needed.

The common wrong approach: counting cycles of the drawing rather than the rank. A map can have many cycles and a small rank.

Pattern 4 — how many edges must be added to remove every bridge?

The augmentation question, answered in the 2-edge-connected components lesson: contract the components, and the answer is half the number of leaves of the resulting forest, rounded up.

What it demands: the bridge forest, and the leaf count.

Why it belongs here: the graph you obtain when the answer is zero is exactly the one this lesson is about — connected and bridgeless.

Pattern 5 — true or false about a hypothesis

A statement such as “every connected graph in which every vertex has even degree has a strong orientation”, or “every bridgeless graph is 2-vertex-connected”. The work is finding the smallest counterexample.

What it demands: a stock of small maps. Triangle, bowtie, two triangles joined by a bridge, K4, a tree, two stations with two streets.

The common wrong approach: checking three or four examples that all happen to agree, and concluding the statement is true.

The reasoning this topic trains

Three habits transfer to unfamiliar questions. First, when a claim says “there exists a choice”, look for the obstruction that no choice can remove. Second, when a construction has to terminate, count what each step consumes and show a step is always available. Third, when two conditions look alike, find the smallest map where they differ, because that map is usually the whole answer.

MCQ Original practice medium

A connected graph has 9 vertices and 13 edges. How many ears does any ear decomposition of it have?

  • (a) 4
  • (b) 5
  • (c) 13
  • (d) it depends on the decomposition

Answer: (b) 5. |E| − |V| + 1 = 13 − 9 + 1 = 5, and each ear raises that quantity by exactly one.

Why the others are wrong: (a) is the number of edges minus the number of vertices, forgetting the +1. (c) is the number of edges, which would be the ear count only if every ear were a single edge. (d) is the misconception: the decomposition varies, its ear count does not.

Edge case: if the graph were disconnected the formula would be |E| − |V| + c, and there would be no single ear decomposition at all.

MCQ Original practice hard

What is the smallest number of vertices in a connected graph that has no bridge but is not 2-vertex-connected?

  • (a) 3
  • (b) 4
  • (c) 5
  • (d) 6

Answer: (c) 5. The bowtie: two triangles sharing one vertex, five vertices and six edges. No edge is a bridge, and the shared vertex is a cut vertex.

Why the others are wrong: with 3 vertices the only bridgeless connected graph is the triangle, which is 2-vertex-connected. With 4 vertices the model checks all 64 graphs and finds all ten bridgeless connected ones are 2-vertex-connected — (b) is the answer that tempts anyone who stops at four.

Why this matters: it is the exact map that separates the two hypotheses, so it is the counterexample to keep.

MSQ Original practice medium

Which of the following are true for every undirected graph G?

  • (a) If G is connected and bridgeless, then G has a strong orientation.
  • (b) If G has a strong orientation, then G is bridgeless.
  • (c) If G is bridgeless, then G is 2-vertex-connected.
  • (d) If G is 2-vertex-connected, then G is bridgeless.

Answer: (a), (b), (d).

(a) is the sufficiency half of Robbins’ theorem. (b) is the necessity half: a bridge can only be crossed one way, so one side is stranded. (d) holds because a bridge’s endpoints are cut vertices in any graph with at least three vertices, so a graph with a bridge is not 2-vertex-connected.

Why (c) is wrong: the bowtie is bridgeless and has a cut vertex. This is the closely confused pair the whole Misconceptions tab is about.

Trap: (a) needs both hypotheses. “Connected” alone fails on a tree; “bridgeless” alone fails on two disjoint triangles.

NAT Original practice hard

How many of the 64 orientations of the complete graph on 4 vertices are strongly connected?

Answer: 24. The model tries all 64 and counts 24. An orientation of K4 is strong exactly when it contains a directed Hamiltonian cycle; the number of strong tournaments on 4 labelled vertices is 24.

How to get it without brute force: count directed Hamiltonian cycles up to rotation — 3! / 4 … the systematic count is longer than the brute force, which is why the page computes it.

Trap: the answer is not 64/2 or any symmetry argument of that shape; the strong orientations are a proper subset and the count must be computed or derived, not guessed.

MCQ Original practice medium

In an ear decomposition of a connected bridgeless graph, which statement about the first ear is correct?

  • (a) It must be a cycle, because nothing is built before it.
  • (b) It must be a single edge.
  • (c) It may be any path, closed or open.
  • (d) It must contain every vertex of the graph.

Answer: (a). Both ends of an ear must be in the part already built. For the first ear that part is empty, so the two ends must be the same vertex — which makes it closed, hence a cycle.

Why the others are wrong: (b) a single edge with both ends at one vertex is a loop, which is not required. (c) an open path would need its ends in an empty set. (d) nothing requires the first cycle to be spanning; on the worked map it touches four of the eight stations.

MCQ Original practice hard

A graph is connected, every vertex has degree at least 2, and it has no strong orientation. Which of these is a possible graph?

  • (a) a cycle
  • (b) two triangles joined by one edge
  • (c) the complete graph on 4 vertices
  • (d) none: those hypotheses force a strong orientation

Answer: (b). It is connected and every vertex has degree 2 or 3, but the joining edge is a bridge, so no orientation is strong.

Why the others are wrong: a cycle and K4 are both bridgeless, so both have strong orientations by Robbins. (d) is the misconception — degree at least 2 is not the hypothesis of the theorem.

The distinction to keep: minimum degree is a local condition; bridgelessness is a condition about every cut.

MCQ Original practice medium

A depth-first search of a bridgeless connected graph orients each tree edge away from the root. How must it orient the remaining edges to obtain a strong orientation?

  • (a) from the earlier-discovered end to the later-discovered end
  • (b) from the descendant to the ancestor
  • (c) from the ancestor to the descendant
  • (d) in any order: the tree edges already make it strong

Answer: (b). Non-tree edges must point back up, so every vertex can climb to the root and the root can descend to every vertex.

Why the others are wrong: (a) is the tempting variant; it makes every arc go from a smaller discovery number to a larger one, so the result is acyclic and never strong. (c) would double the downward flow and leave no way back. (d) is false: the tree alone is acyclic.

Check it: on the worked map, the rule gives a strong orientation; the model verifies this on all 107 bridgeless connected maps on four and five stations.

MSQ Original practice hard

The bowtie is two triangles sharing one vertex. Which statements are true of it?

  • (a) It has no bridge.
  • (b) It has an ear decomposition with two ears.
  • (c) It has no open ear decomposition.
  • (d) It has no strong orientation.

Answer: (a), (b), (c). Every edge lies on one of the two triangles, so no edge is a bridge. The model builds a two-ear decomposition whose second ear starts and ends at the shared vertex. An open decomposition needs two distinct ends at every ear, which is exactly what the shared vertex prevents — and by Whitney’s theorem, open decompositions exist only for 2-vertex-connected graphs.

Why (d) is wrong: the bowtie is bridgeless and connected, so Robbins gives a strong orientation; sending each triangle round works, and the model counts 4 of 64.

The point: closed ears are the loophole that lets a graph with a cut vertex have a decomposition at all.

NAT Original practice easy

A connected graph has 20 vertices and 31 edges, and every edge lies on a cycle. How many ears does an ear decomposition of it have?

Answer: 12. 31 − 20 + 1 = 12. The clause “every edge lies on a cycle” is there to tell you a decomposition exists at all — it is the same as saying the graph is bridgeless.

Without that clause the count would still be 12 if a decomposition existed, but it might not exist. The count is a property of the numbers; existence is a property of the structure.

Definitions in one line each

  • orientation: one direction chosen for every edge.
  • strong: every vertex reaches every other, both ways round.
  • bridge: an edge whose deletion raises the piece count; an edge on no cycle.
  • bridgeless: no edge is a bridge.
  • ear: a path with both ends built and every inside vertex new; may be closed.
  • ear decomposition: all edges as a list of ears, first one a cycle.
  • open: every ear after the first has two distinct ends.
  • circuit rank: |E| − |V| + 1 for a connected graph.

The three theorems, with years

  • Robbins, 1939. A graph has a strong orientation iff it is connected and bridgeless.
  • Whitney, 1932. A graph with at least two edges is 2-vertex-connected iff it has an open ear decomposition.
  • Robbins, 1939 (second form). A graph is 2-edge-connected iff it has an ear decomposition.

The number of ears in either kind of decomposition is the circuit rank.

The procedure, and what each step settles

  1. Connected? No → no strong orientation.
  2. Find a bridge, by deletion or by low[child] > disc[parent].
  3. A bridge → no strong orientation; the cut is the reason.
  4. No bridge → build one: tree edges away from the root, other edges back up.
  5. Check it: traverse from every vertex, or from one vertex and from one vertex in the reversed graph.

Five sentences to remember

  • An ear is a path through new ground with both ends on old ground.
  • One cycle first, then ears, until no edge is left.
  • Ears = streets − stations + 1, always.
  • Orient ear one round and the rest end to end, and it is strong.
  • Bridgeless is about edges; 2-vertex-connected is about stations; the bowtie is where they part.

Four traps

  • Orienting every edge from the earlier discovery number to the later is not the search rule; it is acyclic.
  • Minimum degree two does not imply bridgeless.
  • The ear count is fixed; a decomposition with a different count is wrong.
  • One vertex reaching all others is not strong connectivity.

Small maps worth memorising

  • Triangle: bridgeless, 2-vertex-connected, rank 1, 2 strong orientations of 8.
  • Bowtie: bridgeless, not 2-vertex-connected, rank 2, 4 of 64.
  • Two triangles plus a bridge: rank 2, no strong orientation.
  • K4: rank 3, 24 of 64 strong.
  • Two stations, two streets: rank 1, one closed ear, strong.
  • A tree: rank 0, no ears, no strong orientation.
