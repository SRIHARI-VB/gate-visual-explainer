Topic and objective

TOPIC: The complexity classes P, NP, NP-hard and NP-complete, and the polynomial-time reduction that relates them — the Algorithms section of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus, which names "Basic concepts of complexity classes P, NP, NP-hard, NP-complete".

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State each of the four definitions without mixing them up, read a reduction in the right direction, prove hardness and membership as two separate steps, and recognise the three situations that look like NP-completeness but are not: 2-SAT, a clique size fixed in advance, and a problem outside NP altogether.

I read the topic as four definitions and one arrow. Every exam item here is a question about which definition is being invoked and which way the arrow points, so the lesson computes one reduction end to end rather than listing problems by name.

Prerequisites

  • Asymptotic complexity — polynomial against exponential, and why the base of the exponent matters.
  • Turing machines and undecidability — a decision problem, a decider, and the halting problem.
  • Graph traversals — enough graph vocabulary for CLIQUE and VERTEX COVER.
  • Propositional logic — clauses, literals, and what it means for an assignment to satisfy a formula.

Approximate learning time: 50 to 70 minutes including the practice set. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. P: decided in polynomial time. NP: a short certificate is checkable in polynomial time.
  2. NP-hard: everything in NP reduces to it. NP-complete: that, and it is in NP.
  3. Hardness flows along the arrow; membership flows against it.
  4. One vertex per literal occurrence turns 3-SAT into CLIQUE, and the clique size is the clause count.
  5. 2-SAT and fixed-size clique are in P; the halting problem is hard but outside NP.

Intuition first: solving against checking

Think of a jigsaw puzzle with the picture on the box. Laying the pieces is the hard part, and nobody knows whether it can always be done quickly. Checking a finished puzzle is easy: you look at it and compare. NP is the class of puzzles where the checking is easy. P is the class where the assembling is easy too. The open question is whether those are the same class of puzzles.

Educational interpretation The analogy carries the certificate idea exactly — the finished puzzle is the certificate, and comparing it to the box is the verifier. It does not carry the polynomial bound, which is why the formal definition follows.

Formal definitions

P is the set of decision problems decidable by a deterministic Turing machine in time bounded by a polynomial in the input length.

NP is the set of decision problems for which there is a polynomial p and a polynomial-time verifier M such that an instance x is a yes instance exactly when some certificate u with length at most p(|x|) makes M(x, u) accept.

Reduction. A reduces to B when a polynomial-time computable function f satisfies: x is a yes instance of A exactly when f(x) is a yes instance of B.

NP-hard: every problem in NP reduces to it. NP-complete: NP-hard and in NP.

Symbols, in words

  • |x| — the length of the input in bits, the thing a polynomial is a polynomial in.
  • certificate — a short string that certifies a yes answer, such as a satisfying assignment.
  • verifier — a machine that reads input and certificate and answers accept or reject.
  • A reduces to B — one fast function turning instances of A into instances of B with the same answer.
  • NP-hard — at least as hard as everything in NP.
  • NP-complete — NP-hard, and itself in NP.
  • 2-SAT / 3-SAT — satisfiability where every clause holds at most two, or exactly three, literals.

The assumptions each statement carries

"NP-complete" is a claim about decision problems. An optimisation problem such as "find the largest clique" is not a decision problem at all; its decision form is "is there a clique of size k". "3-SAT is NP-complete" carries the assumption of three literals per clause — with two it is in P. "CLIQUE is NP-complete" carries the assumption that k is part of the input — with k fixed it is in P.

Every one of those assumptions is a place where a correct-looking answer is wrong, and the Edge cases tab takes them one at a time.

Worked example 1 — one certificate, checked clause by clause

Formula: (x1 or x2 or x3) and (not x1 or not x2 or x3) and (not x1 or x2 or not x3) and (x1 or not x2 or not x3). Certificate: x1 = 1, x2 = 0, x3 = 0.

  1. Clause 1 is satisfied by x1 = 1.
  2. Clause 2 is satisfied by not x2 = 1.
  3. Clause 3 is satisfied by not x3 = 1.
  4. Clause 4 is satisfied by x1 = 1.
  5. All four clauses hold, so the verifier accepts. It read 12 literal slots once: linear work.

The page enumerates all 8 assignments and finds exactly 4 accepted: 100, 010, 001 and 111. The other four each leave a clause with three false literals.

Worked example 2 — the reduction graph, built by hand

Four clauses, three literals each, so 12 vertices. Two vertices are joined when they lie in different clauses and are not opposites.

x1 in clause 1 and x1 in clause 4 are joined. x1 in clause 1 and not x1 in clause 2 are not. x1 and x2 in clause 1 are never joined, because they share a clause.

Out of the 66 possible pairs, 42 survive. The page counts them rather than trusting the rule, and separately checks that no edge joins two vertices of one clause.

Worked example 3 — the clique and the assignment, both ways

Take the clique x1 (clause 1), not x2 (clause 2), not x3 (clause 3), x1 (clause 4). Its labels force x1 = 1, x2 = 0, x3 = 0, which is a satisfying assignment.

In the other direction, the assignment 001 satisfies clause 1 by x3, clause 2 by x3, clause 3 by not x1, and clause 4 by not x2. Those four vertices are pairwise compatible, so they form a clique.

The page checks both directions exhaustively: 12 cliques of size 4 and 4 satisfying assignments, every one of them converting correctly. The numbers differ, and that is fine — the reduction only claims that a clique of size 4 exists exactly when the formula is satisfiable.

Worked example 4 — the complement identity in numbers

On five vertices take the graph with edges 1-2, 1-3, 2-3, 3-4, 4-5. Its largest clique is 3, namely vertices 1, 2, 3.

The complement has 10 minus 5 = 5 edges, and its smallest vertex cover has size 2, namely vertices 4 and 5.

3 + 2 = 5. The page repeats this on every graph with four or five vertices — 64 plus 1024, that is 1088 graphs — and the two sizes sum to the vertex count in all of them.

2-SAT: the clause width changes the class

Every clause has at most two literals. Build the implication graph, take strongly connected components, and the instance is satisfiable exactly when no variable shares a component with its negation. That is linear time.

Checked here on 3060 four-clause formulas over three variables against brute force: no disagreement.

Fixed-size clique is in P

"Is there a triangle?" is answered by trying all triples: n cubed. For a fixed k it is n to the k, a polynomial.

CLIQUE is NP-complete only when k arrives as part of the input, so no fixed exponent is available.

The halting problem is NP-hard and outside NP

NP contains only decidable problems, and halting is undecidable, so halting is not in NP. It is still NP-hard, because a decider for halting would decide everything.

Hence NP-hard is not contained in NP-complete. A problem outside NP can never be NP-complete, however hard it is.

An unsatisfiable instance is not a broken instance

The eight-clause formula over three variables has no satisfying assignment. Its reduction graph has 24 vertices and 204 edges, and the largest clique has size 7.

The reduction must answer no, and it does: there is no clique of size 8. A reduction that only worked on satisfiable formulas would not be a reduction.

The boundary that catches people: the arrow direction

To prove a candidate X is NP-hard you reduce a known NP-complete problem to X. Reducing X to 3-SAT proves only that X is in NP — it is the wrong arrow for hardness, and it is offered as a wrong option in the standard multiple-choice version of this question.

If a question gives you both arrows, they are the two halves of a completeness proof: 3-SAT to X gives hardness, X to 3-SAT gives membership. One arrow alone is half a theorem.

"NP means not polynomial"

Why it tempts: the letters look like a negation, and the problems in NP are the ones without known polynomial algorithms.

Why it is wrong: NP means non-deterministic polynomial time. Every problem in P is in NP, so NP contains all the problems that do have polynomial algorithms.

Minimal counterexample: cycle detection is in P and therefore in NP. If NP meant "not polynomial" it could not contain it.

Correct rule: NP is a positive statement about verifying a certificate, not a negative statement about solving.

"NP-hard and NP-complete are the same thing"

Why it tempts: every NP-complete problem is NP-hard, and the exam mostly talks about NP-complete ones.

Why it is wrong: NP-complete adds membership in NP. The halting problem is NP-hard and not in NP.

Minimal counterexample: the halting problem, which is undecidable, so it cannot lie in a class of decidable problems.

Correct rule: NP-complete = NP-hard ∩ NP.

"Reducing my problem to 3-SAT proves it is NP-complete"

Why it tempts: 3-SAT is the famous NP-complete problem, so reducing to it feels like the right move.

Why it is wrong: that arrow proves only membership in NP. Hardness needs the opposite arrow.

Minimal counterexample: 2-SAT reduces to 3-SAT, since every 2-clause is a 3-clause with a repeated literal, and 2-SAT is in P.

Correct rule: reduce a known NP-complete problem to the candidate for hardness.

"A hard problem must be NP-complete"

Why it tempts: the two phrases are used interchangeably in casual writing.

Why it is wrong: completeness also requires membership in NP, and some hard problems are not decision problems at all.

Minimal counterexample: the travelling-salesman optimisation problem asks for a tour, not a yes or no, so it is not in NP as stated.

Correct rule: state the decision version before classifying it.

"More cliques means more satisfying assignments"

Why it tempts: the reduction is described as a correspondence, so the counts feel as though they should match.

Why it is wrong: one assignment can give several cliques, because a clause with two true literals offers a choice.

Minimal counterexample: this formula has 12 cliques of size 4 and 4 satisfying assignments.

Correct rule: the reduction preserves existence, not the number of witnesses.

The one-line summary

Name the claim — membership, hardness or completeness — then check that the arrow points the way that claim needs.

Which question categories apply

Categories A (direct definition), B (property verification), E (counterexample reasoning), F (multiple conditions) and G (misconception trap) all apply, and F and G are the two that carry most of the marks: a question that supplies one condition and asks what is still missing, and a question whose wrong option is the reversed arrow. C (calculation) appears only as counting certificates or clique sizes, and D (construction) only as building a gadget.

A — direct definition

"A problem in NP is NP-complete if …" Supply the missing condition: a known NP-complete problem reduces to it.

Wrong approach: choosing "it reduces to 3-SAT", which proves membership rather than hardness.

F — multiple conditions

"Y is NP-complete and X reduces to Y. What follows?" X is in NP — membership flows against the arrow. X is not necessarily NP-hard, and not necessarily complete.

Wrong approach: reading the arrow as hardness and concluding X is NP-complete.

G — misconception trap

"The problems 3-SAT and 2-SAT are …" The clause width is the whole question: NP-complete and in P respectively.

Wrong approach: answering "both NP-complete" because both are called SAT.

B and E — verification and counterexamples

"Which of these statements are true?" Test each option on a small witness: 2-SAT kills "all SAT is hard", the halting problem kills "NP-hard implies NP-complete", a fixed triangle test kills "CLIQUE is always hard".

Wrong approach: testing the options only on the famous problems, where several false statements happen to hold.

The hypothetical-algorithm family

"Suppose a polynomial algorithm is found for X, an NP-complete problem. What happens to the classes?" If X is in P then every problem in NP is in P, so P = NP, and every nontrivial problem in NP becomes complete as well: P = NP = NP-complete. The answer is a collapse to one class, and the reasoning is one line of transitivity. No claim is made here that any such algorithm exists.

MCQOriginal practice question

X is a decision problem in NP. Which one of the following makes X NP-complete?

  1. X reduces to 3-SAT in polynomial time.
  2. 3-SAT reduces to X in polynomial time.
  3. X reduces to every problem in NP in polynomial time.
  4. Some problem in NP reduces to X in polynomial time.

Answer: B

Membership in NP is given, so only hardness is missing. Hardness means every problem in NP reduces to X, and it suffices to reduce one known NP-complete problem, such as 3-SAT, to X. That is option B.

Why A is wrong. X reducing to 3-SAT proves X is in NP, which the question already tells us. Why C is wrong. That arrow would say X is easy, not hard. Why D is wrong. "Some problem in NP" is too weak; the definition quantifies over all of NP, and one arbitrary member proves nothing.

The concept tested is the direction of the reduction, which is the single most common trap on this topic.

MCQOriginal practice question

The problems 2-SAT and 3-SAT are respectively

  1. in P and NP-complete
  2. both in P
  3. both NP-complete
  4. NP-complete and undecidable

Answer: A

2-SAT is decided in linear time from the implication graph: satisfiable exactly when no variable shares a strongly connected component with its negation. 3-SAT is NP-complete by the Cook–Levin theorem.

Why C appears. Both are called SAT, so the clause width looks like a detail rather than the whole question. Why B appears. Nothing suggests 3-SAT is easy; no polynomial algorithm is known. Why D appears. Both problems are decidable — trying all assignments is a decision procedure, just a slow one.

This lesson checks the 2-SAT test against brute force on 3060 formulas and finds no disagreement.

NATOriginal practice question

A 3-CNF formula has 4 clauses and uses 3 variables. The number of distinct certificates the verifier may be handed, one per assignment, is _______.

Answer: 8

A certificate is one truth value per variable, so the count is 2 to the number of variables, not 2 to the number of clauses. Three variables give 8.

The likely error. 16, from doubling for the clause count, or 4, from reading the clause count as the variable count. The verifier in this lesson reads a 3-bit certificate and accepts exactly 4 of the 8.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements are true?

  1. Every problem in P is in NP.
  2. Every NP-hard problem is in NP.
  3. If a problem is in NP and NP-hard, it is NP-complete.
  4. If an NP-complete problem is in P, then P equals NP.

Answer: A, C and D

A: a decider wrapped in a verifier that ignores the certificate is a polynomial verifier. C: that is the definition of NP-complete. D: every problem in NP reduces to the complete one, so all of NP falls into P.

Why B is false. The halting problem is NP-hard and undecidable, so it is not in NP. The distinction being tested: hardness and membership are independent conditions, and NP-complete is their conjunction.

Partial marking exists in the real exam; no marks are assigned here.

MCQOriginal practice question

Y is NP-complete and X reduces to Y in polynomial time. Which one of the following is necessarily true?

  1. X is NP-complete.
  2. X is NP-hard.
  3. X is in NP.
  4. Y reduces to X in polynomial time.

Answer: C

Membership flows against the arrow. X reduces to a problem in NP, so X is in NP: transform the instance, then run Y verifier. Nothing more follows, because X may be as easy as a problem in P.

Why A and B are wrong. Both would need the opposite arrow. A problem in P reduces to an NP-complete problem and is neither hard nor complete. Why D is wrong. The reverse reduction is not given, and it need not exist for this X.

NATOriginal practice question

A 3-CNF formula has 5 clauses. In the CLIQUE reduction graph, a clique that witnesses satisfiability must have size _______.

Answer: 5

The reduction gives one row of vertices per clause, and vertices in the same row are never adjacent. A clique therefore takes at most one vertex per clause, and it witnesses satisfiability exactly when it takes one from each: size equal to the clause count, 5.

The likely error. 3, the number of literals per clause, or 15, the number of vertices. The vertex count is three times the clause count, but the clique size is the clause count.

MCQOriginal practice question

On a graph with n vertices and no edges, the smallest vertex cover of the complement has size

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. n − 1
  4. n

Answer: C — n − 1

The complement is the complete graph, whose every edge must be touched. Leaving one vertex out still covers every edge, because every edge has another endpoint. Leaving two out leaves an uncovered edge. So the minimum is n − 1.

The check: the largest clique of the original graph is 1, since there are no edges, and 1 + (n − 1) = n, which is exactly the complement identity. Why D appears. n covers every edge but is not smallest. Why A and B appear. Both leave an edge uncovered once n is at least 3.

Revision sheet

P: decided in polynomial time. NP: a polynomial certificate is checked in polynomial time. NP-hard: everything in NP reduces to it. NP-complete: NP-hard and in NP. Reductions run in polynomial time and preserve yes and no.

Definitions, one line each

Ppoly-time decider
NPpoly-time verifier, poly-length certificate
A reduces to Bpoly-time f, x in A iff f(x) in B
NP-hardall of NP reduces to it
NP-completeNP-hard and in NP

Known: P ⊆ NP ⊆ PSPACE. Open: strictness of either inclusion.

Decision procedure

  1. Which claim is being made: membership, hardness, or completeness?
  2. Membership: write a polynomial verifier, or reduce the problem to a known member of NP.
  3. Hardness: reduce a known NP-complete problem to the candidate.
  4. Completeness: do both, and say so in two sentences.
  5. Check the clause width and whether k is part of the input.

Traps, each with its counterexample

"NP means not polynomial" → cycle detection is in both P and NP.

"NP-hard implies complete" → the halting problem.

"Reduce my problem to 3-SAT for hardness" → the arrow points the wrong way.

"All SAT is NP-complete" → 2-SAT is in P.

"CLIQUE is always hard" → triangles are found in n cubed.

Five quick checks

1. A formula with 4 clauses and 3 variables has how many candidate certificates? 8.

2. Its reduction graph has how many vertices? 12.

3. A clique witnessing satisfiability of 5 clauses has size? 5.

4. Largest clique plus smallest cover of the complement equals? n.

5. If an NP-complete problem is in P, what follows? P = NP = NP-complete.

Where this connects

The polynomial-exponential gap is the one asymptotic complexity measures; this lesson only classifies problems by it. Undecidability supplies the example of a problem that is hard but outside NP — that is Turing machines and undecidability. The CLIQUE and VERTEX COVER identities lean on matching and colouring, where independent sets and covers are already related. The satisfiability side of the reduction is propositional logic, and the "if one complete problem is easy then all of NP is easy" argument is the same composition-of-costs reasoning used throughout divide and conquer.
