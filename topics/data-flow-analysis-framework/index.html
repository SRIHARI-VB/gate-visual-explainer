Topic and objective

TOPIC: Data-flow analysis as a framework — the four answers (direction, lattice domain, meet operator, transfer functions) from which every classical analysis is built, instantiated on the three the syllabus names: constant propagation, liveness analysis and common subexpression elimination. This is the phrase "Local optimisation, Data flow analyses: constant propagation, liveness analysis, common sub expression elimination" at the end of Section 7 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given an unfamiliar analysis, read its four parts off its definition rather than recalling its formulas. Write GEN and KILL for a block and derive its transfer function. Decide whether a join must use union or intersection, and say what goes wrong if you choose the other. Compute live variables and available expressions on a small control-flow graph to a fixpoint, including a back edge. Explain why the iteration terminates. Distinguish the meet-over-all-paths answer from the fixpoint answer, and say which one a compiler is allowed to use.

I read this topic as one machine with four knobs, plus four settings of those knobs. The machine is the framework; the settings are reaching definitions, available expressions, liveness and constant propagation. Almost every exam question here is either "compute a fixpoint on this graph" or "which setting is this?", and both are answerable from the same four knobs.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. An analysis is four answers: direction, a lattice of facts, a meet operator, and a transfer function. Formulas are the last thing you need.
  2. The meet operator is union for a may analysis and intersection for a must analysis. Choosing the wrong one makes the answer wrong, not merely imprecise.
  3. The transfer function is GEN ∪ (IN − KILL), and GEN is added after the subtraction so a fact generated inside a block survives its own block.
  4. The iteration terminates because the lattice has finite height and every transfer function is monotone. Both halves are needed.
  5. For gen-kill analyses the fixpoint and the meet-over-all-paths answer coincide. For constant propagation they do not, and the fixpoint is the safe one.

Intuition: four questions asked of every block

A compiler wants to know something about a program without running it. It cannot try every input, so it asks a weaker question that has a definite answer: what is true here on every execution, or on some execution? That question is answered block by block, and the blocks are stitched together by two rules — one for combining what arrives from different directions, and one for what a block does on the way through.

Think of a postman delivering facts along the arrows of the control-flow graph. Some facts must reach every address (a must analysis); some need only reach one (a may analysis). The postman carries a bag with a fixed capacity, and every delivery either adds to the bag or removes from it — which is why the loop over the graph has to stop. Educational interpretation — an analogy for the mechanism, not a description of any implementation.

Formal definition: the framework

A data-flow framework is four things:

  • Direction. Forward if the fact describes what happened before a point; backward if it describes what will happen after.
  • Domain and meet. A set of possible facts, ordered so that every pair has a greatest lower bound, with a meet operator ⊓ that computes it. A fact is one element of this set.
  • Transfer functions. One monotone function per block, from the domain to itself.
  • Boundary value. The one value set by hand — at the entry for a forward analysis, at the exit for a backward one.

Verified fact This quadruple, and the requirement that the transfer functions contain the identity and be closed under composition, is the standard formulation (Ullman's data-flow frameworks lecture; the same axioms in the Dragon Book's chapter on machine-independent optimisation).

Formal definition: the two equations

IN[b] = ⊓ OUT[p] over every predecessor p of b
The facts that arrive at the top of block b are the meet of the facts leaving each block that can jump to b.
OUT[b] = fb(IN[b])
The facts that leave b are what the block does to what arrived. For a gen-kill analysis, fb(x) = GEN[b] ∪ (x − KILL[b]).

A fixpoint is an assignment of a value to every IN and OUT that satisfies both equations at every block simultaneously. The analysis is the problem of finding one.

The word that decides everything: meet

The meet operator says how two paths combine, and it is chosen by the question, not by taste:

  • Union (a may analysis). The fact survives if some path carries it. "Variable x may be live here." "Definition d may reach here."
  • Intersection (a must analysis). The fact survives only if every path carries it. "Expression a+b is available here."

Formal definition: the lattice

A partial order is a relation ⊑ that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. A lattice is a partial order in which every two elements have a greatest lower bound. For a family of sets ordered by inclusion, that bound is the intersection; for sets ordered by superset, it is the union.

x ⊑ y if and only if x ⊓ y = x
The order is not an extra assumption: once you have the meet operator, the order comes with it. That is why the meet operator alone fixes which end is "more precise".

Top is the element ⊤ with x ⊓ ⊤ = x for every x; bottom is ⊥ with x ⊓ ⊥ = ⊥. For liveness (subsets, union-meet) top is the full set and bottom is the empty set. For available expressions (supersets, intersection-meet) top is the empty set and bottom is the full set. Verified fact for the definitions; Mathematical derivation for the two top/bottom identifications.

Formal definition: monotone, and distributive

A function f is monotone if x ⊑ y implies f(x) ⊑ f(y). It is distributive if in addition

f(x ⊓ y) = f(x) ⊓ f(y)
Meeting the inputs before applying f gives the same answer as applying f first and meeting afterwards.

Every gen-kill function f(x) = GEN ∪ (x − KILL) is distributive over intersection, and this page checks it by exhausting all 4096 pairs of GEN, KILL and two input sets over a three-expression universe. The transfer function for addition in constant propagation is monotone but not distributive, which is the whole of scene 9.

The iteration, and why it ends

  1. Initialise every block to the optimistic value — top, meaning "assume the fact holds" — and set the boundary value by hand.
  2. Sweep the blocks in the direction the facts travel, computing the neighbour side first.
  3. Repeat until a whole sweep changes nothing.

Termination. The values only move one way down the lattice, because each transfer function is monotone and the meet of a value with anything is below it. A strict descent cannot be longer than the height of the lattice, so the number of sweeps is at most the height times the number of blocks. Mathematical derivation; the same argument appears in the CMU lattices-and-solvers lecture cited on the Sources tab.

Counterexample: why finite height alone is not enough

Consider a domain with three values ⊤, m, ⊥ and a "transfer function" that maps ⊤ to m, m to ⊤ and ⊥ to ⊤. The domain has height 3, but the sweep alternates between ⊤ and m for ever. Monotonicity is exactly the property this function lacks: ⊥ ⊑ ⊤ but f(⊥) = ⊤ is not ⊑ f(⊤) = m.

So the termination proof is a conjunction, and a question that asks "why does the analysis terminate?" wants both halves. Mathematical derivation

Example 1 — live variables at four exits (GATE CSE 2023)

Four blocks, one branch, one join and a back edge from B4 to B2.

USE/DEF: B1 {m,n} / {i,j,a} B2 {i,j} / {i,j} B3 ∅ / {a} B4 {a} / {i}
Read from the code: B1 initialises, B2 increments i and decrements j, B3 sets a, B4 computes i from a.
OUT[b] = ∪ IN[succ] IN[b] = USE ∪ (OUT − DEF)
The backward equations. IN at EXIT is the empty set: nothing is live after the program ends.

Round 1. OUT(B4) = ∅, IN(B4) = {a}. OUT(B3) = {a}, IN(B3) = ∅. OUT(B2) = {a}, IN(B2) = {a,i,j}. OUT(B1) = {a,i,j}.

Round 2. The back edge now feeds B2 into B4: OUT(B4) = {a,i,j}, IN(B4) = {a,j}. Then IN(B3) = {j}, OUT(B2) = {a,j}, IN(B2) = {a,i,j}. Round 3 changes nothing.

B1 {a,i,j} B2 {a,j} B3 {a,j} B4 {a,i,j}
That is option D of the four printed options, and only one of the four matches.

Mathematical derivation from the equations, cross-checked by a path search at all twenty (block, variable) pairs. Why m and n never appear: they are used inside B1 and never redefined anywhere, so they are dead at every exit.

Example 2 — which variables are live in both blocks (GATE CSE 2015 Set 1 Q50)

Four blocks in a cycle: B1→B2→B4→B1, with B3→B4.

use/def: B1 {q,r,v}/{p,s,u} B2 {r,u}/{v} B3 {s,u}/{q} B4 {r,v}/{q}
The graph has no exit edge, so the only boundary is that the sets start empty.

Iterating gives IN(B1) = {q,r,v}, IN(B2) = {r,u}, IN(B3) = {r,s,u,v}, IN(B4) = {r,v}.

IN(B2) ∩ IN(B3) = {r,u}
The question asks for variables live at both statements, so the answer is the intersection, and it is option C.

Mathematical derivation — the sets are computed, not quoted. The trap: "live in both" is an intersection over two blocks; "live in either" would be a union and would give {r,s,u,v}, which is option B and is wrong.

Example 3 — the same join, two meet operators

B1 computes t = a + b; the left branch computes u = a + b; the right branch runs a = 5; both reach v = a + b.

intersection: IN(B4) = {a+b} ∩ ∅ = ∅
The right branch killed a+b by writing to a, so the expression is not available after the join. v = a+b must be recomputed.
union: IN(B4) = {a+b} ∪ ∅ = {a+b}
This says the expression is available, and a compiler acting on it would reuse a stale t. The answer is wrong, not merely imprecise.

And inside the left branch IN(B2) = {a+b}, so u = a + b can become u = t. That is global common subexpression elimination, and it applies at one block of this graph and not at the other. Mathematical derivation

Example 4 — where the two answers to one program differ

One branch sets x = 1, y = 2; the other sets x = 2, y = 1; both then run z = x + y.

meet over all paths: z = 3
Each path gives a concrete value for z, and both values are 3, so meeting the two path results gives 3.
fixpoint: x is NAC, y is NAC, so z = NAC
The fixpoint meets the inputs first, and two different constants meet to "not a constant". The addition then has nothing to add.
f(x ⊓ y) = NAC but f(x) ⊓ f(y) = 3
A strict inequality in the lattice: the fixpoint is below the path answer. That is exactly what "not distributive" means.

Which one a compiler uses: the fixpoint, because enumerating paths does not terminate in general. The path answer is a bound on how much precision is being lost, not an algorithm. Verified fact that constant propagation is the standard non-distributive example; Mathematical derivation for the numbers, which are computed on the page.

Six boundaries, each with the condition it turns on

An edge case here is a place where a rule that is usually right stops being right. Each card names the condition, not just the exception.

A back edge makes one pass wrong

On a graph with a cycle, a single backward sweep computes each block from values that are still stale. In the 2023 graph the first sweep gives OUT(B4) = ∅ and the second gives {a,i,j}.

Condition: the graph is acyclic. Only then is one pass in the right order a fixpoint. The wrong answer it produces is option A or B of the paper, both of which look plausible. Mathematical derivation

The entry block is not computed from its predecessors

For a forward analysis the entry has no predecessors. Applying the IN equation there would meet an empty family, and the conventional answer to that is top — which silently overwrites the boundary value you set by hand.

Condition: the block is the entry (or the exit, for a backward analysis). Rule: set it once, by hand, and skip it in every sweep. Educational interpretation of a convention that all the cited sources share.

A variable read and then written is in USE

In i = i + 1 the block uses i and defines i. If USE were "variables read that are not written", i would vanish and every liveness answer on the 2023 graph would lose it.

Condition: the read precedes the write. Note: for this block DEF ⊇ USE overlap, so the two textbook conventions for DEF give the same IN — the ambiguity is harmless here and not in general. Mathematical derivation

An expression is killed by any of its operands

a = 5 kills a+b, and so does b = 5. A KILL set that lists only expressions whose left-hand side matches the assignment misses both.

Condition: the analysis is over expressions. For reaching definitions the corresponding rule is narrower: only definitions of the same variable are killed. Mathematical derivation

An empty set is not "no information"

For available expressions the empty set is top: it means "nothing is known to be available", the optimistic starting point. For liveness the empty set is bottom: it means "nothing is live", the pessimistic starting point.

Condition: which end of the order the empty set sits at, which is fixed by the meet operator. Consequence: the initialisation is not a convention you can choose per problem. Mathematical derivation

Distributivity can fail while monotonicity holds

Addition over the constant lattice is monotone: if you know more about x and y, you know at least as much about x+y. It is not distributive: f(NAC ⊓ NAC) = NAC while f(1) ⊓ f(2) = 3 ⊓ 3 = 3.

Condition: the transfer function combines two facts into one. Any function that does that can lose the correlation between them. Consequence: termination still holds; equality of the two answers does not. Mathematical derivation

Six wrong beliefs, and the smallest thing that refutes each

Each card gives the belief, why it is tempting, a minimal counterexample, and the rule that replaces it.

"Meet is always union, because that is what liveness does"

Why it tempts: the first analysis most people meet is liveness or reaching definitions, and both use union. The formula then gets remembered as "IN = union of OUT".

Minimal counterexample: the diamond of Example 3. Union at the join claims a+b is available on a path where a was reassigned. Intersection gives the empty set, which is the truth.

The rule: union for may, intersection for must. Ask whether the fact needs one path or every path, then pick. Mathematical derivation

"Liveness is a forward analysis because the program runs forwards"

Why it tempts: the program executes from top to bottom, so it feels as though the facts must travel that way.

Minimal counterexample: a variable is live at a point because of a future use. To know it, you must look at what comes after, which is why the boundary sits at the exit and the sweep runs backwards.

The rule: direction follows the question, not the execution order. Past facts flow forward; future facts flow backward. Verified fact

"Constant folding and common subexpression elimination are the same optimisation"

Why it tempts: both replace an expression with something cheaper, and both appear in the same paragraph of the syllabus.

Minimal counterexample: x = 4 * 5 becoming x = 20 is constant folding — arithmetic evaluated at compile time, no second occurrence involved. CSE needs a repeated expression and an analysis that proves it still holds the same value.

The rule: folding needs constants; elimination needs a second occurrence plus availability. This is the distinction GATE CSE 2014 Set 1 Q17 turns on. Verified fact

"A fixpoint is unique, so the initialisation does not matter"

Why it tempts: the loop does converge to a fixpoint whatever you start from, which sounds like uniqueness.

Minimal counterexample: a two-block cycle with no boundary condition has two fixpoints — everything and nothing — and both satisfy the equations. Starting at top reaches the greatest, starting at bottom the least.

The rule: the boundary value plus the starting value select the fixpoint. That is why the entry/exit value is part of the framework and not a detail. Mathematical derivation

"The analysis is exact, so a redundant expression is always removed"

Why it tempts: the fixpoint is the correct solution to the equations, and it is easy to read "correct" as "complete".

Minimal counterexample: scene 9. The fixpoint says z is not a constant although every execution gives 3. A missed optimisation is the normal case, not a bug.

The rule: an analysis is sound when it never claims something false, and precise when it claims as much as it safely can. Soundness is required; precision is a quality. Mathematical derivation

"GEN and KILL are properties of the program"

Why it tempts: they are printed in the same tables as IN and OUT, so they look like results of the analysis.

Minimal counterexample: change nothing in the program and change the analysis: for the same block, GEN is {d1,d2} for definitions, {a,b,x} for variables and {a+b, x+1} for expressions. Nothing about the block changed.

The rule: GEN and KILL are local, computed from the block and the choice of fact. Only IN and OUT depend on the rest of the graph. Mathematical derivation

Four shapes a question takes, and the reasoning each demands

This page names no weightage. The shapes below are the ones this topic's previous-year items take, and each is stated as a procedure rather than a formula to recall.

Shape 1 — run the analysis on a printed graph

A four-block graph with a loop, and a request for IN or OUT at every block. This is the most common shape: 2015 Set 1 Q50, 2023, and both 2026 items.

  1. Write USE and DEF (or GEN and KILL) for each block. This is reading, not analysis.
  2. Write the successor list, and mark the back edge explicitly. Most errors are here.
  3. Iterate in the direction the facts travel, and stop when a whole round changes nothing.
  4. Answer from the final round, and check the question's word: exit means OUT, entry means IN.

Common wrong approach: reading the code forwards and reasoning informally about "where each variable is used". It gives the right answer on acyclic graphs and the wrong one the moment there is a loop.

Shape 2 — classify an analysis

"Which of these is a backward analysis?", "Which technique uses live variable analysis?", "Which statement is FALSE about available expressions?"

  1. Ask what a fact describes: the past (forward) or the future (backward).
  2. Ask how many paths are needed: one (union, may) or all (intersection, must).
  3. Ask what the fact is: a variable, a definition or an expression.
  4. Then name the optimisation it enables, because that is usually the option being tested.

Common wrong approach: memorising "liveness = backward" without the other two axes, which fails the moment the options include very busy expressions or a question about which optimisation a must analysis enables.

Shape 3 — a statement about the equations

"For two statements S1 then S2 in a block, which relation holds?" — GATE CSE 2021 Set 2 Q38.

  1. OUT(S1) is the set live just after S1, and IN(S2) is the set live just before S2. They are the same program point, so they are equal by definition.
  2. Every other option adds a set to that identity. To refute one, build a two-statement block where the added set is non-empty.
  3. Answer by identity, then confirm with a witness. Both are quick.

Common wrong approach: substituting into the block-level equation IN = USE ∪ (OUT − DEF) and hoping the algebra produces one of the options. It does not: the question is about statements inside a block, where there is no meet at all.

Shape 4 — a definition or a distinction

"Which one of the following is FALSE?" — GATE CSE 2014 Set 1 Q17, whose four statements mix basic blocks, available expressions, liveness and constant folding.

  1. Check each statement against the analysis it names, not against the others.
  2. The false one is usually a correct idea attached to the wrong name — folding called elimination, or availability called liveness.
  3. Answer in one line per option and move on; these are 1-mark questions.

Common wrong approach: treating all four optimisations as interchangeable "compiler magic" and picking the option that sounds most technical.

The one habit that covers all four shapes

Before computing anything, write down the four knobs for the analysis in the question: direction, fact, meet, transfer. Every shape above is then mechanical — you are never choosing a formula, only filling in four slots and iterating. The questions that look hard are the ones where the four knobs are stated in words rather than symbols.

MCQGATE CSE 2023

Consider the control flow graph with blocks B1 to B4, where B1 initialises i, j and a from m and n; B2 runs i = i + 1 and j = j - 1; B3 runs a = 20; B4 runs i = a + 1. The edges are B1→B2, B2→B3, B2→B4, B3→B4, and a back edge B4→B2. Which option correctly lists the set of live variables at the exit point of each basic block?

  1. B1: {}, B2: {a}, B3: {a}, B4: {a}
  2. B1: {i, j}, B2: {a}, B3: {a}, B4: {i}
  3. B1: {a, i, j}, B2: {a, i, j}, B3: {a, i}, B4: {a}
  4. B1: {a, i, j}, B2: {a, j}, B3: {a, j}, B4: {a, i, j}

Answer: option 4 — B1 {a,i,j}, B2 {a,j}, B3 {a,j}, B4 {a,i,j}

The three rounds. Round 1, sweeping B4 then B3 then B2 then B1 with everything empty: IN(B4) = {a}, IN(B3) = ∅, IN(B2) = {a,i,j}, OUT(B1) = {a,i,j}. Round 2, now with the back edge live: OUT(B4) = IN(B2) = {a,i,j}, so IN(B4) = {a} ∪ ({a,i,j} − {i}) = {a,j}; IN(B3) = {j}; OUT(B2) = {j} ∪ {a,j} = {a,j}; IN(B2) = {i,j} ∪ ({a,j} − {i,j}) = {a,i,j}. Round 3 changes nothing.

Why option 3 fails. It keeps i live at the exit of B2, but B2 defines i and the value it writes is not read before the next write on either successor path. It also drops i at the exit of B4, where the back edge carries it into i = i + 1.

Why options 1 and 2 fail. Both are what one pass without the back edge produces. Option 2 is the classic: correct for acyclic graphs, wrong here.

The check. A path search for "a use of v reachable from the exit with no intervening definition" agrees at all twenty (block, variable) pairs, so this is not a coincidence of the iteration order.

On the question number. Archives number this item differently (27 in the GATE Overflow index, 37 in some other listings), so the year and the question text are given rather than a number that may not match the paper.

MCQGATE CSE 2015 Set 1 Q50

A variable x is live at a statement Si if there exists a statement Sj that uses x, there is a path from Si to Sj, and that path has no intervening assignment to x. For a graph in which B1 has use {q,r,v} and def {p,s,u}, B2 has use {r,u} and def {v}, B3 has use {s,u} and def {q}, and B4 has use {r,v} and def {q}, the variables live both at the statement in B2 and at the statement in B3 are:

  1. p, s, u
  2. r, s, u
  3. r, u
  4. q, v

Answer: option 3 — r and u

The computed sets. Iterating the backward equations gives IN(B1) = {q,r,v}, IN(B2) = {r,u}, IN(B3) = {r,s,u,v}, IN(B4) = {r,v}.

Why the intersection. The question asks for variables live at both points, so the answer is IN(B2) ∩ IN(B3) = {r,u}. Option 2 is the union, which answers a different question — "live at either".

Why r survives in both. B2 uses r and B4 uses r, and no path from either point defines r before a use. Why u survives in both. B2 uses u and B3 uses u, and neither defines it.

Why s is excluded. s is live at the entry of B3, because B3 uses it, but not at the entry of B2: from B2 you reach B4 or B3, and B3's use of s is after B3 has already… in fact the path from B2 through B3 reaches a use of s, but B1 then defines s before any further use, so the value at B2 is not the one B3 reads on the loop's next turn. The computed set settles it, which is the point of running the equations rather than arguing.

MCQGATE CSE 2021 Set 2 Q38

In liveness analysis, USE(S) is the set of variables used in S, IN(S) the set live at the entry of S, OUT(S) the set live at the exit of S. For a basic block consisting of S1 followed by S2, which one of the following is correct?

  1. OUT(S1) = IN(S2)
  2. OUT(S1) = IN(S2) ∪ USE(S1)
  3. OUT(S1) = IN(S2) ∪ OUT(S2)
  4. OUT(S1) = USE(S1) ∪ IN(S2)

Answer: option 1 — OUT(S1) = IN(S2)

Because they are the same program point. The exit of S1 and the entry of S2 are one position in the block, so the set live there is one set with two names. No analysis is needed to see it.

Why option 2 fails. Take S1 as c = a + b and S2 as d = 7, with a and b dead afterwards. Then IN(S2) = ∅ and OUT(S1) = ∅, but USE(S1) = {a,b}, so option 2 predicts {a,b}.

Why option 3 fails. Take S2 as d = 7 with d live after it. Then OUT(S2) = {d} while IN(S2) = ∅ and OUT(S1) = ∅, so option 3 predicts {d}.

Why option 4 fails. It fails for the same witness as option 2.

The trap. Candidates substitute into the block-level equation IN = USE ∪ (OUT − DEF) and look for the algebra to produce an option. That equation relates the two ends of a whole block; this question is about the boundary between two statements inside one, where nothing is combined.

MCQGATE CSE 2014 Set 1 Q17

Which one of the following is FALSE?

  1. A basic block is a sequence of instructions where control enters the sequence at the beginning and exits at the end.
  2. Available expression analysis can be used for common subexpression elimination.
  3. Live variable analysis can be used for dead code elimination.
  4. x = 4 * 5 ⇒ x = 20 is an example of common subexpression elimination.

Answer: option 4 — that transformation is constant folding, not common subexpression elimination

Why option 4 is false. 4 * 5 is evaluated at compile time because both operands are constants. There is no second occurrence of the expression and no analysis of availability involved, so nothing is being "eliminated" as a subexpression.

Why the others are true. Option 1 is the definition of a basic block. Option 2 is the standard use of available expressions — this page's Example 3 is exactly that. Option 3 is standard: if a variable is not live after an assignment, the assignment can go.

The distinction to carry away. Folding needs constants. Elimination needs a repeated expression plus a proof that its value still holds. Both are "optimisation", and the paper is testing whether you can tell them apart.

MSQOriginal practice

Which one or more of the following are TRUE of the framework used for available expressions?

  1. The meet operator is set intersection.
  2. The boundary value at the entry is the set of all expressions.
  3. The transfer function of a block is GEN ∪ (IN − KILL).
  4. Every block is initialised to the empty set before the first sweep.

Answer: 1 and 3

1 is true. Availability is a must property: every path must compute the expression, so the join is an intersection.

3 is true. That is the gen-kill transfer function, and the page checks that it distributes over intersection on all 4096 combinations over a three-expression universe.

2 is false. The entry has no predecessors, so nothing is available there; the boundary value is the empty set. The empty set is top for this analysis, not "all expressions".

4 is false, and it is the subtle one. Initialising every block to the empty set starts the iteration at the bottom of the reversed order, which is the pessimistic end. It still converges, but it converges to the wrong fixpoint — the same error as starting a liveness analysis with everything live. The optimistic start for available expressions is the full set of expressions.

NATOriginal practice

A control-flow graph has five blocks and uses the five variables a, b, c, d, e. Liveness is computed by round-robin iteration starting from the empty set at every block. What is the largest number of rounds the iteration can need before a round changes nothing, counting the confirming round?

Answer: 6

Why. Each variable can enter a block's set at most once, because the sets only grow. A block's set can therefore change at most five times, and after the fifth change one more full sweep is needed to observe that nothing changed. So six rounds suffice.

The bound, stated properly. The lattice of subsets of five variables ordered by inclusion has height 6: the longest strict chain is ∅ ⊂ {a} ⊂ {a,b} ⊂ … ⊂ {a,b,c,d,e}, which has six elements. A strict descent or ascent cannot be longer than the height, so the number of sweeps is at most the height times the number of blocks — here 30 — and in the tight case 6.

Why not 5. Five rounds is the number of changes, not the number of rounds: the sixth is the one that reports "no change" and stops the loop. A question that says "how many iterations until convergence" wants that confirming round.

Why the answer does not depend on the graph. It is an upper bound over all graphs with five variables. A particular graph may converge in two, as the available-expressions diamond on this page does.

MCQOriginal practice

In the graph of Example 3 — B1 computes t = a + b, B2 (left) computes u = a + b, B3 (right) runs a = 5, both reach B4 which computes v = a + b — a compiler performs global common subexpression elimination using available expressions. What is the correct outcome?

  1. Both u = a + b and v = a + b are replaced by copies of t.
  2. Neither is replaced, because the right branch kills a+b.
  3. u = a + b becomes u = t; v = a + b is left alone.
  4. The analysis cannot decide, because the graph contains a join.

Answer: option 3

Why u is replaced. IN(B2) = OUT(B1) = {a+b}, so at the entry of B2 the value of a+b is the one computed in B1. Reusing t is safe.

Why v is not. IN(B4) = OUT(B2) ∩ OUT(B3) = {a+b} ∩ ∅ = ∅, because B3 wrote to a. On that path t no longer holds a+b, so the addition must be redone.

Why option 1 is the trap. It is exactly what the union-meet version of the analysis would license, and it would produce a wrong program on the branch through B3.

Why option 4 is wrong. A join is precisely what the meet operator is for. The analysis has a definite answer at a join; it is the choice of meet that decides whether the answer is right.

MCQOriginal practice

Which statement about constant propagation is correct?

  1. Its transfer functions are not monotone, so the fixpoint may not exist.
  2. It is distributive, so the fixpoint and the meet-over-all-paths answer always agree.
  3. It is monotone but not distributive, so the fixpoint can be less precise than the meet-over-all-paths answer.
  4. Its lattice has infinite height, so the iteration need not terminate.

Answer: option 3

Why it is monotone. Knowing more about the inputs can never make the output less determined: if a fact about x is below another, the computed fact about x+y is below it too. Monotonicity is what makes the fixpoint exist and the iteration terminate.

Why it is not distributive. On the page's diamond, f(x ⊓ y) is NAC while f(x) ⊓ f(y) is 3. A strict inequality, so the two answers differ.

Why option 4 is wrong. The lattice has finite height: UNDEF, then the constants, then NAC. Three levels, so the descent is bounded and the iteration always stops.

Why option 1 is wrong. Non-monotonicity would break termination, but constant propagation does not have that problem — non-distributivity is a loss of precision, not a loss of termination.

The one-screen revision sheet

Everything needed to answer a question on this topic, in the order you would use it.

The four knobs

direction · domain with meet · transfer functions · boundary value
Write these four before touching the graph. Every question in this topic is answered by filling them in.
IN[b] = ⊓ OUT[p] OUT[b] = fb(IN[b])
Forward. Swap IN/OUT and pred/succ for backward, and move the boundary from entry to exit.
fb(x) = GEN[b] ∪ (x − KILL[b])
The gen-kill transfer function. GEN after the subtraction, so a fact generated in the block survives it.

The four analyses

  • Reaching definitions — forward, union (may), facts are definition labels. KILL: other definitions of the same variable. Enables constant propagation, use-def chains.
  • Available expressions — forward, intersection (must), facts are expressions. KILL: any assignment to an operand. Enables global CSE.
  • Liveness — backward, union (may), facts are variables. IN = USE ∪ (OUT − DEF). Enables register allocation, dead-code elimination.
  • Very busy expressions — backward, intersection (must), facts are expressions. Enables hoisting out of loops.

Decision procedure for a computation question

  1. Write USE/DEF (or GEN/KILL) per block. Reading, not analysis.
  2. List the successors and mark every back edge. Errors live here.
  3. Initialise to the optimistic value; set the boundary by hand and never recompute it.
  4. Sweep in the direction the facts travel, neighbour side first.
  5. Stop when a whole sweep changes nothing. Count that sweep.
  6. Read the answer off the final table, checking whether the question says entry (IN) or exit (OUT).

The four errors

  • Union at a must-join — gives a plausible, wrong, unsafe answer.
  • A forgotten back edge — gives the acyclic answer.
  • A dropped GEN — makes facts generated inside a block invisible.
  • Counting only the rounds that changed — an off-by-one on the iteration count.

Three distinctions the paper tests

May against must. One path or every path. Union or intersection. The same graph, two different questions, two different answers.

Sound against precise. Sound means never claiming something false. Precise means claiming as much as is safely possible. The fixpoint is sound; it is not always the most precise safe answer, and scene 9 is the proof.

Folding against elimination. x = 4 * 5 to x = 20 needs only constants. Common subexpression elimination needs a second occurrence plus a proof that the value still holds.
