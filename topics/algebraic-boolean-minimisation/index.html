Topic and objective

TOPIC: Algebraic minimisation of a Boolean function — using the identities of Boolean algebra as rewrite rules to turn one expression of a function into a shorter expression of the same function, including the consensus theorem and the two rules that do most of the work.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a sum of products, apply combine, absorb, eliminate-a-literal and remove-a-consensus-term, naming the rule and the justification at each step. Count literals before and after. Say precisely what the algebraic method does and does not guarantee, and produce a case where it stops short of the minimum. Remove a consensus term when it is the consensus of two terms present, and refuse to remove one when the two terms disagree on two variables.

I read this topic as one rewrite system with a cost attached, and a stopping problem. Prerequisites

Two lessons make this easy; none are strictly required.

  • Boolean algebra and minimisation — the identities, minterms, maxterms, canonical forms, and the map. The rule table here is the same table, used differently.
  • The tabular method — the algorithmic route this lesson is measured against.
  • Combinational circuits — why a literal is a gate input, and what a glitch is.

Approximate learning time: 70 minutes, of which about 25 is the animation.

Five ideas, and the rest follows

  1. A Boolean function is fixed by its truth table; an expression is one description of it, and rewriting an expression never changes the function.
  2. The cost is the number of literals, because a literal is a gate input.
  3. Four rules reduce a sum of products: combine, absorb, eliminate a literal, remove a consensus term. A fifth, factoring, changes the shape.
  4. Every rule is sound and none is complete: the rules can stall on an expression that is still too long, and the answer depends on the order you apply them.
  5. Only the map and the table can certify a minimum. The algebra can produce one; it cannot prove one.

Intuition: editing a sentence, not writing a new one

You are handed a sentence that says the right thing in too many words. You are allowed to delete a word, merge two clauses, or rewrite a phrase into a shorter equivalent — but only edits that keep the meaning exactly. You stop when no edit you can see applies. Whether the sentence is now the shortest possible is a different question, and nothing in the editing tells you.

Educational interpretation The analogy maps: sentence = expression, meaning = truth table, edit = rewrite rule, shortest sentence = minimum-literal expression. It stops mapping at the map and the table, which do not edit — they search.

The formal objects, with every symbol in words

Literal — one occurrence of a variable, negated or not. Product term (a cube) — an AND of literals. Sum of products — an OR of product terms. Cover — the set of minterms on which a product term is 1.

Two expressions are equivalent when they are 1 on exactly the same rows of the truth table. A rewrite replaces a sub-expression by an equivalent one, so equivalence is preserved by construction.

$$\text{cost}(F) = \sum_{\text{terms}} (\text{number of literals in the term})$$ The cost of an expression is its total literal count: every variable occurrence, counted once each time it appears.

A minimum sum of products is a sum of products with the fewest literals among all sums of products for that function; ties are broken by the number of terms.

The rule table, and the dual column

Every line is an identity, so it can be used in either direction. The dual of a line is what you get by swapping AND with OR and 0 with 1 throughout; a valid identity always has a valid dual, so the right-hand column is a consequence rather than a second fact.

In every line, X, Y and Z stand for product terms, not single variables — except in the elimination rule, where X must be a single literal. That exception is the subject of the Edge cases tab.

The assumption under all of it

A rewrite is only legal if the two sides are equal for every assignment. That is why the table is a table of identities and not of handy transformations: a step you cannot justify by one of these lines is a guess, and a guess can change the function.

Mathematical derivation Since each rule is an identity, any rewrite preserves the truth table. The model confirms it mechanically: 250 applications across every pair of three-variable cubes, no difference in the resulting truth table.

What the cost measure ignores

The literal count is a proxy. It tracks the number of gate inputs in a two-level circuit, so it tracks area and wiring, and nothing else. It does not track depth (delay), it does not distinguish a two-level from a multi-level form, and it says nothing about hazards.

So "minimal" always needs a noun after it: a minimal sum of products, a minimal two-level circuit, a minimal literal count. The Animation tab returns to this in its twelfth scene.

Example 1 — the textbook chain, ten literals to eight

F = a′b + a′b′c′d′ + abcd′. This is the worked example in Roth, Fundamentals of Logic Design, section 3.3, under "eliminating literals".

1. a′b + a′b′c′d′ + abcd′ ten literals, three terms

2. a′(b + b′c′d′) + abcd′ factor a′: nothing deleted, the shape changed

3. a′(b + c′d′) + abcd′ b + b′c′d′ = b + c′d′, with X the single literal b

4. b(a′ + acd′) + a′c′d′ expand, then factor b out of a′b + abcd′

5. b(a′ + cd′) + a′c′d′ a′ + acd′ = a′ + cd′, with X the literal a′

6. a′b + bcd′ + a′c′d′ multiply out: a sum of products again

Eight literals, and the exhaustive search over every cover of the six minterms confirms eight is the minimum. Line 5 has seven literals, because a factored form is not a sum of products and its count is not comparable.

Example 2 — combine, factor, eliminate, then an argument the rules do not give

F = Σ(0,1,3,5,7) on a, b, c. Canonical form is five terms and fifteen literals.

1. a′b′c′ + a′b′c = a′b′ combine; c is the only variable that differs

2. a′bc + abc = bc combine; a is the only variable that differs

3. a′b′ + bc + ab′c seven literals, three terms

4. bc + ab′c = c(b + ab′) factor c — nothing deleted

5. b + ab′ = b + a eliminate the literal, with X the single literal b

6. a′b′ + ac + bc six literals; no reducing rule now applies

7. a′b′ + c a case split, not a rule: see below

Step 7 needs an argument of its own. If a′b′ = 1 then the expression is already 1. If a′b′ = 0 then a or b is 1, so a + b = 1 and ac + bc = c(a + b) = c. Either way the expression equals a′b′ + c — three literals, and the exhaustive search confirms three is the minimum. Mathematical derivation

Example 3 — removing a consensus term

F = xy + x′z + yz. The third term is the consensus of the first two: they disagree on x and share y and z.

1. Check the opposition count: x appears unnegated in xy and negated in x′z. One opposition, so a consensus exists.

2. Build it: keep y and z, drop x. The consensus is yz.

3. Check redundancy on the one row that matters: yz = 1 forces y = z = 1, and then x is 0 or 1, so x′z or xy is already 1.

4. Delete yz: F = xy + x′z, four literals instead of six.

5. Confirm: the two expressions agree on all eight rows.

This is the single most useful rule for the "how many literals" questions, because a consensus term is exactly what a naive algebraic reading leaves behind.

Example 4 — the stall, and the three-step route out

F = wx + xy + x′z′ + wy′z′. Nine literals. No reducing rule applies: no pair differs in one variable, no term is contained in another, no X + X′Y with X a single literal, and no term is a consensus of two others.

1. Add wz′, the consensus of wx and x′z′. Safe, because a consensus term is redundant. Eleven literals.

2. Absorb wy′z′, which is contained in wz′. Eight literals.

3. Remove wz′, which is now the consensus of wx and x′z′ and nothing depends on it. Six literals: wx + x′z′ + xy.

Six is the minimum, and every stage is equal to F. The step that made it possible was the one that made the expression longer.

Example 5 — the same expression, two orders

F = a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + b′c + ab, ten literals. This is the example the lecture notes use to show that consensus removal depends on the order of elimination.

Route 1 — remove consensus terms first: a′c′ + ab + b′c. six literals; this is the minimum

Route 2 — absorb first: a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + ab. eight literals; no reducing rule now applies

Both are equal to F, checked row by row. neither step was illegal

Two literals separate them, and the only difference is which pair you looked at first. This is why "I simplified it as far as I could" is not an answer to "what is the minimum".

Example 6 — a previous-year function, by hand

GATE CSE 2021 Set 2, Question 52: f(w,x,y,z) with f(w,0,0,z) = 1, f(1,x,1,z) = x+z, f(w,1,y,z) = wz+y. Ask for the number of literals in the minimal sum of products.

1. Read the three equations as three conditions: x = y = 0 gives 1; w = y = 1 gives x + z; x = 1 gives wz + y.

2. Two rows are covered by none of them — 0010 and 0011 — so they are don't-cares, not zeros. Everything else is fixed.

3. The four rows with x = y = 0 give x′y′, which covers 0, 1 and the two don't-cares, so it is an implicant.

4. The four rows with w = z = 1 give wz, and all four of them are 1, so wz is an implicant.

5. xy covers 6, 7, 14, 15, all of them 1 — the third implicant. Together the three cover every 1 row.

6. Six literals, and the exhaustive search over every cover with those two don't-cares finds nothing shorter.

The official answer is 6. Step 2 is where most attempts go wrong: treating the two unspecified rows as zeros gives a different, larger function and a longer minimum. Step 3 also shows why a don't-care matters — x′y′ is an implicant only because it covers no 0 row.

Edge case 1 — X must be one literal

The rule X + X′Y = X + Y is quoted everywhere without its condition. If X is a product term with more than one literal, deleting the X′ changes the function.

Counterexample: xy + x′y′z. Delete the x′ and you get xy + y′z. The model evaluates both: they disagree at x = 1, y = 0, z = 1, which is minterm 5 in the abc numbering.

Check it yourself: at that row xy = 0 and x′y′z = 0, so F = 0; but y′z = 1, so the "simplified" expression is 1. The correct route factors first: xy + x′y′z has no common factor, so the rule simply does not apply here.

Edge case 2 — the consensus needs exactly one opposition

For xy and x′y′ the two terms disagree on x and on y. There is no consensus. The blind procedure — take all literals, delete the opposed ones — leaves nothing, which reads as the constant 1.

So xy + x′y′ would become xy + x′y′ + 1 = 1. The model finds four rows where that is wrong.

The condition is not decoration: it is the reason the theorem is true. A consensus term is 1 only where y and z are both 1, and on those rows x has already decided which of the two originals is 1.

Edge case 3 — a term that looks like a consensus but is not

The removal rule needs the third term to be the consensus of two others, not merely to be covered by one. In xy + x′z + yz′, the third term yz′ is not the consensus of xy and x′z — their consensus is yz — so it cannot be removed.

There is a weaker, still-true statement: if a term is covered by the consensus of two others, then the consensus absorbs it and the consensus can then be removed. That is the three-step route in Example 4, and it is the reason the route needs an added term rather than a single deletion.

Edge case 4 — the empty term and the constants

A product term with no literals covers all minterms and is the constant 1; the empty sum covers none and is 0. Both appear in the algebra and neither appears in a sensible answer.

So: a cube containing both x and x′ is 0 and should be deleted on sight, and an expression that reduces to 1 or 0 has a one-line minimum with zero literals. The model deletes such cubes before counting.

Edge case 5 — the minimum need not be unique

Of the 255 non-constant three-variable functions, 28 have more than one minimum cover. Two expressions with the same literal count can both be minimal.

This matters for multiple-select questions: "which of the following are minimal sum-of-products forms" can have two correct options, and eliminating one because "there is only one minimum" is a losing move.

Edge case 6 — minimal is not glitch-free

For xy + x′z, the consensus yz is redundant and removable. Keep it and the circuit is glitch-free when y = z = 1 and x changes: xy and x′z can both drop for a moment because of gate delays, while yz stays 1 throughout.

So the minimum-literal answer and the hazard-free answer are different answers to different questions. A question that says "minimum number of literals" wants the deletion; a question about static hazards wants the term kept.

1. "Algebraic simplification gives the minimum"

Why it tempts: every step is an equality, so nothing can go wrong — the expression is always correct, and it always got shorter.

Minimal counterexample: wx + xy + x′z′ + wy′z′ is a fixed point of all four reducing rules at nine literals. The minimum is six.

Correct rule: soundness and completeness are different properties. Every rule preserves the function; no set of these rules guarantees a minimum, and 148 of the 255 three-variable functions stop above it.

2. "If no rule applies, it is minimal"

Why it tempts: in most textbook exercises, the expression you end with does happen to be minimal, so the habit is never punished.

Minimal counterexample: the same nine-literal expression. Nothing applies, and the answer is 40 per cent too long.

Correct rule: "no rule applies" means you are at a fixed point of your rule set, not at a minimum of the function. Only a search over prime implicants can tell you the difference.

3. "The order of the steps does not matter"

Why it tempts: each individual step is an equality, so it feels as if the endpoint should be determined by the function.

Minimal counterexample: a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + b′c + ab. Consensus removals first give six literals; absorption first gives eight. Both are correct.

Correct rule: the function is determined; the expression is not. The order changes which deletions remain available, and therefore the endpoint.

4. "A consensus term is always safe to remove"

Why it tempts: the theorem is usually memorised as "YZ is redundant", with the condition dropped.

Minimal counterexample: xy + x′y′. Two oppositions, no consensus, and the blind application adds the constant 1.

Correct rule: the consensus exists only for exactly one opposition, and the term you remove must be that consensus.

5. "Fewer terms means fewer literals"

Why it tempts: both counts usually fall together, and the term count is the one you can see at a glance.

Minimal counterexample: xy + x′z has two terms and four literals; xy + x′y′ + z has three terms and five literals — and the second is not shorter in either count, but consider x + xy′z′ (four literals, one term) against x + y′z′ (three literals, two terms): more terms, fewer literals.

Correct rule: minimise the literal count; the term count is a tie-break. Every GATE question that names a number names literals.

6. "The dual of an identity is a separate fact to learn"

Why it tempts: notes print both columns, so it looks like twice the memorisation.

Why it is wrong: swapping AND with OR and 0 with 1 throughout a valid identity always yields a valid identity, so the second column is free.

Correct rule: learn the sum-of-products column, derive the other by duality. The consensus theorem's dual is the only one worth writing out, because its removal rule looks different in product-of-sums form.

What this clause is actually asked as

Community observation Across the previous-year lists for this clause, the questions fall into five shapes. The list below is an observation about the papers those lists reproduce, not a prediction.

  • Count the literals of the minimal form (NAT or MCQ).
  • Which expression is equivalent to a given one (MSQ, often four candidates).
  • Which identity is NOT valid (MCQ) — the four options are near-misses of the rule table.
  • Minimal form of a minterm list or a map, with or without don't-cares.
  • Count the prime implicants (NAT) — the bridge to the tabular method.

The algebraic rules appear in all five, but they are the whole method only in the first three.

The reasoning each shape demands

Count the literals: produce a minimum, then count occurrences — not distinct variables. If the function has don't-cares, decide them first.

Which is equivalent: do not simplify; evaluate both expressions on one row where they differ. Three rows is usually enough to eliminate three options.

Which identity is not valid: substitute x = 1, y = 0 into each line. A rule with a dropped condition fails on some assignment, and the fastest assignments to try are the ones where the eliminated variable changes.

Common wrong approach: simplifying every option fully. It is slower than one counterexample row and it invites arithmetic slips.

Verified previous-year question

Verified fact GATE CSE 2021, Set 2, Question 52 (NAT, 2 marks). Consider a Boolean function f(w,x,y,z) such that f(w,0,0,z) = 1, f(1,x,1,z) = x + z, and f(w,1,y,z) = wz + y. The number of literals in the minimal sum-of-products expression of f is ______.

Answer: 6. The three equations cover every row except 0010 and 0011, which are therefore don't-cares rather than zeros. With those two rows free, the minimal sum of products is x′y′ + wz + xy, six literals. This page recomputes the cover exhaustively from the three equations and reaches six, so the answer is not taken on trust.

Where the marks are lost: treating 0010 and 0011 as zeros. That gives a larger function, a longer minimum, and an answer that is wrong by more than one.

The question text is reproduced from two independent question banks that agree on it and on the answer; the paper itself was not read. See the Sources tab.

The check that beats simplification

For "which of these is equivalent", pick one assignment and evaluate. For f(P,Q) = (P′ + Q) ⊕ (P′Q), try P = 1, Q = 0: the left side is (0 + 0) ⊕ (0) = 0, so every option that is 1 there is eliminated in one line.

Educational interpretation This is a strategy, not a theorem: it never proves equivalence, it only disproves it. For an MSQ you still have to prove the survivors, and for that you simplify.

Two rules the options are built from

Nearly every "NOT valid" option is one of these two with something dropped:

  • X + X′Y = X + Y, quoted as x + x′y = y (dropping X).
  • XY + X′Z + YZ = XY + X′Z, quoted as "any three-term expression with one opposition can lose its third term".

Both fail on a row you can find in ten seconds, which is exactly why they are good distractors.

Q1 — counting literals

The literal count of (x + y)(x′ + z)(y + z) is:

(a) 3  (b) 4  (c) 6  (d) 9

(c) 6. Each of the three clauses holds two literals, and a literal is counted every time it occurs. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6.

(a) counts clauses instead of literals. (b) is the literal count of the equivalent sum of products x′y + xz, which is a different expression with a different count. (d) comes from counting three literals per clause.

The product-of-sums form has 6 literals and its sum-of-products equivalent has 4. Which is "minimal" depends on the form the question names.

MCQ original

Q2 — the elimination rule

Which expression is equal to x + x′y?

(a) x + y  (b) y  (c) x  (d) x′y

(a) x + y. Split on x: if x = 1 both sides are 1; if x = 0 then x′ = 1 and both sides are y. The rule is X + X′Y = X + Y with X the single literal x.

(b) drops the X, which is the classic error: at x = 1, y = 0 the left side is 1 and y is 0. (c) drops the Y, which fails at x = 0, y = 1. (d) is the term that was absorbed, not the result.

Write both sides down and test x = 1, y = 0. One row is enough.

MSQ original

Q3 — which rewrites are valid

Mark every rewrite that preserves the function.

I. ab + a′b′c → ab + b′c   II. c + c′d → c + d   III. xy + x′z + yz → xy + x′z   IV. xy + x′y′ → xy + x′y′ + 1

II and III only. II is X + X′Y with X the single literal c. III removes a term that is exactly the consensus of the other two.

I is invalid: X is the two-literal term ab, so the rule does not apply, and the two expressions differ at a = 1, b = 0, c = 1. IV is invalid: ab and a′b′ have two oppositions, so no consensus exists, and adding 1 makes the function constant.

Both invalid lines are the correct rule with its condition dropped — which is how the distractors in this clause are built.

NAT original

Q4 — the minimum for a five-term expression

For F = a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + b′c + ab, the number of literals in a minimal sum of products is ______.

6. a′c′ + ab + b′c: three terms of two literals. The exhaustive search over every cover of the six minterms agrees, and the page's own reducer lands on six when it is run on this expression.

Eight is the other common answer: absorbing first and then stopping gives a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + ab, which is correct and two literals longer.

If you got 8, you are not wrong about the function — you are at a fixed point that is not a minimum. This is the one question on the page where the order of your steps is the whole answer.

MCQ original

Q5 — which term is the consensus

In xy + x′z + yz, which term may be deleted without changing the function?

(a) xy  (b) x′z  (c) yz  (d) none of them

(c) yz. It is the consensus of xy and x′z: the two disagree only on x, and yz keeps what they share.

(a) fails at x = 1, y = 1, z = 0, where xy is the only term that is 1. (b) fails at x = 0, z = 1, y = 0. (d) would be right if no term were a consensus, which is not the case here.

The consensus term is the one containing neither of the opposed literals. Look for the term that shares everything with both and the opposed variable with neither.

MSQ original

Q6 — what the algebraic method guarantees

Mark every statement that is true.

I. Every rewrite in the rule table preserves the function.  II. The method always reaches a minimum-literal expression.  III. The expression you end with can depend on the order of the steps.  IV. Adding a redundant term can lead to a shorter final expression.

I, III and IV. I is what makes the method safe at all. III is the a′b′ + ac + a′c′ + b′c + ab example, six against eight. IV is the stall example, where adding wz′ is what allows three deletions.

II is false: 148 of the 255 three-variable functions stop above the minimum, and 40 of those are still above after one added consensus term.

Statements about a single step are provable. Statements about where the method ends are not.

NAT original

Q7 — build a consensus

The number of literals in the consensus of x′yz and wy′z is ______.

3. The two terms disagree only on y, so the consensus keeps every other literal once: w, x′ and z. That is wx′z, three literals.

If you wrote 4 or 5 you kept the y or the y′. If you said the consensus does not exist, you probably counted the missing literals as oppositions — a literal present in one term and absent from the other is not an opposition.

Opposition means the same variable appearing negated in one term and unnegated in the other. Absence is not opposition.

MCQ original

Q8 — minimum for a minterm list

For F(a,b,c) = Σ(0,1,3,5,7), the number of literals in a minimal sum of products is:

(a) 3  (b) 4  (c) 5  (d) 6

(a) 3. a′b′ + c: the pair 0,1 gives a′b′, and the four minterms 1,3,5,7 all have c = 1, which is the single literal c. Three literals.

(b) and (c) come from covering 1 twice without noticing, or from missing that a single literal can cover four minterms. (d) is the two-term answer written out with both terms fully expanded.

A term with one literal covers half the rows. Whenever a column of the map is all ones, that single literal is in the minimum.

MCQ original

Q9 — factoring and the cost

Which expression is equal to AB + AC + AD and has fewer literals?

(a) A(B + C + D)  (b) A + B + C + D  (c) AB(C + D)  (d) ABCD

(a). A(B + C + D) is four literals against six, and it is the same function: it is 1 exactly when A is 1 and at least one of B, C, D is 1.

(b) is 1 whenever any variable is 1 — far larger than F. (c) is 1 only when A, B and one of C, D are 1, which misses every row where A = 1 and B = 0. (d) is 1 on one row only.

Factoring cuts literals without changing the function, but it is not free: A(B + C) + D is one gate level deeper than AB + AC + D, so the faster circuit is not always the shorter expression.

Definitions, one line each

  • Literal — one occurrence of a variable, negated or not.
  • Product term (cube) — an AND of literals.
  • Sum of products — an OR of product terms.
  • Cover — the minterms on which a term is 1.
  • Cost — the total literal count.
  • Consensus of XY and X′Z — YZ, defined only when the two terms disagree on exactly one variable.
  • Prime implicant — a term whose cover is contained in the 1s and cannot be enlarged.
  • Minimum SOP — fewest literals among all sums of products; term count breaks ties.

The five rules, with the condition each needs

  • Combine: XY + XY′ = X — the two terms differ in exactly one variable and agree everywhere else.
  • Absorb: X + XY = X — the shorter term's literals are all in the longer one.
  • Eliminate a literal: X + X′Y = X + Y — X must be a single literal, or factor first.
  • Remove a consensus: XY + X′Z + YZ = XY + X′Z — exactly one opposition, and the third term must be the consensus.
  • Factor: AB + AC = A(B + C) — changes the shape, deletes nothing, adds depth.

Decision procedure

1. Count the literals you start with. so you can see progress and compare with an answer

2. Combine any two terms differing in one variable. largest reduction per step

3. Absorb any term contained in another.

4. Eliminate a literal where X is a single literal.

5. Remove a term that is the consensus of two others.

6. Repeat to a fixed point, then factor or add a consensus term if still long.

7. Verify on one 0 row and one 1 row. the only step that proves anything

Traps, each with the case that defuses it

  • "Minimal" without a noun — the factored form has 7 literals where the minimal SOP has 8.
  • Deleting a literal from a multi-literal X — xy + x′y′z against xy + y′z, differing at minterm 5.
  • Consensus with two oppositions — xy + x′y′ plus the constant 1.
  • "Nothing applies, so it is minimal" — nine literals against a minimum of six.
  • Counting distinct variables instead of literals — (x+y)(x′+z)(y+z) is 6, not 4.
  • Assuming one minimum — 28 of 255 three-variable functions have two.

The one-screen summary

A rewrite is safe because it is an equality, and the cost it reduces is the literal count. Four rules reduce a sum of products — combine, absorb, eliminate a literal, remove a consensus term — and factoring changes the shape without deleting anything. None of them can promise a minimum: the rules can stall at a fixed point that is 40 per cent too long, and two legal orders on the same expression give six literals and eight. Adding a redundant term is sometimes the way forward, and choosing which one is a search rather than a rule. The map and the table replace that search with an enumeration, which is why they can certify a minimum and the algebra cannot. When the question asks for a number of literals, produce a candidate, count it, and check it on one row where the function is 0.

Textbook

Roth and Kinney, Fundamentals of Logic Design, sections 3.2 and 3.3. Section 3.2 states the consensus theorem XY + X′Z + YZ = XY + X′Z and its dual. Section 3.3 gives the four algebraic methods: combining terms (XY + XY′ = X), eliminating terms (X + XY = X, then consensus), eliminating literals (X + X′Y = X + Y, "simple factoring may be necessary before the theorem is applied"), and adding redundant terms. Supports: the rule table, the four-method framing, Example 1 (example 3-26), Example 4 (example 3-27), and the note that the added term should be chosen so that it will combine with or eliminate others.
