Topic and objective

TOPIC: The exponential and Poisson distributions as one model seen from two sides — the Poisson for how many events fall in a window, the exponential for how long you wait between them, both driven by the same rate.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read a rate and decide which of the two questions is being asked. Write the Poisson mass function and sum it. Compute its mean and variance by summation and see that both equal the parameter. Explain why a binomial with large n and small p approaches it, and how fast. Integrate the exponential density to its tail, and see that outliving the mean is e⁻¹ at every parameter. Test memorylessness from the conditional definition and name the two laws that have it. Show that exponential gaps and a Poisson count are the same process. Convert a rate to a window before using it. Generate every moment from mean = variance = λ. Say where each model stops fitting.

I am reading this microtopic as one habit: a rate is not yet a parameter. Decide whether the question is a count or a wait, multiply the rate by the window, and only then choose a formula. The ten scenes are that habit applied ten ways.

Prerequisites

Three lessons, all published in this repository:

  • Probability and random variables — density against mass, expectation and variance, the definition of a conditional probability. The exponential is the first continuous distribution whose tail you integrate here, so the density idea must already be comfortable.
  • Conditional expectation — needed only for the memorylessness test, which is a conditional probability of the form P(X > s + t | X > s).
  • Mean, median, mode and standard deviation — needed for the standard-deviation question, where the answer is the square root of the mean.

Approximate learning time

About 45 to 60 minutes for the animation, the worked examples and the practice set, if the prerequisites are already comfortable. This is an estimate, not a measurement.

Educational interpretation The five ideas to leave with: a rate is not a parameter; Poisson has one parameter and it sets both mean and variance; outliving the mean is e⁻¹ whatever the parameter; only the exponential and the geometric forget; and a mean gap is not a mean wait unless the gaps are exponential.

From a rate to two distributions

One number, λ, describes a stream of events. Ask "how many in this window" and you get a discrete distribution on the whole numbers. Ask "how long until the next one" and you get a continuous distribution on the positive reals. Neither is more fundamental; each implies the other, and scene 6 shows the algebra.

Level 1 — the intuition

A bus stand with no timetable. On average a bus comes every ten minutes, but no bus knows that. You arrive at a random moment and wait.

Two different questions come out of that one sentence. Over an hour, how many buses? That is a count, and it is Poisson. How long until the next one? That is a wait, and it is exponential.

Educational interpretation The bus stand is a good picture of the rate and a poor one of independence: real buses bunch together, which is exactly the failure scene 9 computes.

Level 2 — the formal statements

Poisson. For λ > 0 and k = 0, 1, 2, …

P(X = k) = e^(−λ) λ^k / k!

Exponential. For λ > 0 and x ≥ 0, the density is

f(x) = λ e^(−λx)   and   P(X > x) = e^(−λx)

Every symbol in words: λ is the rate, in events per unit time; k is a whole number of events; k! is 1 × 2 × … × k; e is 2.718…; x is a length of time; f(x) is the height of the density at x, not a probability; P(X > x) is the area under the density beyond x.

Level 3 — where the exponential tail comes from, in two lines

The tail is the integral of the density from x to infinity. Substitute u = λt, so du = λ dt:

P(X > x) = ∫ from x to ∞ of λ e^(−λt) dt = [ −e^(−λt) ] from x to ∞ = 0 + e^(−λx)

So the tail is one exponential evaluated at x, and every exponential question is that substitution. The mean follows from the same integral by parts, or from the tail-sum identity for a non-negative variable, E[X] = ∫ from 0 to ∞ of P(X > x) dx = ∫ e^(−λx) dx = 1/λ. Derivation

The page checks the mean a second way, by numerically integrating x·λe^(−λx) at λ = 0.5 and λ = 7, and gets 2.00000000 and 0.14285714 against the formulas 2 and 1/7.

Level 4 — the Poisson mean and variance, by summation

The page does not quote these. It sums. With the masses at k = 0 to 59 for λ = 3:

Σ p(k) = 1.000000000000000
Σ k p(k) = 3.000000000000
Σ k² p(k) − (Σ k p(k))² = 3.000000000000

The variance equals the mean because for this family the second factorial moment is λ² + λ, so the second moment is λ + λ² and subtracting λ² leaves λ. Derivation

Level 5 — why the binomial converges

The binomial mass is C(n,k) p^k (1−p)^(n−k). Put p = λ/n and let n grow. Two limits do the work: C(n,k)/n^k → 1/k!, and (1 − λ/n)^n → e^(−λ). Substituting both leaves e^(−λ) λ^k / k!, which is the Poisson mass. Derivation

The page measures the convergence rather than asserting it. Over k = 0 to 19, the largest absolute gap is 0.0428 at n = 10, 0.00343 at n = 100, 0.000337 at n = 1000, 0.0000336 at n = 10000 and 0.00000336 at n = 100000. Each factor of ten in n buys a factor of about ten in accuracy — the error is of order λ²/(2n).

Level 6 — the boundary: when is Poisson legitimate?

The Poisson limit needs n large and p small with np moderate. The counting model itself needs three things: a constant rate, independence of arrivals, and no simultaneous arrivals.

A useful test that costs nothing: if the observed variance is far from the observed mean, the model does not fit. Scene 9 exhibits ten windows with mean 1 and variance 2.4, where a Poisson model would have put almost no mass on the values the data actually takes.

Level 7 — the boundary: when is exponential legitimate?

The exponential has a constant hazard rate: at every age, the chance of failing in the next instant is the same λ. That is a strong assumption about the world and it is why the distribution is used for memoryless things — radioactive decay, the wait for a Poisson arrival — and not for machine wear, where the hazard rises with age.

Community observation Reliability courses describe this as the "bathtub" curve: high hazard when new, low in middle life, rising when old. The exponential is the flat middle section only. The lesson does not claim GATE asks about bathtub curves; it is here so you know what the memorylessness assumption buys and costs.

Example 1 — the road junction (GATE CSE 2013, Q2)

"Suppose p is the number of cars per minute passing through a certain road junction between 5 PM and 6 PM, and p has a Poisson distribution with mean 3. What is the probability of observing fewer than 3 cars during any given minute in this interval?"

Options were 8/(2e³), 9/(2e³), 17/(2e³) and 26/(2e³).

Answer: 17/(2e³), option C. "Fewer than three" is k = 0, 1, 2. The masses are e⁻³, 3e⁻³ and 4.5e⁻³, which add to 8.5e⁻³ = 17/(2e³) = 0.423190.

Where the distractors come from. 8/(2e³) and 9/(2e³) are two of the three terms, reported alone. 26/(2e³) is 1 + 3 + 9 = 13 doubled, which is what you get by adding P(3) as well and then mis-scaling — P(p ≤ 3) is 0.647232.

Example 2 — the component lifetime (GATE CSE 2021, Set 1, Q18)

"The lifetime of a component of a certain type is a random variable whose probability density function is exponentially distributed with parameter 2. For a randomly picked component of this type, the probability that its lifetime exceeds the expected lifetime (rounded to 2 decimal places) is ______."

Answer: 0.37. The official key accepts 0.35 to 0.39.

The mean is 1/λ = 0.5. The probability asked for is P(X > 0.5) = e^(−2 × 0.5) = e⁻¹ = 0.367879.

The faster route. P(X > E[X]) = e^(−λ · 1/λ) = e⁻¹ for every λ. The page evaluates this at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 7 and 100 and gets 0.367879441171 every time. The parameter is a distractor.

Example 3 — the second moment (GATE CSE 2017, Set 2, Q48)

"If a random variable X has a Poisson distribution with mean 5, then the expectation E[(x+2)²] equals ______."

Answer: 54. Variance equals mean, so Var(X) = 5 and E[X²] = Var + (E[X])² = 5 + 25 = 30. Expand and apply linearity: E[X² + 4X + 4] = 30 + 4(5) + 4 = 54.

The trap. Using (E[X])² = 25 as the second moment gives 49. The difference between 54 and 49 is exactly the variance, which is the only place a GATE question on this can hide a mark.

Example 4 — the requests in 45 minutes (GATE IT 2007, Q57)

"In a multi-user operating system, on average 20 requests are made to use a particular resource per hour. The arrival of requests follows a Poisson distribution. The probability that no request is made in 45 minutes is…"

Answer: e⁻¹⁵. The rate is 20 per hour and the window is 0.75 hour, so λ = 15. No request is the mass at zero, e⁻¹⁵ = 3.06 × 10⁻⁷.

Why this is worth doing. The same question appears in university and UGC-NET papers, and the only thing that varies is the window. Using the rate 20 directly gives e⁻²⁰ = 2.06 × 10⁻⁹, which is nine times smaller; using the fraction 0.75 gives e^(−0.75) = 0.472.

Example 5 — memorylessness in use

A component's lifetime is exponential with mean 4 years. It has already run 3 years. What is the probability it runs at least 2 more?

λ = 1/4. Memorylessness says the answer is the probability that a new component runs 2 years: e^(−2/4) = e^(−0.5) = 0.6065.

Checked the long way: P(X > 5 | X > 3) = e^(−5/4) / e^(−3/4) = 0.2865 / 0.4724 = 0.6065. Derivation

And if the lifetime were uniform on 0 to 4? Given 3 years survived, two more is impossible, so the answer is 0 rather than 0.6065. That is the whole content of the memorylessness assumption.

Example 6 — the arrival list on the canvas

Scene 1 draws twenty arrivals over ten units of time, listed explicitly in the lesson's model rather than generated at random, so the same frame is drawn on every replay. The rate is 20/10 = 2 per unit, so the mean gap is about 0.5; measured on the list it is 0.4947, which is the span 9.4 over the nineteen gaps. The longest gap is 0.8, and it occurs twice.

The point of the picture is not the specific list. It is that one list answers both questions: count the ticks inside a window and you are doing the Poisson question; measure between two ticks and you are doing the exponential one. The two questions are asked of the same picture, which is why the two distributions share a parameter.

Boundaries, counterexamples and the condition each one violates

Each card names the condition that fails, not merely the answer that changes. A worked example is not a proof, and a picture is not a proof; the numbers below are computed by the lesson's own model and re-checked against a second route.

Counterexample 1 — a non-memoryless continuous law

Let X be uniform on 0 to 10. Then P(X > 5) = 0.5, but P(X > 10 | X > 5) = 0 / 0.5 = 0.

Condition violated: constant hazard. The uniform has a hazard that jumps to infinity at 10, so surviving changes the future.

A fixed wait of 10 fails in the other direction: P(X > 5) = 1 while P(X > 10 | X > 5) = 0.

Counterexample 2 — a mean gap that is not a mean wait

Gaps of 1 minute nine times in ten and 91 minutes once in ten. The mean gap is 0.9 + 9.1 = 10. The second moment is 0.9 + 828.1 = 829.

An arrival at a random time waits E[X²] / (2E[X]) = 829 / 20 = 41.45 on average — more than four times the mean gap.

Condition violated: memorylessness. For exponential gaps of mean 10 the same formula gives 200/20 = 10, and for fixed gaps of 10 it gives 100/20 = 5. One mean gap, three answers.

Counterexample 3 — counts whose variance is not the mean

Ten one-minute windows with counts 0, 0, 3, 0, 0, 4, 0, 0, 0, 3. The mean is 1 and the variance is 2.4.

Condition violated: a constant rate. Bursty arrivals put mass on 3 and 4, where a Poisson(1) model places almost none — P(X ≥ 3) at λ = 1 is 0.0803 against the observed 3 windows in 10.

Over-dispersion, variance above the mean, is the standard diagnostic. The Poisson is not "the distribution of counts"; it is the distribution of counts from a stable rate.

Edge case — the two different thresholds

At λ = 2, P(X > the mean, 0.5) = e⁻¹ = 0.3679, but P(X > the parameter, 2) = e⁻⁴ = 0.0183.

A question that says "exceeds the expected lifetime" wants the first. A question that says "exceeds 2 units" with parameter 2 wants the second. They differ by a factor of twenty.

Edge case — zero events, and the limit at zero

P(X = 0) = e^(−λ) is the largest single mass when λ is small, and it is the answer to every "none happened" question.

As λ → 0 the Poisson puts all its mass at 0 and the exponential mean 1/λ → ∞. As λ grows, the Poisson mass at 0 falls below any threshold; at λ = 15 it is 3.06 × 10⁻⁷. Both limits are finite and well defined, which is why no question in this topic is ever "undefined" except through a division by zero.

Closely confused pair 1 — Poisson against binomial

The binomial needs a fixed number of trials n. The Poisson has no n: it counts over a continuous window with no ceiling.

Poisson is legitimate as an approximation when n is large and p small with np moderate. The page measures the error: 0.0428 at n = 10, 0.000337 at n = 1000.

The distinction that matters in an exam: if the question gives n and p, the exact answer is binomial and the Poisson is an approximation; if the question gives a rate per unit time, there is no n and the Poisson is the model.

Closely confused pair 2 — exponential against geometric

Both are memoryless; one is continuous and one is discrete. The geometric counts the number of trials until the first success, with P(X > n) = (1−p)ⁿ. The page checks at p = 0.3: P(X > 8 | X > 5) = 0.7³ = 0.343, exactly P(X > 3).

The distinction that matters: a wait measured in whole trials is geometric; a wait measured in time is exponential. Putting a discrete wait into e^(−λx) is the error, and so is putting a continuous wait into (1−p)ⁿ.

"λ = 2, so the mean is 2"

Why it tempts: in the Poisson, λ is the mean, so the habit transfers. But in the exponential λ is a rate and the mean is 1/λ.

The minimal correction: at λ = 2 the mean is 0.5. P(X > 2) = e⁻⁴ = 0.0183 while P(X > 0.5) = e⁻¹ = 0.3679.

The rule: in the Poisson λ is a count per window; in the exponential λ is a count per unit time and the mean is its reciprocal.

"Poisson is the distribution of counts"

Why it tempts: every textbook example is a count — cars, calls, misprints.

The minimal counterexample: the ten windows with counts 0, 0, 3, 0, 0, 4, 0, 0, 0, 3 have mean 1 and variance 2.4. They are counts and they are not Poisson.

The rule: the Poisson is the distribution of counts from a constant, independent rate. Check variance against mean before using it.

"The second moment is the square of the mean"

Why it tempts: the two are equal for a constant, and E[X]² is the only square in sight when a question asks for E[(X+2)²].

The minimal correction: at λ = 5, E[X²] = 30 and (E[X])² = 25. The gap is the variance.

The rule: E[X²] = Var(X) + (E[X])², always, for any variable with a finite second moment.

"Memorylessness means the distribution has no memory of anything"

Why it tempts: the name invites a vague reading, and the property looks like an absence rather than a shape.

The precise reading: the conditional tail from s is the same function as the unconditional tail, shifted. The distribution still has a parameter and still has a mean; what it loses is any dependence on elapsed time.

The rule: P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t). Test it with two numbers; do not argue from the name.

"Mean gap 10, so I wait 10 on average"

Why it tempts: it is true for the exponential, which is the distribution most questions use, so the habit is never punished.

The minimal counterexample: gaps of 1 nine times in ten and 91 once in ten have mean 10 and a mean wait of 41.45. Fixed gaps of 10 have a mean wait of 5.

The rule: the mean wait is E[X²]/(2E[X]), which equals the mean gap only when the gaps are exponential.

"Fewer than 3 and at most 3 are the same kind of question"

Why it tempts: both are cumulative, and under time pressure the boundary term is the first thing dropped.

The minimal correction: at λ = 3, P(p < 3) = 0.423190 and P(p ≤ 3) = 0.647232. Both appear as options in the 2013 paper.

The rule: write the index set before computing — {0,1,2} against {0,1,2,3} — and only then add terms.

What the paper actually asks, and the reasoning each kind demands

Six patterns cover every question this topic has produced in the papers the research reached. Each card gives the reasoning the pattern requires and the wrong approach it invites. Nothing here predicts 2027; the patterns are a description of past papers.

Pattern 1 — a cumulative Poisson probability

Shape: a rate, a window, and "fewer than k" or "at least k".

Reasoning demanded: convert to λ, write the index set, add the masses. Answers are often left in the form c/(2e³) rather than decimal.

Wrong approach: computing one mass and stopping, or including the boundary term. Seen in GATE CSE 2013 Q2.

Pattern 2 — a moment from mean = variance

Shape: "Poisson with mean 5, find E[(X+2)²]" or "find the standard deviation".

Reasoning demanded: Var = mean, so E[X²] = λ + λ², then linearity. For a spread, take the square root of the mean.

Wrong approach: using λ² as the second moment, or reporting the variance where the standard deviation was asked. Seen in GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 Q48 and 2016 Set 1 Q4.

Pattern 3 — an exponential tail at a named point

Shape: a density with parameter λ and a threshold, often the mean itself.

Reasoning demanded: P(X > x) = e^(−λx). If the threshold is the mean, the answer is e⁻¹ and the parameter is irrelevant.

Wrong approach: treating the parameter as the mean and evaluating e^(−λ·λ). Seen in GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q18.

Pattern 4 — the unit conversion

Shape: a rate per hour, a window in minutes, a question about a count.

Reasoning demanded: λ = rate × window, in matching units, before anything else.

Wrong approach: using the rate as λ. Seen in GATE IT 2007 Q57, and in university papers on the same question.

Pattern 5 — a statement set (MSQ)

Shape: four statements about the two distributions, of which some are true.

Reasoning demanded: check each against a definition. The true statements are usually "mean equals variance" for Poisson, "memoryless" for exponential, and "Poisson is a limit of the binomial". The false ones are usually "λ is the mean" for the exponential and "the exponential is symmetric".

Wrong approach: treating a familiar-sounding statement as true. Each one has to be tested.

Pattern 6 — the two-direction question

Shape: a rate, and a question that could be about a count or a wait, phrased to see whether you noticed which.

Reasoning demanded: decide from the words. "How many" is Poisson; "how long", "exceeds", "lasts at least" is exponential.

Wrong approach: matching the word "Poisson" in the stem and ignoring that the question asks for a time. The two distributions share λ, which is exactly why the words have to be read.

MCQGATE CSE 2013, Q2

Suppose p is the number of cars per minute passing through a certain road junction between 5 PM and 6 PM, and p has a Poisson distribution with mean 3. What is the probability of observing fewer than 3 cars during any given minute in this interval?

  1. 8/(2e³)
  2. 9/(2e³)
  3. 17/(2e³)
  4. 26/(2e³)

Answer: C — 17/(2e³) = 0.423190

Fewer than three is k = 0, 1, 2. The masses are e⁻³, 3e⁻³ and (9/2)e⁻³, summing to (1 + 3 + 4.5)e⁻³ = 8.5e⁻³ = 17/(2e³).

Why A and B are wrong. They are single terms of the sum, reported alone. Why D is wrong. It corresponds to including k = 3, which gives P(p ≤ 3) = 0.647232, and then rescaling.

This is a CSE paper item. Some catalogues file it as Q41; the paper and the question text are confirmed, the number is a catalogue's filing.

NATGATE CSE 2021, Set 1, Q18

The lifetime of a component of a certain type is a random variable whose probability density function is exponentially distributed with parameter 2. For a randomly picked component of this type, the probability that its lifetime exceeds the expected lifetime (rounded to 2 decimal places) is ______.

Answer: 0.37

The mean is 1/2 = 0.5, so the question is P(X > 0.5) = e^(−2 × 0.5) = e⁻¹ = 0.367879, which rounds to 0.37.

The shortcut, with its assumption. P(X > E[X]) = e^(−λ · 1/λ) = e⁻¹ for every λ, so the parameter can be ignored. The assumption is that the question asks about the mean and not about the parameter — here it says "expected lifetime", so the shortcut is safe.

The official key accepts 0.35 to 0.39. This is a CSE paper item.

NATGATE CSE 2017, Set 2, Q48

If a random variable X has a Poisson distribution with mean 5, then the expectation E[(X + 2)²] equals ______.

Answer: 54

Var(X) = 5, so E[X²] = 5 + 25 = 30. Then E[X² + 4X + 4] = 30 + 20 + 4 = 54.

Why 49 appears. It uses (E[X])² = 25 as the second moment. The gap between 49 and 54 is exactly the variance, which is the quantity the Poisson supplies for free.

Official key 54, no tolerance. This is a CSE paper item.

MCQGATE CSE 2016, Set 1, Q4

Consider a Poisson distribution for the tossing of a biased coin. The mean for this distribution is μ. The standard deviation for this distribution is given by

  1. √μ
  2. μ²
  3. μ
  4. 1/μ

Answer: A — √μ

The variance of a Poisson variable equals its mean, so the variance is μ and the standard deviation is √μ.

Why C appears. It is the variance, reported where the standard deviation was asked — the commonest single slip in this topic. Why B appears. It is the square, the wrong direction. Why D appears. It is the reciprocal, which is the exponential's mean given a rate.

This is a CSE paper item.

OriginalNAT

A server receives requests at a rate of 12 per minute, following a Poisson process. What is the probability that the gap between two consecutive requests exceeds 10 seconds? Give the answer to 3 decimal places.

Answer: 0.135

The rate is 12 per minute, which is 0.2 per second. A gap of 10 seconds is a wait, so the exponential tail applies: e^(−0.2 × 10) = e⁻² = 0.135335.

The trap. Using λ = 12 with x = 10 gives e⁻¹²⁰, which is astronomically small. The rate must be expressed per second before it meets a wait in seconds. Equivalently, work in minutes: 10 seconds is 1/6 minute and e^(−12/6) = e⁻².

OriginalMSQ

X is exponential with rate λ > 0. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. P(X > E[X]) does not depend on λ
  2. E[X] = λ
  3. Var(X) = 1/λ²
  4. P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t) for all s, t ≥ 0

Answer: A, C and D

A is true: P(X > 1/λ) = e⁻¹ for every λ. C is true: the variance of an exponential is 1/λ², the square of the mean. D is the memorylessness property.

Why B is false. It is the single most common error in the topic: E[X] = 1/λ, not λ. The statement is true for the Poisson and false for the exponential, which is exactly what the option is testing.

OriginalNAT

A component's lifetime is exponential with mean 4 years. It has already operated for 3 years without failing. What is the probability that it operates for at least 2 more years? Give the answer to 3 decimal places.

Answer: 0.607

λ = 1/4. By memorylessness the answer is P(X > 2) = e^(−0.5) = 0.606531.

The long way: P(X > 5 | X > 3) = e^(−1.25)/e^(−0.75) = 0.286505/0.472367 = 0.606531.

The trap. Computing P(X > 5) = 0.2865 and stopping. That is the probability of lasting five years from new, not two more years from now.

OriginalMCQ

Over ten one-minute windows, a counter records 0, 0, 3, 0, 0, 4, 0, 0, 0, 3 events. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. The counts are Poisson with λ = 1, since the mean is 1
  2. The counts are Poisson with λ = 2.4, since the variance is 2.4
  3. The counts are over-dispersed, so a Poisson model is not supported
  4. No conclusion is possible from ten observations

Answer: C

The mean is 1.0 and the variance is 2.4. A Poisson variable has variance equal to its mean, so the sample contradicts the model.

Why A and B are wrong. Both pick one of the two sample moments and ignore the other; the test is precisely the comparison between them. Why D is wrong. Ten observations are few, and a formal test would want more, but the qualitative conclusion — variance well above mean, arrivals clustering — is exactly what the comparison is for.

The one-screen revision sheet

Everything on this page is either a definition or a consequence of one. If you can rebuild the right-hand column from the left, you can answer every question in the topic.

Poisson — the count

P(X = k) = e^(−λ) λ^k / k!, k = 0, 1, 2, …

E[X] = λ  ·  Var(X) = λ  ·  SD = √λ

E[X²] = λ + λ²  ·  E[(X+a)²] = λ + λ² + 2aλ + a²

P(X = 0) = e^(−λ)  ·  P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − e^(−λ)

Sum of independent Poissons is Poisson with the rates added.

Exponential — the wait

f(x) = λ e^(−λx), x ≥ 0

E[X] = 1/λ  ·  Var(X) = 1/λ²  ·  SD = 1/λ

P(X > x) = e^(−λx)  ·  P(a < X < b) = e^(−λa) − e^(−λb)

P(X > E[X]) = e⁻¹ = 0.367879, for every λ

P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t) — memoryless.

The four-step procedure

  1. Count or wait? "how many" → Poisson; "how long", "exceeds", "lasts at least" → exponential.
  2. Convert the rate. λ = rate × window, in matching units. 20 per hour over 45 min is 15.
  3. Read the inequality. "Fewer than k" stops at k−1. "At least one" is 1 − P(0).
  4. For a spread or a second moment, use variance = mean, and take the square root for the standard deviation.

Six traps, one line each

  • In the exponential, λ is a rate; the mean is 1/λ.
  • The Poisson's variance equals its mean — check it before fitting.
  • E[X²] is not (E[X])²; the gap is the variance.
  • The standard deviation is √λ, not λ.
  • A mean gap is not a mean wait unless the gaps are exponential.
  • P(X > the mean) and P(X > the parameter) are different questions.

Quick self-check, no working shown

If you can answer these four in under a minute each, the topic is in hand. The answers are on the Practice tab and in the transcript.

(a) Calls arrive at 5 per hour. Probability of exactly 2 in 90 minutes? (b) X exponential with mean 3; P(X > 6)? (c) Poisson with mean 4; the standard deviation? (d) Gaps of 2 minutes exactly, ten in a row; the mean wait for a random arrival?

Answers: (a) λ = 7.5, so e^(−7.5) 7.5²/2 = 0.0156. (b) λ = 1/3, so e⁻² = 0.1353. (c) √4 = 2. (d) half of 2, which is 1 — not 2, because fixed gaps are not memoryless.
