Topic and objective

TOPIC: The differential mean value theorem as an exam object. Rolle's theorem, Lagrange's mean value theorem, the exact hypothesis each needs, what the guaranteed point does and does not license, and the three uses that questions actually test: finding the point, bounding a change, and counting zeros. This is the phrase Mean value theorem in the Calculus clause of the GATE 2027 CS syllabus.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a function and an interval, decide whether the theorem applies, name what it guarantees, compute the point when it is asked for, and bound a change from the range of the derivative — without claiming a uniqueness or a location the theorem never promised.

I read the topic as one equation, three uses. The equation is that the derivative at some interior point equals the chord slope. Every number on the page is computed here and substituted back into the equation it came from.

Prerequisites

  • Limits, continuity and differentiation — what a derivative is, and what continuity at a point excludes.
  • Maxima and minima — why a zero derivative is a candidate extremum, and why it is not a certificate.
  • The mean value theorem for integrals — the differently named theorem that is most often confused with this one.

Approximate learning time: 45 to 65 minutes including the practice set. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. Some interior point has derivative equal to the chord slope. That is the whole theorem.
  2. Continuity on the closed interval, differentiability only on the open one. Endpoints are exempt.
  3. Existence, not uniqueness: two points, or every point, is a perfectly normal outcome.
  4. Multiplied out, the conclusion is a bound: change equals a derivative value times the width.
  5. Rolle read backwards caps the zeros of a function by the zeros of its derivative.

Intuition first: the average slope must happen somewhere

Drive from one town to another, 120 km apart, in two hours. Your average speed is 60 km/h. Even if you stopped for tea and raced on the highway, at some instant your speedometer read exactly 60. That is the mean value theorem: a smooth trip cannot avoid its own average.

Educational interpretation The car image is mine and it is deliberately loose: it hides the requirement that the position be differentiable, which is exactly the condition a teleporting car would violate. The theorem is about functions whose graph has a tangent at every interior point, and the page tests that condition rather than assuming it.

Formal statements

Lagrange's mean value theorem. If f is continuous on the closed interval from a to b and differentiable on the open interval from a to b, then there exists at least one c with a < c < b such that f prime of c equals f of b minus f of a, divided by b minus a.

Rolle's theorem. Under the same two hypotheses, and with f of a equal to f of b, there exists at least one c with a < c < b such that f prime of c equals 0.

Cauchy's mean value theorem. If f and g are continuous on the closed interval and differentiable inside, and g prime is never 0 inside, then some interior c satisfies f prime of c over g prime of c equals the ratio of the two changes.

The zero-derivative consequence. If f prime is 0 at every point of an interval and f is continuous there, then f is constant on that interval.

Symbols, in words

  • closed interval from a to b — every point between the two ends, the ends included. Continuity is demanded here.
  • open interval from a to b — the points strictly between the ends. Differentiability is demanded here.
  • f prime of c — the slope of the tangent at the point c.
  • the chord slope — rise over run between the two ends; the average rate of change.
  • c — a point the theorem produces. It is never a value you were given, and it is never at an end.
  • g prime never 0 — the condition that makes Cauchy's ratio defined; without it the denominator of the ratio can vanish.

The assumptions, and what each one is for

Continuity at the endpoints is needed because the chord is built from the endpoint values. Move one endpoint value and the chord changes, so the point that matched it can disappear.

Differentiability inside is needed because the conclusion names a derivative at an interior point. One corner is enough to destroy it, as absolute x on minus 1 to 1 shows.

An interval, for the constant theorem is needed so that any two points can be joined inside the domain. A domain in two pieces cannot be joined, and there a zero derivative does not force a constant.

Why Rolle implies the general theorem, in one line of algebra

Let s be the chord slope and define g of x as f of x minus f of a minus s times x minus a. Then g of a is 0, and g of b is f of b minus f of a minus s times b minus a, which is also 0 because s is exactly that quotient. So g has level ends, and Rolle gives an interior point where g prime is 0. But g prime is f prime minus s, so f prime equals s there. Rolle is therefore not a separate result to memorise: it is the theorem with the chord removed, and the page checks the same point arises both ways on x squared over 0 to 2.

Five worked examples, every number computed

Each example below is solved by the model on this page: the guaranteed point is found by bisection on the equation the theorem names, then substituted back into that equation. The residue printed on the Animation tab is the difference between the two sides.

Example 1 — the chord and the point

Question. Apply Lagrange's mean value theorem to x squared on the interval from 0 to 2 and find c.

Step 1, hypotheses. A polynomial: continuous on the closed interval, differentiable inside. Met.

Step 2, the chord slope. f of 2 is 4 and f of 0 is 0, so the slope is 4 over 2, which is 2.

Step 3, solve. f prime is 2x, so 2c equals 2 and c equals 1, which is inside.

Step 4, check the picture. The tangent at c is y equals 2x minus 1, with value 1 at x equals 1, where the curve is also 1. The chord has value 2 there, so the two lines are parallel and distinct.

Example 2 — Rolle, twice over

Question. Apply Rolle to x squared minus 4x plus 3 on 1 to 3, and verify it by the auxiliary function.

Step 1, level ends. f of 1 is 1 minus 4 plus 3, which is 0. f of 3 is 9 minus 12 plus 3, also 0.

Step 2, solve. f prime is 2x minus 4, so c equals 2, and f of 2 is minus 1, the vertex.

Step 3, the second route. On x squared over 0 to 2 the chord is 2x, so the auxiliary function is x squared minus 2x. It is 0 at both ends and its derivative 2x minus 2 vanishes at 1, which is the same point Example 1 gave.

Example 3 — three hypothesis tests

Absolute x on minus 1 to 1. Continuous, level ends, chord slope 0. The derivative is minus 1 on the left and plus 1 on the right, and does not exist at 0. No interior point has derivative 0, so the conclusion fails — the hypothesis that fails is differentiability.

x below 1 and 0 at 1, on 0 to 1. Both end values are 0, so the chord is flat, but the derivative is 1 at every interior point. The hypothesis that fails is continuity, and it fails at the endpoint where continuity was demanded.

Root x on 0 to 1. The chord slope is 1 and 1 over 2 root c equals 1 gives c equals 0.25. The derivative is not finite at 0, yet the theorem applies: differentiability at an endpoint is not required.

Example 4 — two points, and then all of them

x cubed minus x on minus 1 to 1. Both end values are 0. The derivative is 3 x squared minus 1, zero at plus and minus 1 over root 3, that is plus and minus 0.577350. Both are inside, so the answer is a pair of points.

The identity on 0 to 1. The chord slope is 1 and the derivative is 1 everywhere, so every interior point is admissible. Comparing the two functions is the fastest way to see that the theorem controls existence only.

Example 5 — a bound and a count

The bound. On 100 to 101, root 101 minus 10 equals 1 over 2 root c for some interior c. That derivative lies between 1 over 2 root 101 and 1 over 20, and combining the two inequalities puts root 101 between 10.049751 and 10.05, while the true value is 10.049876.

The count. x cubed minus 3x plus 1 has zeros at minus 1.879385, 0.347296 and 1.532089, located by bisection. Its derivative 3 x squared minus 3 vanishes at minus 1 and plus 1, one in each gap.

The consequence. Since the derivative has exactly two zeros, the function can have at most three, and it has three. Rolle read backwards caps the zeros of a polynomial by its degree.

Where the standard recipe stops working

Each case is a boundary of the theorem, not a curiosity. A question sitting on one of them is testing whether the hypotheses were read or the formula was memorised.

A corner inside the interval

One interior point of non-differentiability is enough. Absolute x on minus 1 to 1 is continuous everywhere and differentiable everywhere except at 0, and there is no point with derivative 0.

Contrast: root x on 0 to 1 is not differentiable at an endpoint and the conclusion still holds. The position of the failure is what matters, not its existence.

A derivative that is unbounded

The theorem does not require the derivative to be bounded or continuous, only to exist inside. Root x on 0 to 1 has a derivative that grows without bound near 0, and the guaranteed point is a perfectly ordinary interior number.

What boundedness gives you is the bound form: a change no larger than the maximum derivative times the width. Without a bound the theorem still holds; only the estimate is lost.

More than one admissible point

x cubed minus x on minus 1 to 1 has two, at plus and minus 0.577350. The identity on 0 to 1 has every interior point. A numerical solver that stops at the first sign change reports one and is incomplete.

The remedy costs one line: after solving, count the solutions that lie strictly inside the interval, and report the count as part of the answer.

A domain that is not an interval

The constant theorem needs an interval. On the domain with 0 removed, the function that is 0 for negative x and 1 for positive x has derivative 0 everywhere and is not constant.

Every use of the theorem on a pair of points implicitly joins them by an interval inside the domain. When that interval is missing, the conclusion is missing too.

Cauchy's ratio with a vanishing denominator

The two-function version needs the second derivative to be non-zero inside; otherwise the ratio of derivatives is undefined at the very point the theorem produces.

The condition is also what makes the proof work: it is the hypothesis that lets the auxiliary determinant function satisfy Rolle. Dropping it is not a technicality but a hole in the statement.

The counterexample that matters most

Absolute x on minus 1 to 1 satisfies continuity, has level ends, and has a flat chord — two of the three ingredients of Rolle. It fails only differentiability at the single point 0, and the conclusion is false.

Any argument of the form "the picture looks flat at the bottom, so the derivative must vanish somewhere" fails here: the bottom is a corner, and a corner has no derivative at all.

Six beliefs that produce a wrong option

Each entry states the belief, says why it is wrong, and gives the smallest case that refutes it. Every number is computed on this page.

"The tangent at c is the chord"

Why it is wrong. The theorem makes the tangent parallel to the chord. A tangent equal to the chord would mean the curve is the chord, which it is not unless the function is linear.

Counterexample. x squared on 0 to 2: at c equals 1 the tangent is 2x minus 1 with value 1, while the chord has value 2.

"Differentiability is needed at the endpoints too"

Why it is wrong. The hypothesis is differentiability on the open interval only, because the conclusion names only an interior point. Rejecting an example on an endpoint throws away a valid application.

Counterexample. Root x on 0 to 1 is not differentiable at 0, and the theorem gives c equals 0.25.

"The point c is unique"

Why it is wrong. The statement is an existence statement. Uniqueness is a property of the particular function, and it must be shown separately.

Counterexample. x cubed minus x on minus 1 to 1 gives two points, plus and minus 0.577350.

"A zero derivative means a maximum or a minimum"

Why it is wrong. A zero derivative is a candidate. What decides is the sign change of the derivative across the point, which the theorem says nothing about.

Counterexample. x cubed is strictly increasing and its derivative is 0 at the origin.

"The mean value theorem and the mean value theorem for integrals are the same"

Why it is wrong. The first matches a derivative to a chord slope; the second matches a function value to an average value. The equations differ, so the points differ.

Counterexample. On x squared over 0 to 2 the first point is 1 and the second is 1.154701.

"Cauchy's theorem is the ordinary one applied twice"

Why it is wrong. The two applications produce two points that need not coincide, and a ratio of derivatives requires one common point.

Counterexample. x squared against x cubed on 1 to 2: separately 1.5 and 1.527525, together 1.555556.

How the topic is examined

What follows describes question shapes this topic supports, written from the syllabus wording and the structure of the material. No question below is presented as an official previous-year item, and no year, paper or question number is claimed. The practice set is labelled original for the same reason.

Pattern 1 — find the point

The item gives a function and an interval and asks for c, or for the number of admissible points. The work is the chord slope, then the equation f prime of c equals it.

First move. Write the chord slope as a fraction, not a decimal, so the equation for c stays exact. Then count the solutions inside.

Pattern 2 — does the theorem apply?

The item gives a piecewise function or one with a corner and asks which conclusion follows. The answer is decided by the hypotheses, before any computation.

First move. Locate the discontinuity or corner and check whether it is at an endpoint or inside. Inside destroys the conclusion; at an endpoint it may not matter.

Pattern 3 — bound a change

The item gives a derivative bound and asks for a bound on a function value, or gives two values and asks for a bound on the derivative somewhere. The work is one multiplication.

First move. Write the change as the derivative times the width, then use the smallest and largest the derivative can be. Two-sided bounds need the monotonicity of the derivative.

Pattern 4 — count zeros

The item says a function has k zeros and asks the least number its derivative must have, or the reverse. Rolle connects the two counts, one gap at a time.

First move. Decide the direction: function zeros give a lower bound on derivative zeros; derivative zeros give an upper bound on function zeros.

Pattern 5 — which theorem is being quoted

The item offers four conclusions, each true of a different theorem: a slope point, a height point, a value point, a derivative-value point. Only one matches the equation it names.

First move. Read the quantity on the left of the equality. A derivative means this theorem; a function value means the integral version or the intermediate value theorem.

What a wrong option usually is

In this topic the distractors are systematic: a point at an endpoint, a unique point where two exist, the integral-mean point offered for the differential theorem, the tangent confused with the chord, and a conclusion drawn from a function with a corner.

Use. Any option naming c equal to a or to b is wrong immediately, because the theorem places c strictly between them.

NATOriginal practice question

For the function x cubed minus x on the interval from minus 1 to 1, the positive value of c guaranteed by Rolle's theorem is _______. Give three decimal places.

Answer: 0.577

Both end values are 0, so Rolle applies. The derivative is 3 x squared minus 1, which is zero at c equal to plus or minus 1 over root 3, about plus or minus 0.577350. The positive one is asked for.

Why 0.333 appears. That is the value of c squared, with the square root left out. Why 1 appears. That is the endpoint, where the theorem places nothing. Why 1.732 appears. That is root 3 itself, the reciprocal being forgotten.

The page locates both points and checks that the derivative vanishes at each to twelve places.

MCQOriginal practice question

For the function root x on the interval from 0 to 1, which statement is correct?

  1. The theorem does not apply, because the function is not differentiable at 0
  2. The theorem applies and the guaranteed point is c = 1/4
  3. The theorem applies and the guaranteed point is c = 1/2
  4. The theorem applies and the guaranteed point is c = 3/4

Answer: B

The function is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable on the open interval, so the theorem applies. The chord slope is 1, and 1 over 2 root c equals 1 gives root c equals 1/2, so c equals 1/4.

Why A is wrong. Differentiability at an endpoint is not a hypothesis; the conclusion never names an endpoint. Why C is wrong. c equals 1/2 solves root c equals 1 over root 2, which is the chord slope of root x on 0 to 1/2, a different interval. Why D is wrong. It is the midpoint of the interval, offered because midpoints feel plausible.

The page checks that the derivative at 1/4 equals the chord slope, and that the derivative at the endpoint grows without bound.

MSQOriginal practice question

For the function absolute x on the interval from minus 1 to 1, which of the following are true?

  1. The function is continuous on the closed interval
  2. The function is differentiable at every interior point
  3. The two end values are equal
  4. The conclusion of Rolle's theorem holds for this function

Answer: A and C

The function is continuous everywhere, both end values are 1, and the chord is therefore flat. But the derivative is minus 1 on the left half and plus 1 on the right half, and does not exist at 0, so no interior point has derivative 0.

Why B is wrong. The corner at 0 is an interior point where the derivative does not exist. Why D is wrong. With the conclusion false, at least one hypothesis must fail, and the one that fails is differentiability inside.

This is the closest counterexample pair in the topic: two of Rolle's three ingredients hold, and the third is exactly the one the picture hides.

NATOriginal practice question

Applying the mean value theorem to root x on the interval from 100 to 101, the best upper bound it gives for root 101 minus 10 is _______. Give six decimal places.

Answer: 0.050000

The change equals 1 over 2 root c for some c between 100 and 101. The derivative decreases, so the change is less than 1 over 2 root 100, which is 1 over 20, that is 0.05. The true change is 0.049876, so the bound is close but not attained.

Why 0.049876 appears. That is the exact value, which the theorem does not supply. Why 0.000050 appears. That is the change divided by the width, that is the derivative, not the change. Why 0.002500 appears. That is the square of 0.05, from squaring the width by mistake.

The page also computes the matching lower bound 10.049751 and checks that the true value lies strictly between the two.

MCQOriginal practice question

For f of x equal to x squared and g of x equal to x cubed on the interval from 1 to 2, the point c guaranteed by Cauchy's mean value theorem is

  1. 1.5
  2. 14/9
  3. the square root of 7/3
  4. 5/3

Answer: B

The changes are 3 and 7, so the ratio is 3 over 7. The ratio of derivatives is 2c over 3 c squared, which is 2 over 3c. Setting 2 over 3c equal to 3 over 7 gives c equals 14 over 9, about 1.555556.

Why A is wrong. 1.5 is the point from applying the ordinary theorem to x squared alone. Why C is wrong. That is the point from applying it to x cubed alone, about 1.527525. Why D is wrong. 5/3 is the midpoint of the interval, a plausible-looking number with no derivation.

This is the closely confused pair of the topic: three different points, one for each of three different theorems, and only one answers the question asked.

NATOriginal practice question

A function is differentiable on the whole real line and has exactly four distinct real zeros. The least number of distinct real zeros its derivative must have is _______.

Answer: 3

Four zeros create three gaps between consecutive zeros. On each gap the function has equal end values, so Rolle gives an interior point where the derivative is zero. The three points lie in three disjoint intervals, so they are distinct.

Why 4 appears. The count of the function's zeros is not the count of the derivative's. Why 2 appears. That is the number of gaps in a three-zero function, one step behind. Why 1 appears. That is what a single application of Rolle gives, forgetting that each gap is a separate interval.

The page checks the same logic on x cubed minus 3x plus 1: three zeros, two gaps, and exactly two derivative zeros, one in each gap.

One page to revise from

The statements with their hypotheses, the three uses, the numbers this lesson established, and the traps — in the order you would use them under exam pressure.

The statements, with their conditions

Lagrange. Continuous on the closed interval, differentiable inside: some interior c has f prime of c equal to the chord slope.

Rolle. The same, plus equal end values: some interior c has f prime of c equal to 0.

Cauchy. The same for two functions, with the second derivative non-zero inside: some interior c has the ratio of derivatives equal to the ratio of changes.

Constant theorem. Zero derivative at every point of an interval: the function is constant there.

Monotonicity. Positive derivative on an interval forces a strict increase; the converse fails.

The three uses

  1. Find the point. Compute the chord slope, solve f prime of c equal to it, keep the solutions strictly inside, report how many there are.
  2. Bound a change. Write the change as a derivative value times the width, then use the smallest and largest the derivative can take.
  3. Count zeros. Function zeros force derivative zeros between them; derivative zeros cap the number of function zeros.

The numbers to remember from this page

x squared on 0 to 2: chord slope 2, point 1, tangent 2x minus 1.

x squared minus 4x plus 3 on 1 to 3: level ends, point 2, vertex value minus 1.

Absolute x on minus 1 to 1: derivative plus or minus 1, never 0. Root x on 0 to 1: point 0.25.

x cubed minus x on minus 1 to 1: two points, plus and minus 0.577350.

Sine over 0.3 to 2.1: change 0.567689, point 1.249936. Exponential on 0 to 1: change 1.718282, point 0.541325.

Root 101: between 10.049751 and 10.05, true value 10.049876.

Cauchy on 1 to 2: ratio 3 over 7, point 14 over 9, against 1.5 and 1.527525 separately.

x cubed minus 3x plus 1: zeros at minus 1.879385, 0.347296, 1.532089; derivative zeros at minus 1 and 1.

The five traps, in one line each

The tangent is parallel, not equal. Endpoints need continuity but not differentiability. One point is guaranteed, not a unique one. A zero derivative is a candidate, not an extremum. The integral version matches a height, not a slope.
