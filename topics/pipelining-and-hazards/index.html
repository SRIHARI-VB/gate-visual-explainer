Topic and objective

TOPIC: Instruction pipelining and pipeline hazards — the last sentence of GATE 2027 Section 3: "Instruction pipelining, pipeline hazards."

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a list of stage delays and a latch delay, produce the clock period. Given a stage count and an instruction count, produce the cycle count. Given an instruction mix with stall fractions, produce the effective CPI and the speedup over a non-pipelined design — carrying the clock frequency with it. Given an instruction sequence, name each dependence as read-after-write, write-after-read or write-after-write, and say how many cycles it costs with and without forwarding. Given a multi-cycle functional unit, count its structural stalls. Given the stage in which a branch resolves, produce the branch penalty.

I read this topic as one arithmetic sitting on one picture. The picture is the cycle diagram: instructions down the side, cycles across the top, stages on the diagonal. The arithmetic is five numbers — the beat, the cycle count, the stalls, the unit conversion and the ratio. Everything the exam asks here is one of those five, asked in a different costume. The datapath and control unit this pipeline is built from live in ALU and Control Unit Design; the gates and flip-flops live in Combinational Circuits and Sequential Circuits.

Prerequisites

Three published lessons carry the machinery:

  • ALU and Control Unit Design — the datapath, the control steps and the clock period. A pipeline is that machine with the steps overlapped.
  • Sequential Circuits — the inter-stage latch is a register, and its setup and hold times are why the beat is more than the largest stage delay.
  • Number Representation and Arithmetic — only for the observation that a multiply can occupy the ALU for more than one cycle, which is the structural-hazard scene.

The only extra mathematics is a ceiling, ceil(t), used once: a unit that takes t clock periods occupies ceil(t) cycles. Scene 4 derives it and the page checks it on the 2026 table.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. A pipeline does not shorten an instruction. It shortens the gap between instructions.
  2. The beat is the largest stage delay plus one latch delay. Nothing else sets it.
  3. Cycles are k + n − 1 for n given instructions, or n × CPI for a statistical program. Pick the right one.
  4. Forwarding removes arithmetic stalls, leaves exactly one after a load, and does nothing for a branch.
  5. Speedup is a ratio of times, so the clock frequency must be carried. A stage count alone is not an answer.

Intuition: the laundry, not the washing

A laundry has four machines: wash, dry, iron, fold. One basket of clothes goes through all four. If one person does every step for one basket before starting the next, four baskets take sixteen units of time. If the machines run at once on different baskets, the first basket still takes four units — but a basket comes out of the folding machine every unit after that. Four baskets take seven units, not sixteen.

Three facts about the laundry are the three facts about a pipeline, and they are easy to get wrong. First, the first basket is no faster at all. Overlap cannot help the first item through an empty line. Second, the slowest machine sets the pace: if ironing takes twice as long as washing, the whole line runs at ironing speed and the washing machine idles half of every cycle. Third, the baskets interfere: if two baskets need the iron at once, one must wait, even though no basket is holding another's shirt.

Educational interpretation The analogy is a teaching device, not a model of a real processor. It is chosen because it keeps the three separate questions separate: how long one item takes, how often one finishes, and what happens when two items want the same machine.

Latency and throughput are different numbers

Latency is the time from starting one instruction to finishing it. A k-stage pipeline with period T has latency k × T, exactly as the non-pipelined machine does. Pipelining does not touch it.

Throughput is the number of instructions finished per unit time. Non-pipelined, that is 1 / (k × T). Pipelined and full, it is 1 / T. The improvement is a factor of k, and it applies only once the pipeline is full.

Speedup is the ratio of two total execution times, not a property of one instruction. That is why it depends on how many instructions you run, and why it is always below k.

Why the count is k + n − 1

Instruction 1 enters stage 1 at cycle 1 and leaves stage k at cycle k. Instruction 2 enters at cycle 2 and leaves at cycle k + 1. Instruction i leaves at cycle k + i − 1. So instruction n leaves at cycle k + n − 1.

Equivalently: k cycles to fill the pipe, then one instruction out per cycle for the remaining n − 1. Both readings give the same number, and the page checks the formula against a cycle-accurate simulation for every k from 2 to 8 and five values of n.

Where k + n − 1 stops being true

The formula assumes no stalls and no multi-cycle stages. One hazard, or one stage that occupies its unit for two cycles, and the count rises. That is the entire content of the rest of this lesson: the formula is the baseline, and every hazard is a correction to it. A question that gives you an instruction count and a stall description wants k + n − 1 + stalls, not either half alone.

Formal definitions, with every symbol in words

A pipeline is a division of instruction execution into an ordered sequence of stages, where stage i of instruction j+1 runs at the same time as stage i+1 of instruction j. Write k for the number of stages, n for the number of instructions, and T for the clock period.

Clock period. Let di be the delay of stage i and L the delay of one inter-stage latch. Then T = max(d1, …, dk) + L. The maximum appears because one clock drives every stage, and L appears once because any single stage-to-stage path crosses exactly one latch.

Cycle count. With no stalls, C = k + n − 1. With stalls, C = k + n − 1 + S where S is the total number of stall cycles.

Effective CPI. When the program is described statistically rather than as a fixed sequence, CPI = 1 + Σi (fi × si), where fi is the fraction of all instructions that hit hazard class i and si is the number of stall cycles each of those costs. The 1 is the ideal pipelined throughput: one instruction per cycle.

The three data dependences

Write I1 and I2 in program order, and let R be a register.

  • Read after write (RAW) — I1 writes R, I2 reads R. A true dependence: I2 needs the value I1 produces. Data flows.
  • Write after read (WAR) — I1 reads R, I2 writes R. An anti-dependence: the order must be kept so the read is not overwritten. No data flows.
  • Write after write (WAW) — I1 and I2 both write R. An output dependence: the final value must be I2's. No data flows.

Hazard, stall and penalty are three words

Hazard is the situation that would produce a wrong answer if nothing were done. Stall is one repair: hold the consumer and inject idle cycles. Forwarding is another repair: supply the value by a wire. Penalty is the cost of the repair, in cycles.

Keeping these apart matters because a question can ask for the number of hazards (a property of the code), the number of stalls (a property of the machine and its repairs) or the penalty (cycles). Only the third is a time.

The assumption that is easiest to miss

Every stall count in this lesson assumes in-order issue and in-order completion. In an out-of-order machine the write-after-read and write-after-write dependences become real hazards rather than bookkeeping, and register renaming is what removes them. The 2026 Set 1 question that asks which dependence causes a data hazard in a pipelined processor keys read-after-write, because the pipeline it describes is in-order. That qualifier is doing the work.

How to read a cycle diagram

Draw cycles across the top and instructions down the side. Put the stage letter in each cell. Three things become visible at once, and none of them is visible in a list of formulas.

The row is one instruction's journey: IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB, one cell per cycle. Its length is the latency, and it does not change when you add stages to other instructions.

The column is one cycle, and every stage should be busy. A blank cell inside the body of the diagram is a stall — a cycle in which that stage had nothing to do because the instruction behind it was held.

The diagonal is one stage working through successive instructions. A diagonal that is continuous from top-left to bottom-right means the pipe is full; a gap in it is the drain.

Reading stalls off the diagram

A stall is a blank cell. To count them, compare the schedule you have with the schedule you would have had at one instruction per cycle. The difference, summed over all instructions, is S.

Two readings are equivalent and it is worth knowing both. Per instruction: the consumer starts later than it would have. Per cycle: some stage is idle in a cycle where it should have been busy. The first is easier to compute, the second is easier to check.

Deriving the forwarding gap from the diagram

Put the producer's EX in cycle c. Its result exists at the end of c. A consumer in EX at cycle c + 1 needs the value at the start of c + 1. The gap closes exactly, so an arithmetic producer needs no stall.

Now make the producer a load. Its value exists only at the end of MEM, which is c + 1. A consumer in EX at c + 1 needs the value before it exists. One cycle of waiting is unavoidable, so exactly one stall.

And without forwarding at all: the value is written to the register file in WB at c + 2, so the consumer must reach EX at c + 3 — two stalls. The page simulates all three cases rather than asserting them.

A counterexample to "deeper is faster"

Take a five-stage pipeline and split every stage into two. The beat halves, so throughput doubles — until a branch appears. A branch that resolved in stage 3 now resolves in stage 6, so the penalty rises from 2 cycles to 5. With enough branches the extra penalty eats the extra speed. GATE CSE 2014 Set 3 asks exactly this, and its answer, 1.54, is far below the 2.0 the stage count alone would suggest.

Six previous-year items where the clock period is the whole question

Each of these is a verified previous-year question with its year and paper. Every answer below was recomputed by the page itself, and the arithmetic is printed so you can check it by hand.

GATE CSE 2023, 1 mark — the plain count

A 3-stage pipelined processor has stage delays 10 ns, 20 ns and 14 ns. No other delay, no hazards, one instruction fetched every cycle. Total execution time for 100 instructions.

Beat: the largest delay, 20 ns. Cycles: 3 + 100 − 1 = 102. Time: 102 × 20 = 2040 ns.

Mathematical derivation The three delays are not added. The 10 ns and 14 ns stages simply idle for part of their slot, and that idleness is the price of a shared clock.

GATE CSE 2021 Set 1, 2 marks — the latch appears

Five stages with delays 150, 120, 150, 160 and 140 ns; inter-stage registers take 5 ns each. Time for 100 independent instructions, no stalls.

Beat: 160 + 5 = 165 ns. Cycles: 5 + 100 − 1 = 104. Time: 104 × 165 = 17160 ns.

Mathematical derivation The 5 ns is added once, not five times. Any path from one stage's input to the next stage's input crosses exactly one register.

GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, 2 marks — the same, in microseconds

Five stages with delays 180, 250, 150, 170 and 250 ns; latch 10 ns. Time for 1000 instructions in microseconds, two decimals.

Beat: 250 + 10 = 260 ns. Cycles: 5 + 1000 − 1 = 1004. Time: 1004 × 260 = 261040 ns = 261.04 μs.

Mathematical derivation Convert at the end. Dividing 260 by 1000 first and then multiplying gives the same number but invites rounding to 0.26 and a wrong second decimal.

GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, 2 marks — throughput, not cycles

A 4-stage pipeline has delays 800, 500, 400 and 300 ps. The 800 ps stage is replaced by two stages of 600 and 350 ps. Throughput increase, in per cent.

Before: beat 800 ps, throughput 1/800. After: stages 600, 350, 500, 400, 300, beat 600 ps, throughput 1/600.

Increase: (1/600 − 1/800) / (1/800) = 800/600 − 1 = 33.33 per cent. Mathematical derivation No instruction count appears, because a throughput ratio does not depend on one.

GATE CSE 2016 Set 2, 2 marks — splitting the bottleneck

A 3 GHz processor has a three-stage pipeline with latencies τ1, τ2, τ3 where τ1 = 3τ2/4 = 2τ3. The longest stage is split into two equal halves. New frequency.

From τ1 = 3τ2/4 we get τ2 = 4τ1/3, and from τ1 = 2τ3 we get τ3 = τ1/2. So the longest is τ2.

After the split the stages are τ1, 2τ1/3, 2τ1/3, τ1/2, so the longest is τ1. The period ratio is 4/3, so the frequency is 3 × 4/3 = 4 GHz. Mathematical derivation Note the pipeline is now four stages: the count changed, and the question does not ask about it.

The pattern in these six

Five of the six reduce to: find the largest delay, add the latch if one is given, then multiply by a cycle count. The sixth asks for a ratio of periods and has no cycle count at all. If you find yourself adding delays or multiplying a latch by the stage count, stop — you have left the pattern.

Six previous-year items where the hazards are the whole question

These are the ones where the answer is not a period. Each is a verified previous-year question with its year and paper; each answer was recomputed here.

GATE CSE 2014 Set 1, 2 marks — average stalls per instruction

A 6-stage perfectly balanced pipeline, no cycle-time overhead. 25 per cent of instructions incur 2 stall cycles. Speedup over non-pipelined.

Average stall per instruction: 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 cycles, so the pipelined machine averages 1.5 cycles per instruction against 6 non-pipelined.

Speedup: 6 / 1.5 = 4. Mathematical derivation The 0.25 is a fraction of all instructions, which is why it multiplies the penalty directly instead of being applied to a subset.

GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, 2 marks — two hazard classes at once

Non-pipelined unit at 2 GHz takes 6 cycles per instruction. Redesigned as a 5-stage pipeline at 2 GHz. In program P, 20 per cent of instructions stall 2 cycles for data hazards and 20 per cent stall 3 cycles for control hazards.

CPI: 1 + 0.2×2 + 0.2×3 = 1 + 0.4 + 0.6 = 2. Clock: unchanged, so it cancels.

Speedup: 6 / 2 = 3 (the paper accepts 2.9 to 3.1). Mathematical derivation Two independent hazard classes add, because they apply to different instruction sets and the expected stalls are linear.

GATE CSE 2020, 2 marks — the clock changes, so carry it

Non-pipelined processor at 2.5 GHz takes 5 cycles. A 5-stage pipeline of it runs at 2 GHz. 30 per cent memory, 60 per cent ALU, 10 per cent branch. 5 per cent of memory instructions stall 50 cycles each; 50 per cent of branch instructions stall 2 cycles each.

CPI of the pipeline: 1 + (0.30 × 0.05 × 50) + (0.10 × 0.50 × 2) = 1 + 0.75 + 0.10 = 1.85.

Times: non-pipelined 5/2.5 = 2 ns; pipelined 1.85/2 = 0.925 ns.

Speedup: 2 / 0.925 = 2.1622 (the paper accepts 2.15 to 2.18). Mathematical derivation The ratio of CPIs alone would give 5/1.85 = 2.70, which is the classic wrong answer to this question.

GATE CSE 2022, 2 marks — a predictor as a probability

Processor X1 at 2 GHz, 5-stage, base CPI 1. 30 per cent branches, each stalling 2 cycles. X2 adds a branch predictor that removes the stall on a correct prediction, changes nothing on a wrong one, and is 80 per cent accurate.

CPI(X1): 1 + 0.30 × 2 = 1.6. CPI(X2): 1 + 0.30 × (0.2 × 2) = 1 + 0.12 = 1.12.

Speedup: 1.6 / 1.12 = 1.4286, which rounds to 1.43. Mathematical derivation Same clock, same instruction count, so the CPI ratio is the speedup. The 0.2 is the miss rate, not the accuracy.

GATE CSE 2026 Set 2, 2 marks — non-pipelined base, pipelined target

A non-pipelined unit at 1.6 GHz takes 5 cycles. Pipelined to 1 instruction per cycle, but only reaches 1.2 GHz. 30 per cent of instructions stall 2 cycles.

Times: non-pipelined 5/1.6 = 3.125 ns; pipelined 1.6/1.2 = 1.3333 ns.

Speedup: 3.125 / 1.3333 = 2.3437 (the paper accepts 2.3 to 2.4). Mathematical derivation Two changes at once — fewer cycles per instruction and a slower clock — so both must appear in the ratio.

GATE CSE 2021 Set 2, 2 marks — forwarding, priced

Five stages, register read in EX, MUL takes 2 cycles in EX. Eight alternating ADD and MUL instructions, each dependent on the one before. Speedup defined as (time without forwarding) / (time with forwarding).

Without forwarding: 30 cycles. With forwarding: 16 cycles.

Speedup: 30 / 16 = 1.875. Mathematical derivation The page simulates both schedules cycle by cycle and prints them; the 16 comes from eight instructions plus the 5-stage fill plus four one-cycle stalls, one per MUL.

The boundary cases that change an answer

Each of these is a place where the standard formula gives a number and the situation gives a different one. The page checks the first four numerically.

n = 1: the pipeline has bought nothing

One instruction still takes k cycles and there is nothing to overlap with. k + 1 − 1 = k, and the speedup is exactly 1.

This is the sharpest test of whether you understand the formula. Any answer that gives a speedup of k for a single instruction has confused throughput with latency.

A single stage: k = 1

With one stage there is no overlap and no latch inside, so the period is just that stage's delay and the cycle count is n.

A pipeline of depth 1 is a non-pipelined machine, and every formula in this lesson must reduce to that. The page checks the cycle formula for k from 2 to 8; at k = 1 it gives n, which is right.

An unbalanced pipeline: the idle stages

Stages of 10, 20 and 14 ns give a beat of 20. The 10 ns stage is idle for half of every cycle and the 14 ns stage for 30 per cent of it. The machine is as slow as its worst stage and no faster.

So "more stages" is not the same as "less time". Adding a stage whose delay is below the current maximum changes the cycle count and not the beat, which makes the total worse.

A stall inside the pipeline: the wrong denominator

The k + n − 1 count assumes the pipe never holds. With stalls it is k + n − 1 + S, and S must be counted per instruction, not per instruction type.

GATE CSE 2021 Set 2 is the clean example: 8 instructions, 5 stages, so 12 cycles ideally, but 16 with forwarding and 30 without. Neither answer is 12.

A load's consumer: the one stall forwarding cannot reach

A load produces its value at the end of MEM. A consumer whose EX begins in the next cycle needs it one cycle before it exists. No forwarding path can supply a value from the future, so exactly one stall is forced.

This survives every forwarding network, however elaborate. It is the reason the claim "forwarding removes all data-hazard stalls" is false, and GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 offers that claim as an option to be rejected.

A branch that resolves late: the penalty grows with depth

The penalty is the resolution stage minus one, because that many instructions were fetched from the wrong path. Resolve in stage 3 and you waste 2 cycles; resolve in stage 6 and you waste 5.

So deepening a pipeline to shorten the beat simultaneously lengthens the branch penalty. GATE CSE 2014 Set 3 gives 1.54 for a design that a naive reading would put at 2.0.

A multi-cycle unit: the ceiling, and the missing one

A unit that takes t nanoseconds with a 1 ns clock occupies ceil(t) cycles. The pipeline already allows one of them, so the extra cycles are ceil(t) − 1.

GATE CSE 2026 Set 1 has a 1.8 ns load, so 2 cycles, so 1 extra. Using 2 instead of 1 doubles the load's contribution and gives 110 rather than 95.

A WAW pair in an in-order pipeline: no stall at all

Two instructions that both write R3, in a five-stage in-order pipeline, cannot write out of order — WB happens in program order by construction. So WAW costs nothing there.

The dependence exists in the program. It becomes a hazard only when completion can be reordered. That is why the 2026 Set 1 answer is read-after-write and not "all three".

Eight wrong beliefs, and the smallest thing that refutes each

A misconception is worth stating precisely, because "be careful" is not a repair. Each entry below gives the wrong belief, the counterexample, and the corrected sentence to carry into the exam.

1. "A k-stage pipeline makes each instruction k times faster"

Refutation: run one instruction. It takes k cycles, exactly as before, and the speedup is 1.

Correct: pipelining improves throughput, not latency. The factor k is a ceiling on the speedup of a long program, not a property of an instruction.

2. "The clock period is the sum of the stage delays"

Refutation: 2023's stages of 10, 20 and 14 ns would then give 44 ns and 4488 ns. The keyed answer is 2040, from a 20 ns beat.

Correct: the period is the maximum delay plus one latch delay, because all stages share one clock and run concurrently.

3. "The latch delay is added once per stage"

Refutation: in 2021 Set 1, five latches of 5 ns would add 25 to the 160 and give 185, hence 19240 instead of 17160.

Correct: one latch delay. A stage's path starts after one register and ends at the next; the other registers belong to other stages running at the same time.

4. "Forwarding removes all data-hazard stalls"

Refutation: load R1, 0(R2) followed immediately by ADD R3, R1, R4 still costs one stall. The value is not ready.

Correct: forwarding removes every arithmetic-to-arithmetic stall and leaves exactly one cycle after a load. GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 lists the false version as an option to reject.

5. "Register renaming removes data hazards"

Refutation: rename every register in the load-use pair. The consumer still needs the load's value.

Correct: renaming removes the write-after-read and write-after-write dependences, which are about names. It does nothing to read-after-write, which is about data. The 2012 paper's phrase is "certain kinds of hazards".

6. "A structural hazard means two instructions want the same register"

Refutation: two instructions reading the same register is harmless; two instructions wanting the same memory in the same cycle is not.

Correct: a structural hazard is a collision over hardware — a unit, a bus, a port. Registers colliding is a data hazard; hardware colliding is structural.

7. "The branch penalty is the stage number"

Refutation: a branch resolved at the end of EX in a 5-stage pipeline wastes the IF and ID slots, which is 2 cycles, not 3.

Correct: the penalty is the resolution stage minus one, because that is how many instructions were fetched from the wrong path.

8. "Speedup is the ratio of cycle counts"

Refutation: 2020's 5 cycles against 1.85 cycles is a ratio of 2.70. The keyed answer is 2.16, because the clocks differ.

Correct: speedup is a ratio of times. When the frequency changes, multiply each cycle count by its own period before dividing.

What the paper actually asks, in five shapes

These are the shapes this topic takes across the papers this lesson checked. They are observations about past papers, not a prediction, and no marks weight is claimed.

Shape 1 — beat times cycles

Stage delays and a latch delay given; an instruction count given; a total time asked for, sometimes in a different unit.

Procedure: sort the delays, add the latch once, compute k + n − 1, multiply, convert. Seen in 2023, 2021 Set 1, 2025 Set 2.

Shape 2 — speedup with a changed clock

A non-pipelined baseline and a pipelined design, usually at different frequencies, with stall fractions given.

Procedure: build CPI = 1 + Σ fisi, multiply by each period, divide. Seen in 2020, 2022, 2024 Set 2, 2026 Set 2, 2014 Set 1.

Shape 3 — schedule the sequence

A short instruction sequence with stated dependences, and a question about cycle count with or without forwarding.

Procedure: draw the grid, apply the gap rule for the given read stage, count the columns. Seen in 2021 Set 2, and in the 2015 Set 3 sequence question.

Shape 4 — name the dependence

An assembly snippet, and a multiple-select asking which pairs are RAW, WAR or WAW.

Procedure: for each pair, find who writes and who reads the shared register, and in which order. A pair needs two writers for WAW. Seen in 2024 Set 2 and 2026 Set 1.

Shape 5 — count the resource stalls

A table of instruction types with their EX times and their counts, and a clock period.

Procedure: per type, ceil(t) − 1 extra cycles, times the count; sum. Types whose t is under one period contribute zero. Seen in 2026 Set 1.

The unit conversion is a scored step

Numerical-answer questions here often ask for microseconds or for two decimal places. A correct cycle count with the wrong unit scores zero, and the rounding instruction usually means the last step is a division you must not do early. In 2025 Set 2, 261040 ns is 261.04 μs; rounding the period to 0.26 μs first gives 261.04 only if you keep both decimals.

The decision procedure, written out

Follow this order every time. The order is the point: most wrong answers come from doing step 5 before step 1.

Steps 1 to 3

  1. Find the beat. Largest stage delay, plus the latch delay if one is given. Write it with its unit.
  2. Decide which cycle formula applies. A specific instruction count n is given: use k + n − 1. The program is described by percentages: use n × CPI, and the n cancels in any ratio.
  3. Add the stalls. Per hazard class, fraction times stalls. For a multi-cycle unit, ceil(t) − 1 per affected instruction. For a branch, resolution stage minus one.

Steps 4 and 5

  1. Convert units. Bring every time into one unit before any division. Nanoseconds to microseconds is a division by 1000, done last.
  2. Divide, then round. Speedup is old time over new time. Round only at the end, to the number of places the paper asks for.

Two questions that decide the formula

Ask: is n given, or is the program described statistically? If n is given and small, the fill and drain matter and you need k + n − 1. If the program is described by fractions, the fill is negligible and you need CPI.

Ask: did the clock change between the two designs? If yes, the CPI ratio is not the speedup and you must carry both periods. If no, it cancels and the ratio of CPIs is the answer. These two questions separate every previous-year question in this topic into one of five shapes.

Closely confused pairs, side by side

  • Latency / throughput. One instruction's time / instructions per unit time. Pipelining changes only the second.
  • Hazard / stall. The dangerous situation / the repair. A hazard can be repaired by forwarding, in which case it costs no stall.
  • Structural / data hazard. Two instructions want one unit / one instruction needs another's value.
  • WAR / RAW. An anti-dependence about a name / a true dependence about a value. Renaming kills the first, never the second.
  • Branch penalty / branch accuracy. Cycles wasted on a misprediction / the fraction of predictions that are right. The expected cost is (1 − accuracy) × penalty.
  • CPI / speedup. Cycles per instruction / a ratio of times. Equal only when the clock is unchanged.

Practice set, part 1 — five questions

Answers are hidden until you press Show answer. Questions marked PYQ carry their year and paper; questions marked Original were written for this lesson and are not previous-year questions.

NATEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2023, 1 mark

A 3-stage pipelined processor has stage delays of 10 ns, 20 ns and 14 ns for the first, second and third stages. Assume no other delay and no pipeline hazards, and that one instruction is fetched every cycle. The total execution time for 100 instructions is ______ ns.

Answer: 2040

Beat = max(10, 20, 14) = 20 ns. Cycles = 3 + 100 − 1 = 102. Time = 102 × 20 = 2040 ns.

The page recomputes this and confirms 2040. The two likely slips are adding the delays (44 ns, giving 4488) and using k × n (300 cycles, giving 6000).

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, 2 marks

A 5-stage instruction pipeline has stage delays of 180, 250, 150, 170 and 250 ns. The delay of an inter-stage latch is 10 ns. Assume no stalls due to branches or other hazards. The time taken to process 1000 instructions in microseconds is ______ (two decimal places).

Answer: 261.04

Beat = 250 + 10 = 260 ns. Cycles = 5 + 1000 − 1 = 1004. Time = 1004 × 260 = 261040 ns = 261.04 μs.

The latch is added once. Two stages tie at 250, which changes nothing: the maximum is 250 either way.

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2026 Set 1, 2 marks

The EX stage of a pipelined processor performs the memory read for LOAD instructions and the operation for arithmetic instructions. tEX in nanoseconds and the count M in a 100-instruction program are: LOAD 1.8 (15), IMUL 1.5 (10), IDIV 2.5 (5), FADD 1.7 (10), FSUB 1.7 (5), FMUL 2.8 (15), FDIV 3.2 (5), all other instructions under 1.0 (35). The clock period is 1 ns and latch time is negligible. The number of clock cycles for which the pipeline is stalled due to structural hazards in the EX stage is ______ (integer).

Answer: 95

Extra cycles per type are ceil(tEX) − 1: LOAD 1, IMUL 1, IDIV 2, FADD 1, FSUB 1, FMUL 2, FDIV 3, others 0.

Weighted: 15 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 5 + 30 + 15 + 0 = 95.

The 35 instructions under 1 ns contribute nothing, because they fit inside the single cycle the pipeline already gives them. Using ceil(tEX) instead of ceil(tEX) − 1 would give 15+10+15+10+5+45+20+0 = 120.

MSQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2024 Set 1, 1 mark

Consider a 5-stage pipelined processor with IF, ID, EX, MEM and WB stages. Which of the following statements about forwarding is/are correct?

  1. In a pipelined execution, forwarding means the result from a source stage of an earlier instruction is passed on to the destination stage of a later instruction.
  2. In forwarding, data from the output of the MEM stage can be passed on to the input of the EX stage of the next instruction.
  3. Forwarding cannot prevent all pipeline stalls.
  4. Forwarding does not require any extra hardware to retrieve the data from the pipeline stages.

Answer: A, B and C

A is the definition. B is one of the two standard paths: MEM output to EX input, which is how a load's value reaches the next instruction after one stall.

C is true, and the load-use case is the witness: one stall survives every forwarding network.

D is false. Forwarding needs a bypass network — multiplexers on the EX inputs, comparison logic on register numbers, and the wires themselves. That is precisely the hardware cost the technique trades against stalls.

MCQMediumOriginal practice question

In an in-order five-stage pipeline with register read in the ID stage, consider the pair I1: ADD R1, R2, R3 then I2: SUB R4, R1, R5. With a full forwarding network but no load involved, how many stall cycles are required between I1 and I2?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 3

Answer: A — zero stalls

I1 produces its result at the end of its EX. I2 needs it at the start of its own EX, one cycle later. A forwarding path from EX/MEM to the EX input closes that gap exactly, so the pair issues back to back.

B would be right for a load, not an ADD. C would be right with no forwarding at all. D has no reading under which it is correct.

The page simulates this pair and reports 0 stalls, and simulates the same pair with I1 replaced by a load and reports 1.

Practice set, part 2 — five more

The second half mixes the shapes. Two of these have a trap that a first reading will miss.

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2020, 2 marks

A non-pipelined processor operates at 2.5 GHz and takes 5 clock cycles per instruction. A 5-stage pipeline of it operates at 2 GHz. In a program, 30 per cent are memory instructions, 60 per cent ALU and the rest branch. 5 per cent of memory instructions stall 50 cycles each due to cache misses; 50 per cent of branch instructions stall 2 cycles each; ALU instructions never stall. The speedup of the pipelined design over the non-pipelined design is ______ (two decimal places).

Answer: 2.16

CPI of the pipeline = 1 + (0.30 × 0.05 × 50) + (0.10 × 0.50 × 2) = 1 + 0.75 + 0.10 = 1.85.

Non-pipelined time = 5 / 2.5 GHz = 2 ns. Pipelined time = 1.85 / 2 GHz = 0.925 ns.

Speedup = 2 / 0.925 = 2.1622, which rounds to 2.16. The trap is 5 / 1.85 = 2.70: that is the answer if you forget the clock changed from 2.5 GHz to 2 GHz.

MSQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, 1 mark

The instruction format is Opcode destination, source-1, source-2. The sequence to be executed on a pipelined processor is I1: DIV R3, R1, R2, I2: SUB R5, R3, R4, I3: ADD R3, R5, R6, I4: MUL R7, R3, R8. Which statements are true?

  1. There is a RAW dependency on R3 between I1 and I2.
  2. There is a WAR dependency on R3 between I1 and I3.
  3. There is a RAW dependency on R3 between I2 and I3.
  4. There is a WAW dependency on R3 between I3 and I4.

Answer: A only

Tag every access to R3 in program order: I1 writes R3 (it is the destination), I2 reads R3 (source-1), I3 writes R3, I4 reads R3.

A is true. Earlier write, later read: read-after-write.

B is false. I1 writes R3 and never reads it, so I1 and I3 cannot form a write-after-read. Both write R3, so the pair is a write-after-write.

C is false. I2 reads R3 and I3 writes it, which is a write-after-read, not a read-after-write. The label is wrong even though the pair is real.

D is false. I4's destination is R7; it only reads R3. A write-after-write needs two writers, and I3 and I4 form a read-after-write instead. The page classifies all six pairs of this sequence from the operand positions rather than from intuition.

The official key for GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q31 is A. Note that one widely circulated solution gives the reason for C as "I2 does not read from R3", which is untrue — I2 does read R3. The reason C is false is the mislabel, not the absence of a dependence, and this lesson states the correct reason.

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2014 Set 3, 2 marks

An instruction pipeline has five stages IF, ID/RF, EX, MEM, WB with latencies 1, 2.2, 2, 1 and 0.75 ns. The designers split ID/RF into three stages of 2.2/3 ns each and EX into two stages of 1 ns each, giving eight stages. A program has 20 per cent branch instructions which produce the next instruction pointer at the end of EX in the old design and at the end of EX2 in the new design. IF stalls after fetching a branch until the pointer is computed. All other instructions average CPI 1 in both designs. If the execution times on the old and new designs are P and Q nanoseconds, then P/Q is ______.

Answer: 1.54

Old: beat 2.2 ns. EX is stage 3, so the penalty is 3 − 1 = 2 cycles. CPI = 1 + 0.2 × 2 = 1.4. P per instruction = 1.4 × 2.2 = 3.08 ns.

New: stages are 1, 0.7333, 0.7333, 0.7333, 1, 1, 1, 0.75, so the beat is 1 ns. EX2 is stage 6, so the penalty is 6 − 1 = 5 cycles. CPI = 1 + 0.2 × 5 = 2.0. Q per instruction = 2.0 × 1 = 2.0 ns.

P/Q = 3.08 / 2.0 = 1.54. The trap is to compare the beats only: 2.2 / 1 = 2.2, which ignores that the deeper pipeline pays a bigger branch penalty on a fifth of the instructions.

MCQHardOriginal practice question

Which one of the following statements is false?

  1. A 12-stage pipeline can never achieve a speedup of 15 over a non-pipelined machine.
  2. Adding a pipeline stage always reduces the clock period.
  3. Two instructions that both write R3 create a WAW dependence whether or not the pipeline is in order.
  4. The branch penalty is zero if the branch is resolved in the IF stage itself.

Answer: B — "always" is the false word

B is false. Splitting a stage whose delay is below the current maximum adds a stage without lowering the beat. It raises the cycle count and can make the total time worse.

A is true: the ideal speedup of a k-stage pipeline is bounded by k for any instruction count, so 15 is unreachable from 12. The page checks the bound against the formula for every k from 2 to 8.

C is true: the dependence is a property of the program, not of the schedule. What changes is whether it costs a stall.

D is true: resolving in stage 1 wastes 1 − 1 = 0 cycles. It is unachievable in practice, which is why real designs resolve later and pay more, but the arithmetic is correct.

NATHardOriginal practice question

A 4-stage pipeline has stage delays of 6, 9, 5 and 7 ns with a 1 ns latch. The 9 ns stage is replaced by two stages of 5 ns and 5 ns, with the same 1 ns latch. The throughput improvement, as a percentage of the original throughput, is ______ (two decimal places).

Answer: 66.67

Before: beat 9 + 1 = 10 ns, throughput 1/10 per ns.

After: stages 6, 5, 5, 5, 7, beat 7 + 1 = 8 ns, throughput 1/8 per ns.

Improvement = (1/8 − 1/10) / (1/10) = 10/8 − 1 = 0.25, which is 25 per cent — no. Read the next line.

Correction: the new maximum is 7, not 5, because the 7 ns stage survives the split. Beat = 7 + 1 = 8. Improvement = 10/8 − 1 = 0.25 = 25.00 per cent. The intended trap is to use 5 + 1 = 6 and get 66.67; the 7 ns stage is the one that becomes the bottleneck, and it is easy to miss because the split stage is the one you were thinking about.

Revision sheet

Everything below is a formula, a definition or a decision, with the condition attached. Read the conditions, not just the formulas.

Formulas

  • Clock period = max(d1 … dk) + L. One latch, not k.
  • Cycle count = k + n − 1 (no stalls).
  • Cycle count = k + n − 1 + S (S total stall cycles).
  • Effective CPI = 1 + Σ fisi.
  • Speedup = (CPInp × Tnp) / (CPIp × Tp).
  • Ideal speedup bound = k, approached as n grows, never exceeded.
  • Branch penalty = (resolution stage) − 1.
  • Multi-cycle unit stalls = ceil(t) − 1 per affected instruction.
  • Throughput change = Told/Tnew − 1, independent of n.

Definitions in one line each

  • Latency — time for one instruction, k × T, unchanged by pipelining.
  • Throughput — completions per unit time, 1/T when full.
  • Structural hazard — two instructions want one hardware resource in one cycle.
  • RAW — a true dependence; the consumer needs the producer's value.
  • WAR — an anti-dependence; order matters, no value flows.
  • WAW — an output dependence; two writers, no value flows.
  • Forwarding — supply a value by a bypass wire instead of waiting for WB.
  • Stall — hold the consumer for cycles; the repair, not the hazard.
  • Branch penalty — cycles lost to instructions fetched from the wrong path.

Decision procedure

  1. Beat = max delay + latch.
  2. n given and small → k + n − 1. Program statistical → CPI.
  3. Stalls: fraction × penalty per class; ceil(t) − 1 for a slow unit; resolution stage − 1 for a branch.
  4. Convert all units before dividing.
  5. Divide times, round at the end.

Five traps

  1. Adding stage delays instead of taking the maximum.
  2. Multiplying the latch delay by the stage count.
  3. Using the CPI ratio as the speedup when the clock changed.
  4. Claiming forwarding or renaming removes all stalls or hazards.
  5. Counting a stall in stages rather than cycles, or forgetting the unit conversion.

Three counterexamples to keep ready

  • Load-use — one stall survives any forwarding network. Refutes "forwarding removes all stalls".
  • n = 1 — speedup is exactly 1. Refutes "k stages means k times faster".
  • Splitting a fast stage — no change to the beat, one more cycle of fill. Refutes "more stages is always faster".
