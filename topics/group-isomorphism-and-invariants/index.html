Topic and objective

TOPIC: Group isomorphism — when two groups are the same group with the elements renamed — and the invariant hunt, which is the only way to prove that two groups are not the same.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State isomorphism as a bijection preserving the operation and derive the three consequences that come free; prove two groups are the same by writing the witness map down and checking it on every ordered pair; name the six invariants a relabelling cannot change and compute each one from a multiplication table; separate Z4 from V4, Z6 from S3, and D4 from Q8 with the cheapest invariant that works; see the brute-force search and recognise the element-order profile as that search with the impossible candidates removed; and state exactly why a matching profile is never a proof of sameness, with a computed counterexample at order 16.

I read the request as the relation that makes “Groups” a classification rather than a list. Monoids and groups owns the axioms, the ladder semigroup to abelian group, the order of an element, cyclic groups, subgroups, cosets and Lagrange. That lesson prints “same order, different group” once and moves on; this page owns what it does not: the definition, the witness, the six invariants, the search, and the counterexample that stops the profile being treated as a test.

Prerequisites

Needed: what a group is (closure, associativity, identity, inverse), what the order of an element is, and what a bijection is. All of that is in Monoids and groups and Functions and their properties.

Helpful: relations, because isomorphism is an equivalence relation and the three properties are checked on the page.

Not needed: Lagrange’s theorem (the page uses only counting), cosets, normal subgroups, quotient groups, rings or fields. None of them is used or assumed.

What is computed, not quoted

Eight groups are built as real multiplication tables: Z4, V4, Z6, S3, D4, Q8, Z4×Z4 and Q8×Z2. From those tables the page computes element orders, the element-order profile, abelianness, the centre, every subgroup by exhaustive subset search, and every isomorphism by exhaustive bijection search.

Four pairs are compared. The witness Z6 → Z2×Z3 is checked on 36 ordered pairs. The order-16 pair is compared profile by profile. Every automorphism count is the output of the same search run with both arguments the same group.

1. Intuition, and 2. the formal definition

Intuition. Two shops have the same opening hours written on two different clocks. One clock has twelve marks, the other has twenty-four. A customer does not care which clock is on the wall: if there is a faithful conversion between the two dials that turns “we open at eight and close at six” on one into the same schedule on the other, the two shops are open the same hours. An isomorphism is that conversion. It is not a comparison of two things; it is a translation that shows there was only ever one thing.

Formal. Let G and H be groups with operations ∙ and ∘. A map φ : G → H is an isomorphism when it is a bijection and φ(a ∙ b) = φ(a) ∘ φ(b) for every a and b in G. When one exists, G and H are isomorphic, written G ≅ H. VERIFIED FACT

Read the equation as: combine first and then rename, or rename first and then combine — the answer is the same. Both halves of the definition are load-bearing. A bijection that scrambles the table is not an isomorphism, and a table-preserving map that is not a bijection is a homomorphism, which is a different and weaker object.

3. Three consequences that come free

The identity goes to the identity. Put b equal to the identity of G. The equation forces φ(identity) to behave as an identity on the image, and a group has only one identity.

Inverses go to inverses. Apply the equation to a and its inverse, and the product lands on the identity; the image of the inverse is therefore the inverse of the image.

Order is preserved. If a taken k times gives the identity, so does φ(a) taken k times, and conversely. This single consequence is the engine of the whole lesson. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

4. Isomorphism is an equivalence relation

Reflexive: the identity map preserves the operation. Symmetric: the inverse of a bijection is a bijection, and the equation can be read backwards. Transitive: two operation-preserving bijections compose into one.

Checked on the page on four groups of order 6: reflexive 4 of 4, symmetric on all 12 ordered pairs, transitive on all 64 ordered triples. Because it is an equivalence relation, “the groups of order n” splits into classes, and a classification question is a question about the number of classes.

5. The six invariants

An invariant is a number computed from the table alone, which no relabelling can change. The page computes six: the order |G|; whether the group is abelian; the element-order profile (how many elements of each order); the size of the centre; the number of subgroups of each order; and the automorphism count, the number of isomorphisms from the group to itself.

Each is necessary. None is sufficient, and the last scene before the exam shows a pair that shares a profile and is still not one group. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

6. Boundaries: what the definition does and does not say

It says: there exists a bijection with that property. It does not say: the map is unique (Z6 has two isomorphisms onto Z2×Z3, computed), or that the groups have the same elements (they need not share a single element), or that the map is order-preserving in any other sense.

It is also not a statement about size. Two infinite groups can be isomorphic; two groups of the same finite order need not be.

7–9. GATE, practice, consolidation

GATE application. Two shapes only: compare two named groups, or count the classes of a given order. The first is answered by the cheapest invariant that disagrees; the second needs the short list of small groups.

Practice. Seven questions, one of them a previous-year question with its year printed on it.

Consolidation. Before computing anything, decide whether you are being asked to prove sameness or difference. Sameness needs a witness; difference needs an invariant. They are different jobs and only one of them can be done by counting.

Example 1: sameness, proved by a witness

Claim: Z6 ≅ Z2×Z3, the integers mod 6 against pairs of bits added component by component.

The map. φ(x) = (x mod 2, x mod 3). It sends 0 to (0,0), 1 to (1,1), 2 to (0,2), 3 to (1,0), 4 to (0,1) and 5 to (1,2) — six distinct images, so it is a bijection.

The check. All 36 ordered pairs are tested against φ(a+b) = φ(a) + φ(b). The page reports 0 violations, and separately reports that the order of every one of the six elements is preserved. Exactly two of the 120 bijections fixing the identity pass, which is why the automorphism count of Z6 is 2. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 2: difference at order 4

Z4 has element orders 1, 4, 2, 4: one element of order 2. V4 has element orders 1, 2, 2, 2: three elements of order 2.

A bijection cannot send one element to three, so no isomorphism exists. Subgroups 3 against 5, and automorphisms 2 against 6, say the same thing twice more. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 3: difference at order 6

Z6 is abelian; S3 is not, because the transposition (0 1) and the 3-cycle (0 1 2) do not commute. Abelianness is preserved, so the two are not isomorphic.

The profile agrees for a second reason: Z6 is 1, 1, 2, 2 by element order and S3 is 1, 3, 2 with no element of order 6 at all. The centre is 6 against 1. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 4: difference at order 8, where the cheap tests fail

D4 and Q8 are both non-abelian, both have centre of size 2, and both have three subgroups of order 4. Four of the six invariants agree.

The profile does not: D4 is 1, 5, 2 and Q8 is 1, 1, 6. Five elements of order 2 against one. The total subgroup counts also differ, 10 against 8, and the automorphism counts differ sharply, 8 against 24. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 5: the profile is not a test

Z4×Z4 and Q8×Z2 both have order 16 and both have the profile 1, 3, 12 — one element of order 1, three of order 2, twelve of order 4.

Z4×Z4 is abelian and Q8×Z2 is not, so they are not isomorphic. The profile, which separated every earlier pair, is silent here. This is computed on the page from two real tables, not quoted.

Counterexample: a matching element-order profile with no isomorphism

Z4×Z4 and Q8×Z2. Both order 16. Both 1 element of order 1, 3 of order 2, 12 of order 4. One is abelian, the other is not.

So “the profiles agree, therefore the groups are isomorphic” is false, and this is the smallest counterexample the page can build from its own tables. It matters because the profile is the invariant students reach for first, and after three successful separations in a row it is tempting to promote it to a test.

The correct reading is a filter: agreement keeps the pair alive, disagreement kills it. Only a witness map can finish the argument.

The search is finite, and that is a real theorem

An isomorphism must send the identity to the identity, so at most (n−1)! bijections need trying, each with n² product checks. At order 8 that is 5040 × 64 = 322560 checks — large, but finite.

The page does not claim to run all 322560. It runs the pruned search and reports how many candidates survived the filter. Where the filter leaves nothing, no product is tested at all, and that is stated rather than glossed.

Order 1 and the trivial group

The group with one element is isomorphic to itself and to nothing else. Its profile is a single 1, it is abelian, its centre is the whole group, it has exactly one subgroup, and its automorphism count is 1.

Every group of prime order is cyclic, so prime orders have exactly one class each. The first order with more than one class is 4.

The map is not unique

Z6 has two isomorphisms onto Z2×Z3, one sending the element 1 to (1,1) and one sending it to (1,2). Both are correct and both are witnesses.

So “the isomorphism” is wrong phrasing. The definition asks for existence, and the count of witnesses is the automorphism count of the group, 2 here.

Isomorphic is not equal

Z6 and Z2×Z3 share no element at all: one is built from residues mod 6, the other from ordered pairs. They are isomorphic, not equal.

The habit worth forming is to say “the same group up to renaming”, and never “the same set”.

Two invariants that fail where you expect them to work

The centre size does not separate D4 from Q8: both are 2. The number of subgroups of order 4 does not either: both are 3.

Any invariant can fail on some pair. That is why the exam answer names the invariant that does disagree rather than asserting that a particular one always works.

1. “Same order, so the same group”

Wrong. Order 4 has two groups, order 6 has two, order 8 has five. Z4 and V4 are both of order 4 and are not isomorphic; D4 and Q8 are both of order 8 and are not.

Right. Order is the cheapest filter and only ever rules a pair out. A match means the comparison has not started.

2. “The element-order profile decides it”

Wrong. Z4×Z4 and Q8×Z2 share the profile 1, 3, 12 and are not isomorphic, as the page computes.

Right. A mismatching profile proves difference. A matching profile proves nothing. This is the single most costly confusion in the topic.

3. “Isomorphic means equal”

Wrong. Z6 and Z2×Z3 have no element in common. An isomorphism is a bijection between two different sets, not an identity of sets.

Right. “The same group with the elements renamed.” Every table-only question has the same answer in both; no element is shared.

4. “A bijection is enough”

Wrong. Z4 and V4 have the same cardinality, so bijections exist. What does not exist is a bijection satisfying φ(a+b) = φ(a) + φ(b) for all pairs.

Right. Both halves of the definition are needed, and the equation is checked on every ordered pair — 36 of them at order 6.

5. “Abelianness is not an invariant, since it is not a number”

Wrong. An invariant does not have to be a number. Abelianness is a yes-or-no property computed from the table, and an operation-preserving bijection carries the equation ab = ba across unchanged.

Right. Anything read off the table is preserved. That includes abelianness, the centre, the subgroup counts and the element orders.

6. “Two non-abelian groups of the same order are basically the same”

Wrong. D4 and Q8 are both non-abelian of order 8 and are as different as two groups can be: five elements of order 2 against one, ten subgroups against six, eight automorphisms against twenty-four.

Right. Non-abelianness is one bit of information. The profile carries far more, and even the profile is not complete.

Two shapes, and the tool each demands

Shape 1 — compare two named groups. “Are these two groups isomorphic?” or “which of the following is isomorphic to G?” Run the four steps cheapest first and stop at the first disagreement. Almost every such question is settled by abelianness or by the element-order profile, in that order of cost.

Shape 2 — count the classes of a given order. “How many non-isomorphic groups of order n are there?” This is not a comparison; it needs the list. For the orders that get asked the list is short: 4 gives Z4 and V4; 6 gives Z6 and S3; 8 gives five; 9 gives Z9 and Z3×Z3; a prime gives one. COMMUNITY OBSERVATION for the observation that these two shapes cover the questions seen on this topic.

The four-step procedure, in order

1. Compare |G|. Different → not isomorphic, done.

2. Compare abelianness. One abelian and one not → done.

3. Compare the element-order profile. Mismatch → done. Match → still open.

4. Write the map and check every ordered pair. This is the only step that can answer yes.

Wording that changes the answer

“non-isomorphic groups of order 4” — 2, and the groups are Z4 and V4.

“non-isomorphic abelian groups of order 4” — still 2, because every group of order p² is abelian.

“non-isomorphic groups of order 6” — 2, but one of them is non-abelian, so adding the word abelian would change the answer to 1.

That last pair is the trap: at order 4 the extra word is free, at order 6 it is not.

How to write the answer in two lines

For difference. Name the invariant, give both values, and say that an isomorphism preserves it. One sentence, full marks.

For sameness. Give the map, say it is a bijection, and say why the equation holds. Without the map there is no answer, however confident the reasoning sounds.

MCQ PYQ — GATE CSE 2007

How many different non-isomorphic abelian groups of order 4 are there?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5

Answer: (1), that is 2. The two are Z4, the integers mod 4, and V4, the Klein four-group of pairs of bits. The page builds both as tables and confirms that no bijection between them preserves the operation.

The word abelian does no work at order 4, because every group of order p² is abelian. Option 3 or 4 usually comes from counting subgroups instead of groups: Z4 has 3 subgroups and V4 has 5, and neither number is the answer. Option 5 is the count for order 8, not order 4.

MCQ original practice

Z4 and V4 both have four elements and both are abelian. Which single computation proves they are not isomorphic?

  1. Comparing their numbers of subgroups: 3 against 5
  2. Counting elements of order 2: one against three
  3. Computing the order of each group: 4 against 4
  4. Noting that one is written additively and one multiplicatively

Answer: (2). An isomorphism sends an element of order 2 to an element of order 2, so it would have to send one element to three. No bijection can do that. This is the profile test, and the page reports it as the reason the pruned search leaves zero candidates.

Option 1 also works and is a genuine second proof, but the question asks for the count that settles it most directly, and the subgroup count is a consequence of the same fact. Option 3 is a match, so it proves nothing. Option 4 is notation, not structure: how a group is written says nothing about its table.

MSQ original practice

Which of the following are preserved by every group isomorphism?

  1. The number of elements of order 2
  2. Whether the group is abelian
  3. The number of subgroups of order 3
  4. The number of elements that commute with every element

Answer: all four. Element order is preserved, so the count of elements of each order is preserved. Abelianness is the equation ab = ba carried through the map. A relabelling carries subgroups to subgroups of the same size. Commutation is a table fact, so the centre is carried to the centre.

The reason to include this question is that all four are invariants and none of them is sufficient on its own: the page's order-16 pair agrees on the first three shapes of profile and still differs, and D4 and Q8 agree on the centre size and still differ.

NAT original practice

How many isomorphisms are there from Z6 to Z2×Z3?

Answer: 2. Once the image of the element 1 is fixed, everything else is forced, because every element of Z6 is a multiple of 1. The image of 1 must have order 6, and exactly two elements of Z2×Z3 do: (1,1) and (1,2).

This number is also the automorphism count of Z6, which the page computes by running the same search with both arguments the same group. The tempting wrong answer is 1, on the assumption that “the isomorphism” is unique.

MCQ original practice

A student writes: “D4 and Q8 both have order 8 and both are non-abelian, so they must be isomorphic.” What is the defect?

  1. Nothing: the argument is sound, and the two groups are isomorphic.
  2. Their element-order profiles differ: 1, 5, 2 against 1, 1, 6.
  3. They have different numbers of subgroups of order 4.
  4. One is cyclic and the other is not.

Answer: (2). D4 has five elements of order 2 and Q8 has one, so the profiles disagree and no isomorphism exists. The page computes both profiles from the tables and reports zero surviving candidates out of 5040.

Option 3 is false: both have exactly three subgroups of order 4. Option 4 is false for both: a group of order 8 with an element of order 8 would be cyclic and hence abelian, and neither is. Option 1 is the misconception itself, and the profile is its smallest refutation.

MCQ original practice

Which statement about the element-order profile is correct?

  1. If two finite groups have the same profile, they are isomorphic.
  2. If two finite groups have different profiles, they are not isomorphic.
  3. Both of the above.
  4. Neither of the above.

Answer: (2). A different profile is a proof of difference, because the profile is an invariant. The converse fails: Z4×Z4 and Q8×Z2 both have profile 1, 3, 12 and are not isomorphic, one being abelian and the other not.

This is the whole asymmetry of the topic in one question. Option 3 is the answer most often chosen, and it is the reason the counterexample at order 16 is on the page.

NAT original practice

How many groups of order 8 are there, up to isomorphism?

Answer: 5. Three are abelian — Z8, Z4×Z2 and Z2×Z2×Z2 — and two are not: D4, the symmetries of the square, and Q8, the quaternions.

The page builds D4 and Q8 and separates them by profile. The full count of five is taken from the standard small-groups table cited in the Sources tab, not derived here: this page enumerates only the two non-abelian groups it needs.

The six facts to carry

1. G ≅ H means there is a bijection φ with φ(a∙b) = φ(a)∘φ(b) for all a, b. Bijection plus table, both halves.

2. Order of element is preserved, so the element-order profile is preserved. That one consequence does most of the work.

3. Sameness is proved by a witness map, checked on every ordered pair. There is no other kind of proof.

4. Difference is proved by an invariant that disagrees. Any one is enough.

5. A matching invariant is a filter, not a proof. The profile fails at order 16: Z4×Z4 against Q8×Z2.

6. The procedure is order, then abelianness, then profile, then the map — cheapest first, and only the last step can say yes.
