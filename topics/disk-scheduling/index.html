Topic and objective

TOPIC: I/O scheduling — disk scheduling. The arm of a hard disk has to visit a set of cylinders in some order; six rules for choosing that order, and the arithmetic that scores each one. This is the "I/O" half of the clause "CPU and I/O scheduling" in Section 8 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus. The CPU half is the separate lesson CPU Scheduling.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a disk range, a starting head position, a direction where one is stated and a queue of cylinder requests, write the service order for FCFS, SSTF, SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK or C-LOOK, sum the head movement, convert it to seek time or to power when the question supplies a rate, and say which request is served before which. State the one property each rule buys and the one it costs.

I read this topic as one list and one sum. Every rule here produces a permutation of the queue; every question is either "what is your permutation" or "what does your permutation cost". The rules differ only in what they are allowed to look at — arrival order, distance, or direction — and the whole difficulty is bookkeeping: the physical end of the disk, the jump in the circular variants, and the request that sits exactly under the head.

Prerequisites

  • Absolute value and summing gaps. Every total in this topic is $$\sum |c_{i+1} - c_i|$$ — the sum of the distances between consecutive cylinders, never the sum of the cylinder numbers.
  • A line of numbers as a picture of a disk. Cylinders are integers on a line from 0 to the last cylinder. The head is a point on it that moves.
  • Average of a rotation. A sector is equally likely to be anywhere, so the expected wait for it is half a rotation. Built in CPU Scheduling only as far as averages go; the rotation itself is defined on the Build-up tab here.
  • What a seek is, in hardware terms. The arm moves in one dimension only. Sector and platter numbers never change the seek distance — they are why rotational latency and head switching exist, which is a different cost.

Nothing from file systems is assumed. Where a question needs the block-to-cylinder mapping, it is stated: a block maps to exactly one cylinder, and that is all the scheduler needs.

  3. SSTF. 236 on the same queue, and the far request that is served last.
  4. Greedy is not optimal. Four requests, 139 against 118.

  6. C-SCAN. 382, of which the jump is more than half.
  7. LOOK and C-LOOK. 299, 322, and GATE 2016's 346.

  10. The procedure. Five steps and five traps.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. Every policy is a permutation of the queue plus a sum of absolute differences. Write the order down first; the total is a reading of it.
  2. SSTF is greedy and is not optimal: on head 50 with requests 11, 40, 60 and 90 it travels 139 where 118 is possible. It can also starve a request indefinitely.
  3. SCAN holds a direction to the physical end of the disk; LOOK reverses at the last request. That single difference is 331 against 299 on the reference queue.
  4. C-SCAN and C-LOOK jump back without serving anything, and the jump is charged. On the reference queue it is 199 of C-SCAN's 382 cylinders.
  5. Movement is not the only score. A question can charge per cylinder, per reversal, or per millisecond, and the cheapest schedule can change between those models.

Intuition: a lift that only goes up until the top floor

A building has floors 0 to 199 and one lift. People press buttons. The lift's motor is slow, and every floor it passes costs the same whether it stops or not. The question is what rule the lift follows.

Rule one, first come first served: answer the buttons in the order they were pressed. A press for floor 183, then one for floor 37, sends the lift up and straight back down, and the person at 65 waits through both trips.

Rule two, nearest first: always go to the closest pressed button. The lift never travels far in one go, and total travel falls a lot — but the person at 183 is only ever served if no nearer button appears, and on a busy day it never does.

Rule three, the elevator rule: keep going up until the top floor, then keep going down until the bottom. Everyone is served within two sweeps. The cost is that the lift visits the top floor even when nobody asked for it.

Educational interpretation The analogy carries the mechanism exactly: one moving object, positions on a line, a cost per unit of travel, and rules that differ only in what they are allowed to look at. It does not carry the timing: a real disk also waits for rotation, which the lift has no analogue for.

Formal statement

A disk has cylinders numbered from $$L$$ (lowest) to $$H$$ (highest). A request is a cylinder number in that range. The head is at some cylinder $$h$$. A schedule is an ordering $$c_1, \dots, c_n$$ of the pending requests, and its cost in head movement is

$$\text{movement} = |c_1 - h| + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} |c_{i+1} - c_i|$$the distance from the head to the first request, plus every distance between consecutive requests

A seek is the arm moving between cylinders. If one cylinder of travel costs $$s$$ milliseconds, seek time is $$s \times \text{movement}$$. Nothing in the definition mentions time of arrival unless the rule being defined uses it — FCFS does, and the other five do not.

Access time, term by term

Seek time: the arm moves to the right cylinder. Proportional to the distance in cylinders, at a rate the question states.

Rotational latency: the right sector rotates under the head. At $$r$$ rotations per second the full turn takes $$1/r$$, and the average wait is $$1/(2r)$$ because the sector is equally likely to be anywhere. At 7200 rpm that is 8.33 ms per turn and 4.17 ms on average.

Transfer time: the bytes move. Size divided by transfer rate; 4 KB at 100 MB/s is about 0.04 ms.

Derivation The three add. With the stated parameters a 100-cylinder access is $$10 + 4.17 + 0.04 = 14.21$$ ms, and the seek is 70% of it. That dominance is the entire justification for a scheduler that only reorders.

The two families, in one line each

Position-based: FCFS looks at arrival, SSTF at distance. Neither has a direction, so neither can guarantee a bound on waiting.

Direction-based: SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK and C-LOOK all hold a direction and sweep. The sweep is what bounds the wait; the difference between the four is where the head turns round and whether the return trip serves anything.

Every question in this topic is answerable from those two sentences plus the arithmetic. If a rule has no direction, it cannot bound starvation; if it has a direction, the direction must be stated.

The six rules, stated precisely

Read these as algorithms, not descriptions. Each one takes the same three inputs — the pending set, the head position, and for the last four a direction — and returns an order.

FCFS

Serve the requests in the order they appear in the question. Ignore distance entirely. Never preempts, never reorders. Bound on waiting: one pass of the queue.

SSTF

Serve the pending request with the smallest $$|c - h|$$, move the head there, remove it, repeat. Ties need a stated rule; this lesson breaks a tie toward the lower cylinder and says so wherever it matters. Bound on waiting: none.

SCAN (elevator)

Hold the direction. Serve every request met on the way. Continue to the physical end of the disk ($$H$$ going up, $$L$$ going down) even with nothing pending there, then reverse and serve on the way back.

C-SCAN

Hold the direction, serve on the way, go to the physical end, then jump to the other end ($$H \to L$$ going up) without serving anything, and sweep in the same direction again. The jump is real movement and is charged.

LOOK

SCAN, except the head reverses at the last pending request in its direction rather than at the physical end. It "looks" for requests before turning. Same service order as SCAN, smaller total.

C-LOOK

C-SCAN, except the head reverses at the last pending request and jumps to the lowest pending request — not to $$L$$ — then sweeps in the same direction.

The one-line summary of the whole family

SCAN and C-SCAN pay for a guarantee with a trip to the end of the disk; LOOK and C-LOOK keep the guarantee and drop the trip. Circular versions pay for uniform waiting with a jump that serves nothing. Everything else is arithmetic on the resulting order.

Worked example 1 — the reference queue, all six rules

Cylinders 0 to 199, head at 53, pending requests 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67 in that arrival order. This is the queue the standard textbook example uses, and every total below was computed by the model on this page, then re-checked as the sum of its own legs.

Italic entries are the ends of the disk, visited with nothing pending there. The best order over all 8! permutations is 208, so no rule here is optimal — and the 208 happens to be LOOK downward's total on this queue.

Why SCAN downward and SSTF tie at 236

SCAN downward: 53 down to 0 is 53, then 0 up to 183 is 183, total 236. SSTF happens to reach 236 by a completely different route. A coincidence of this queue, not a property.

This is worth internalising because a remembered number can look like understanding. Change one request and the two separate immediately; the arithmetic does not.

Counterexample: SSTF is not optimal

Head at 50, requests 11, 40, 60, 90. SSTF goes 40, 60, 90, 11 with legs 10, 20, 30, 79 — total 139. The order 11, 40, 60, 90 has legs 39, 29, 20, 30 — total 118.

The greedy rule minimised each step and lost by 21 cylinders, because the last leg is chosen for it. This is why "SSTF minimises total head movement" is a false statement, and why a question asking for the minimum needs an argument.

Worked example 2 — GATE CSE 2015 Set 1, Q30

"Suppose the following disk request sequence (track numbers) for a disk with 100 tracks is given: 45, 20, 90, 10, 50, 60, 80, 25, 70. Assume that the initial position of the R/W head is on track 50. The additional distance that will be traversed by the R/W head when SSTF is used compared to SCAN (assuming SCAN moves towards 100 when it starts execution) is ______ tracks." Numerical answer item.

Step 1 — SSTF, with the head request included

From 50: distances are 5 to 45, 30 to 20, 40 to 90, 40 to 10, 0 to 50, 10 to 60, 30 to 80, 25 to 25, 20 to 70. The nearest is 50 itself, so it is served first at zero cost.

Order 50, 45, 60, 70, 80, 90, 25, 20, 10 with legs 0, 5, 15, 10, 10, 10, 65, 5, 10, total 130.

Step 2 — SCAN upward, with the trip to 100

Moving toward 100, it serves 60, 70, 80, 90, then continues to track 100 because SCAN goes to the end, then reverses through 45, 25, 20, 10.

Climb 50 to 100 is 50; return 100 to 10 is 90; total 140. Legs 50, 10, 10, 10, 10, 55, 20, 5, 10.

Step 3 — the answer, and the ambiguity

The gap is $$|130 - 140| = 10$$, and the published key is 10. Read strictly, SSTF travels ten cylinders fewer, so the word "additional" names the wrong direction; the key takes the magnitude, which is what a numerical answer item can accept.

Two other numbers are close enough to tempt: LOOK would give 120, so a LOOK/SSTF gap would be 10 as well — by coincidence — and dropping the request at 50 changes SSTF to 130 anyway, since its leg is zero.

What the three wrong answers look like

Answering 120 or 130 comes from using LOOK's turning point or from stopping SCAN at the last request. Answering 0 comes from reading "additional" literally and rounding a negative up to zero. Answering 20 comes from charging the return trip twice.

All three are bookkeeping errors, not conceptual ones — which is the pattern across this topic.

Worked example 3 — GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q48 (C-LOOK) and GATE CSE 2018 Set 1 (power)

Two items that look different and are the same procedure: write the order, then apply the cost model the question names.

2016: C-LOOK from 63

Queue 47, 38, 121, 191, 87, 11, 92, 10 on cylinders 0 to 199, head at 63 moving toward larger numbers.

Climb: 87, 92, 121, 191. Then C-LOOK jumps to the smallest pending request, which is 10, and climbs again: 11, 38, 47.

Legs $$24 + 5 + 29 + 70 + 181 + 1 + 27 + 9 = 346$$. The jump alone is 181, more than half the answer. Jumping to 0 instead of 10 would give 356; that is the trap.

2018: SSTF, scored in power

Cylinders 72, 134, 20, 86, 116, 16; head at 80. SSTF order 86, 72, 116, 134, 20, 16 with legs 6, 14, 44, 18, 114, 4 — 200 cylinders.

Directions: up, down, up, up, down, down. Reversals at 86, at 72 and at 134 — 3. The starting direction is given, so the first move is not a reversal.

Power $$= 200/100 \times 20 + 3 \times 15 = 40 + 45 = 85$$ mW. The sector and platter numbers in the question are decoration.

Worked example 4 — GATE CSE 2014 Set 1, Q19

201 cylinders numbered 0 to 200, arm at 100, queue 30, 85, 90, 100, 105, 110, 135, 145. SSTF: when is cylinder 90 served?

From 100 the nearest is 100 itself — the head is already there, distance 0 — then 105 (5), then 110 (5). From 110, 90 is 20 away and 85 is 25, so 90 is next.

Order 100, 105, 110, 90, 85, 135, 145, 30. Cylinder 90 is served fourth, so after 3 requests. Missing the request at 100 gives 2; that is the whole question.

The pattern behind all four

Nothing here needed a new idea. Each item was: fix the four facts (range, head, direction, whether the head cylinder is in the queue), write the order by the named rule, sum the absolute differences, then convert to the unit asked. The exam varies the rule and the cost model, not the procedure.

Where the definitions bite

Each card is a boundary case, the condition it violates, and the value it produces. These are the cases that separate a remembered formula from a computed answer.

Edge case: the request under the head

If a request equals the head cylinder it is served first in SSTF at zero cost, and it is still a request. GATE 2015's queue contains 50 with the head at 50, and GATE 2014's contains 100 with the arm at 100.

Dropping it changes neither the total (its leg is 0) but it changes the order, and GATE 2014 asks about the order. That is why it is dangerous.

Edge case: the last request is at the end of the disk

If the highest pending request is 199, then LOOK and SCAN agree exactly, and C-LOOK and C-SCAN agree exactly. The distinction is real but invisible on that particular queue.

So "SCAN = LOOK here" is true for that queue and false as a rule. A question that puts a request at the end is testing whether you noticed, not whether you memorised.

Edge case: no request on one side of the head

Head at 150 moving up, every request below 150. SCAN climbs to 199, serves nothing, and comes all the way back: 49 cylinders of travel that served nothing before the first request. LOOK turns at once and costs 0 for that phase.

The same asymmetry is why a downward SCAN and an upward SCAN on one queue give different totals — 331 against 236 on the reference queue.

Edge case: the jump in the circular variants

C-SCAN's jump is exactly $$H - L$$ (199 here). C-LOOK's jump is $$(\text{highest pending}) - (\text{lowest pending})$$, which is usually smaller: 169 against 199 on the reference queue.

Both are charged. A question that says "ignore the return movement" is asking for a different, non-standard quantity and must say so.

Edge case: a disk numbered 1 to 100

GATE 2015's queue is on "100 tracks", and the request set contains 10 and 90 but nothing at 100 or 1. If the numbering is 0 to 99, upward SCAN goes to 99 and the total is 138; if it is 1 to 100, upward SCAN goes to 100 and the total is 140.

The published key of 10 requires 140, so the paper reads the tracks as 1 to 100. This is a genuine ambiguity in the wording and it is worth noticing rather than memorising.

Edge case: ties in SSTF

Two requests equidistant from the head, or two arrivals in the same instant, make the schedule non-unique. On the reference queue no tie arises, which is why the total is safe to quote.

Where a tie does arise, an option list may contain both totals. The rule: the question must state a tie-break, and if it does not, say which one you used.

Edge case: one request, or none

One request: movement is the distance from the head to it, and every policy gives that. No requests: movement is 0 and the head does not move — SCAN still has a direction, but with nothing pending there is nothing to sweep.

These are worth stating because a formula like "distance to the end plus distance back" has to be read as 0 rather than as a negative.

Edge case: the units

Cylinders, milliseconds, milliwatts, or "number of requests serviced before X" — four different quantities, all derived from the same order. Converting mid-sum is how 346 becomes 350.

Do the whole sum in cylinders, then multiply once. If a per-reversal charge exists, count reversals from the order, not from the picture.

Seven beliefs that cost marks

Each card states the wrong belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule that replaces it.

1. "SSTF minimises total head movement"

Tempting because the name says shortest, and on the standard queue it beats FCFS by 404 cylinders.

Refuted by head 50 with requests 11, 40, 60, 90: SSTF travels 139, the order 11, 40, 60, 90 travels 118.

Rule: SSTF minimises the next step only. It is a heuristic; the minimum over all orders needs its own argument.

2. "SCAN and LOOK are the same thing"

Tempting because the service order is identical on most queues, and the two names are used loosely.

Refuted by the reference queue: LOOK 299, SCAN 331, a gap of 32 cylinders that served nothing.

Rule: SCAN turns at the physical end of the disk; LOOK turns at the last pending request.

3. "The C-SCAN return jump is free"

Tempting because nothing is read during the return, so it can feel like a reset.

Refuted by the arithmetic: the jump is 199 of C-SCAN's 382 cylinders on the reference queue.

Rule: the arm physically travels the range. Movement is charged whether or not a request is served.

4. "Average rotational latency is one full rotation"

Tempting because the rpm gives a rotation period directly and the half factor is easy to drop.

Refuted by 7200 rpm: 8.33 ms per turn, 4.17 ms on average. Using 8.33 doubles every latency in the answer.

Rule: average wait is half a rotation, $$1/(2r)$$, because the sector is equally likely to be anywhere.

5. "More requests means more movement"

Tempting because each request is another stop.

Refuted by adding a request at a cylinder the head already passes: the total is unchanged, and adding the head's own cylinder adds exactly 0.

Rule: movement is set by the spread of the set and the order, not by the count.

6. "The minimum-movement schedule also minimises time and power"

Tempting because seek time is proportional to movement, so the two look identical.

Refuted by GATE 2018's model, which charges 15 mW per reversal: a schedule with slightly more movement and one fewer reversal can win.

Rule: read which quantity is being scored before choosing a policy.

7. "The order does not matter, only the total"

Tempting because most questions ask for a number.

Refuted by GATE 2020, which asks which statement about the order is false, and GATE 2014, which asks which request is served third.

Rule: write the order down every time. It costs one line and it answers half the questions in the topic.

Six shapes of question, and the reasoning each demands

Every item in this topic is one of these. The procedure is the same in all six; what changes is which line of the order the question reads.

1. Total head movement under a named rule

The default. Write the order, sum the absolute differences, report cylinders.

Common wrong approach: summing the destinations, or omitting the physical-end visit or the jump.

2. Identify the algorithm from a service order

Look at the shape: monotone then reverse means SCAN or LOOK; a single long jump in the same direction means C-SCAN or C-LOOK; no pattern means FCFS or SSTF.

Discriminator: whether the head turned at the disk end or at a request, and whether anything was served on the return.

3. Which request is served k-th

Only the order answers this. GATE 2014 asks it with a request sitting exactly under the arm.

Common wrong approach: computing a total and reading a position off it.

4. True/false statements about the order

GATE 2020's form: five requests, SSTF, which statement is false. Produce the order once, then test each claim against it.

Common wrong approach: checking only the first two services and guessing.

5. A cost model that is not distance

Time per cylinder, power per hundred cylinders plus power per reversal, or a stated seek-time function. Convert once, at the end.

Common wrong approach: counting the starting direction as a reversal, or letting sector numbers into the distance.

6. Comparison of two rules on one queue

GATE 2015's form: the gap between SSTF and SCAN. Compute both totals on the same queue and subtract.

Common wrong approach: reusing the first rule's order for the second, or assuming the gap is signed.

The four facts to extract from any question stem

The cylinder range (and whether it starts at 0 or 1), the head position, the starting direction when the rule has one, and whether the head's own cylinder appears in the queue. Every wrong answer in this topic is traceable to one of those four being misread, and none of them is a conceptual error.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2020

Five requests (P, 155), (Q, 85), (R, 110), (S, 30), (T, 115) are pending. The head is at cylinder 100 and SSTF is used. Which statement is FALSE?

(A) T is serviced before P. (B) Q is serviced after S, but before T. (C) The head reverses direction between servicing Q and P. (D) R is serviced before P.

Answer: (B)

Order: R (110), T (115), Q (85), S (30), P (155). Distances from 100: R 10, T 15, Q 15, S 70, P 55; from 110, T is 5. Total movement 225.

Q is served third, T second and S fourth, so Q is after T and before S — the reverse of what B claims. (A), (C) and (D) all hold on the computed order.

NATEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2014 Set 1, Q19

A disk has 201 cylinders numbered 0 to 200. The arm is at cylinder 100 and the queue is 30, 85, 90, 100, 105, 110, 135, 145. Under SSTF, the request for cylinder 90 is serviced after servicing ______ requests.

Answer: 3

Order: 100, 105, 110, 90, 85, 135, 145, 30. The request at 100 is the head position and is served first at zero cost, then 105, then 110; 90 is fourth.

Answering 2 is the standard error: it comes from ignoring the request that equals the head position.

NATMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q48

Requests for cylinders 47, 38, 121, 191, 87, 11, 92, 10. C-LOOK is used, the head is at cylinder 63 moving toward larger numbers, and cylinders are numbered 0 to 199. Total head movement in cylinders is ______.

Answer: 346

Climb 87, 92, 121, 191; jump to the smallest pending request 10; climb 11, 38, 47. Legs 24 + 5 + 29 + 70 + 181 + 1 + 27 + 9 = 346.

Jumping to 0 instead of 10 gives 356. Jumping to 199 first and treating it as C-SCAN gives 191 + 199 + 47, which is not an option because the jump is to the lowest request.

NATHardPYQ — GATE CSE 2015 Set 1, Q30

Disk of 100 tracks, request sequence 45, 20, 90, 10, 50, 60, 80, 25, 70, head initially at track 50. The additional distance traversed by SSTF compared with SCAN, with SCAN moving toward 100, is ______ tracks.

Answer: 10

SSTF: 50, 45, 60, 70, 80, 90, 25, 20, 10 with legs 0, 5, 15, 10, 10, 10, 65, 5, 10 = 130. SCAN upward: climb 50 to 100 = 50, return 100 to 10 = 90, total 140.

The gap is 10, and the key is the magnitude. Note the word "additional" names the opposite direction to the arithmetic; LOOK would give 120, and SCAN over 0 to 99 would give 138.

NATHardPYQ — GATE CSE 2018 Set 1

A disk has 200 cylinders 0 to 199. Requests arrive at cylinders 72, 134, 20, 86, 116, 16; the head is at cylinder 80. Power is 20 mW per 100 cylinders of movement and 15 mW per reversal of direction. Under SSTF, total power in mW is ______.

Answer: 85

SSTF order 86, 72, 116, 134, 20, 16 with legs 6, 14, 44, 18, 114, 4 = 200 cylinders. Directions up, down, up, up, down, down: reversals at 86, 72, 134, so 3.

Power = 200/100 × 20 + 3 × 15 = 40 + 45 = 85. Counting the initial direction as a reversal gives 100; counting only 2 reversals gives 70.

NATMediumOriginal practice question

On the reference queue with the head at 53 moving toward lower cylinders, SCAN is used on cylinders 0 to 199. Total head movement in cylinders is ______.

Answer: 236

Downward: 53 to 37 to 14, then to 0 (the end), then up through 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183. Movement = 53 + 183 = 236.

236 is also SSTF's total on this queue. That is a coincidence of this request set, and the coincidence is exactly what a careless answer relies on.

NATMediumOriginal practice question

GATE 2016's queue — 47, 38, 121, 191, 87, 11, 92, 10, head at 63 moving toward larger numbers, cylinders 0 to 199 — is served by LOOK instead of C-LOOK. Total head movement in cylinders is ______.

Answer: 309

Climb 87, 92, 121, 191, then reverse and serve on the way down: 47, 38, 11, 10. Legs 24 + 5 + 29 + 70 + 144 + 9 + 27 + 1 = 309.

The difference from C-LOOK's 346 is the shape of the turn: LOOK serves on the return (144 + 9 + 27 + 1), C-LOOK jumps without serving (181) and climbs again (1 + 27 + 9).

MSQEasyOriginal practice question

Which of the following are true of rotational latency?

(A) On average it is half a rotation. (B) It doubles when the rotation speed doubles. (C) It is unaffected by the order in which requests are served. (D) At 7200 rpm the average is about 4.17 ms.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D)

Average wait is $$1/(2r)$$ because the sector is equally likely to be anywhere. Nothing a scheduler does changes how long the platter takes to come round.

(B) is false: doubling the speed halves the latency, since period and rate are inversely related.

MCQEasyOriginal practice question

Which statement about disk scheduling is TRUE?

(A) SSTF always achieves the minimum possible total head movement. (B) FCFS can starve a request indefinitely. (C) C-SCAN charges movement for the return trip even though nothing is served on it. (D) LOOK always travels further than SCAN.

Answer: (C)

(C) is the definition: the jump is physical travel and is counted — 199 of C-SCAN's 382 cylinders on the reference queue.

(A) is refuted by 139 against 118. (B) is false: FCFS serves everything within one pass of the queue. (D) is backwards: LOOK ≤ SCAN always, because it stops at the last request instead of the end.

Practice, page 3 of 3 — traps and a distinction

The last three items test the two things this topic gets wrong most often: a closely confused pair, and a cost model that is not distance.

MCQMediumOriginal practice question

Head at 53, requests 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67, cylinders 0 to 199. Which pair of rules produces the same service order but different totals?

(A) FCFS and SSTF   (B) SCAN upward and LOOK upward   (C) SCAN upward and C-SCAN   (D) SSTF and C-LOOK

Answer: (B)

Both serve 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183, 37, 14 in that order. SCAN additionally travels to 199 and back, so 331 against LOOK's 299.

This is the cleanest statement of the SCAN/LOOK distinction: the orders agree, the movement does not, and the gap is exactly the useless trip. (C) differs in order because C-SCAN does not serve on the return.

NATHardOriginal practice question

A disk rotates at 6000 rpm and one cylinder of seek costs 0.2 ms. A request needs a 50-cylinder seek and transfers 8 KB from a drive that moves 50 MB per second. Average access time in milliseconds, rounded to two decimals, is ______.

Answer: 15.16 ms

Seek 50 × 0.2 = 10.00 ms. Rotation period 60000/6000 = 10 ms, so average latency 5.00 ms. Transfer 8192 / (50 × 106) = 0.164 ms.

Total 10.00 + 5.00 + 0.16 = 15.16 ms. Using a full rotation instead of half gives 20.16; using 50 MB as 50 MiB gives 0.156 and the same rounded answer, which is worth knowing before spending time on the unit.

NATMediumOriginal practice question

Head at 100, cylinders 0 to 199, requests 60, 100, 140, 20. How many cylinders does C-LOOK travel if the head starts moving toward larger numbers?

Answer: 200

Order: 100 (the head's own cylinder, cost 0), 140, then the jump to the lowest pending request 20, then 60. Legs 0 + 40 + 120 + 40 = 200.

C-SCAN on the same queue would be 99 up to 199, the 199-cylinder jump, then 60: 358. The 158-cylinder saving is exactly the difference between jumping to the end of the disk and jumping to the lowest request.

One-screen revision sheet

Everything in this lesson that is worth carrying into the exam room. The numbers are from the reference queue: cylinders 0 to 199, head 53, requests 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67.

Formulas

$$\text{movement} = |c_1 - h| + \sum |c_{i+1} - c_i|$$distance from the head to the first request, then between consecutive requests

$$\text{access} = \text{seek} + \frac{1}{2r} + \frac{\text{bytes}}{\text{rate}}$$seek is distance × time per cylinder; the rotation term is half a turn

$$\text{C-SCAN} = (H - h) + (H - L) + c_{\text{last after wrap}}$$climb to the end, the full-range jump, then the last request served after the wrap

$$\text{C-LOOK} = (c_{\max} - h) + (c_{\max} - c_{\min}) + (c_{\text{2nd}} - c_{\min})$$with $$c_{\min}$$ and $$c_{\max}$$ the lowest and highest pending requests

The six rules, one line each

FCFS — arrival order. 640 here. No starvation, longest travel.

SSTF — nearest pending. 236 here. Greedy, not optimal, can starve.

SCAN — sweep to the physical end, reverse, serve both ways. 331 up, 236 down.

C-SCAN — sweep to the end, jump back without serving, sweep again. 382.

LOOK — sweep to the last request, reverse, serve both ways. 299.

C-LOOK — sweep to the last request, jump to the lowest request, sweep again. 322.

Five traps

  1. SCAN visits the end of the disk; LOOK does not. The gap is 32 cylinders here.
  2. An unstated direction makes SCAN unanswerable — 331 against 236 here.
  3. The C-SCAN and C-LOOK jump is charged; 199 of 382 here.
  4. Average rotational latency is half a rotation, not one.
  5. A request equal to the head position costs 0 but changes the order — and the order is often the question.

Properties worth remembering

LOOK ≤ SCAN and C-LOOK ≤ C-SCAN, always, because the only difference is a trip that serves nothing.

The minimum over all orders is at most any policy's total; on the reference queue it is 208 and no named rule achieves it.

SSTF can starve; FCFS cannot; SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK and C-LOOK bound the wait to about two sweeps, which is what "no starvation" means here.

Numbers to sanity-check against
