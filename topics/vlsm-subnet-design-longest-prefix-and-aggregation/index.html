Topic and objective

TOPIC: Subnet design with unequal prefixes (VLSM), the forwarding rule that reads those prefixes, and the merging of prefixes back into larger blocks.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Convert a list of host requirements into the smallest block that holds each one. Place those blocks largest-first at aligned addresses, and prove the placement is legal. Read what is left as a set of maximal aligned blocks and decide whether a new requirement fits. Resolve a destination address to the longest prefix among the routes that match it, and say why row order is irrelevant. Merge sibling prefixes into one, refuse to merge non-siblings, and keep a more specific route where part of an aggregate belongs elsewhere.

I read this clause as the design and forwarding half of "CIDR Notation": not how to read a prefix, which the CIDR lesson owns, but how to choose the prefixes for a network and how a router reads them back.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A block is a power of two at an aligned address. The start must be a multiple of the size.
  • A need becomes the smallest block that holds it. 400 hosts means a /23, not a /24.
  • Free space is a set of blocks, not a count. 62 free addresses may contain no /26.
  • Forwarding takes the longest matching prefix. Length competes only among routes that match.
  • An aggregate is a parent whose two halves you own. Otherwise it advertises addresses you do not have.

Intuition first: a shelf of boxes that halve

Imagine a shelf 1024 centimetres long and a rule that a box may only be 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … 1024 centimetres long, and must start at a multiple of its own length. You cannot cut a 100 cm box. To hold 100 objects you take the smallest box that holds them, which is 128 cm. The box must sit at 0, 128, 256 … and nothing may overlap.

Educational interpretation The shelf carries the alignment rule and the rounding-up rule, which are the two things that decide most answers. It does not carry the reserved network and broadcast addresses, or the forwarding rule, which the next cards supply.

Vocabulary, every term in words

Block (CIDR block) — a run of $$2^{32-p}$$ consecutive addresses whose first address is a multiple of that size. Written $$a.b.c.d/p$$.

Prefix length p — the number of leading bits fixed. Larger p means a smaller block.

Host bits h — $$32-p$$, the bits left free inside the block.

Usable addresses — $$2^h-2$$, with two exceptions: a /31 on a point-to-point link holds 2, and a /32 is one host route.

VLSM — variable-length subnet masking: the same parent split into blocks of different prefix lengths.

Longest prefix match — the forwarding rule: among the routes that match a destination, take the one with the largest p.

Aggregate (supernet) — one shorter prefix that covers exactly the addresses of several longer ones.

The arithmetic, in four lines

size = 232−p  ·  usable = 2h − 2  ·  aligned ⇔ start mod size = 0
network = address AND mask(p)  ·  broadcast = network + size − 1

The mask has p ones followed by $$32-p$$ zeros, so "AND the mask" clears the host bits. RFC 4632 §5.1 requires the mask to be contiguous, which is why the free bits are always one run at the right-hand end.

Mathematical derivation Alignment and size are not independent: a block of size S at address A covers A to A+S−1, and two blocks of the same size are either disjoint or identical exactly when both are aligned. That is why an aligned free region can always be written as a sum of blocks, and why the decomposition in the animation is unique.

The host table you should know by sight

/24 → 256 · 254    /25 → 128 · 126    /26 → 64 · 62
/27 → 32 · 30    /28 → 16 · 14    /29 → 8 · 6
/30 → 4 · 2    /31 → 2 · 2    /32 → 1 · 1

Read each entry as addresses · usable hosts. Converting a requirement to a prefix is then a lookup: 200 hosts needs 254, so a /24; 100 needs 126, so a /25.

Verified fact RFC 3021 (December 2000) states that in a point-to-point link with a 31-bit prefix the two addresses MUST be interpreted as host addresses, and that the limited broadcast replaces the directed broadcast.

The design procedure

  1. Convert every requirement to the smallest prefix that holds it.
  2. Sort the blocks largest first.
  3. Place each at the lowest address that is aligned for its size and does not overlap a placed block.
  4. Decompose the free space into maximal aligned blocks and record their sizes.
  5. Answer the "does it still fit" question by size, never by total.

Mathematical derivation Step 3 is a first-fit allocation of power-of-two blocks. It is the standard buddy-system procedure, and the reason it is safe is the one in the next card.

Why largest first, and what it does not promise

A block of size S needs an aligned hole of at least S. Allocating a large block later is therefore risky: earlier allocations can cut every hole below S even when the free total is large. Largest first keeps the big holes intact for the big blocks.

Unverified claim I do not claim that largest-first first-fit succeeds for every requirement list whose total fits — the lesson checks this one design, not a theorem. What is proved on the page is narrower and enough: the five placements are aligned, disjoint, inside the parent, and the free space sums to the remainder.

The forwarding rule, stated exactly

A route $$(N, p)$$ matches a destination A when the first p bits of A equal the first p bits of N, which is the same as $$\text{network}(A,p)=N$$. Among all matching routes, the one with the largest p forwards the packet. If none matches, the packet is dropped.

Verified fact RFC 4632 §5.1: "Forwarding in the Internet is done on a longest-match basis."

When two prefixes may be merged

Two blocks of the same size are siblings when their addresses agree on all but the last prefix bit: $$\text{net}(A,p)$$ and $$\text{net}(B,p)$$ with $$p-1$$ bits in common. Their parent is the block of prefix $$p-1$$, and replacing the two rows by it changes no address.

Any other pair has a longest common prefix $$q \lt p-1$$, and the only block containing both is of prefix q — which also contains addresses neither row owns. Verified fact RFC 4632 §5.1 rule 2 requires a router that generates an aggregate to discard traffic matching the aggregate but no more specific route, and requires a re-homed network to be advertised explicitly: that is the "hole" the lesson keeps as its own row.

1. The full design: five needs inside 100.64.0.0/22

The parent holds 1024 addresses, from 100.64.0.0 to 100.64.3.255, and 1022 of them are usable. The requirements are 400, 200, 100, 50 and 2 hosts.

400 → /23 · 512 addr · 510 usable → 100.64.0.0/23
200 → /24 · 256 addr · 254 usable → 100.64.2.0/24
100 → /25 · 128 addr · 126 usable → 100.64.3.0/25
 50 → /26 ·  64 addr ·  62 usable → 100.64.3.128/26
  2 → /31 ·   2 addr ·   2 usable → 100.64.3.192/31

962 addresses are allocated and 62 remain. Mathematical derivation Each prefix is the smallest whose usable count covers the requirement: 400 does not fit 254, and 200 does not fit 126. The two-host link takes a /31 under RFC 3021 rather than a /30; with a /30 the design would leave 60 free instead of 62, and the free set would be four blocks instead of five.

The free space, decomposed: 100.64.3.194/31 (2), 100.64.3.196/30 (4), 100.64.3.200/29 (8), 100.64.3.208/28 (16), 100.64.3.224/27 (32). The largest is 32 addresses, so a new LAN needing 30 hosts fits and a new LAN needing 40 does not.

2. Why the /31 is not a mistake

The formula $$2^h-2$$ gives 0 for a /31 and would call the block unusable. RFC 3021 gives both addresses to the two ends of the link, so a /31 holds 2 hosts, costs 2 addresses, and carries no directed broadcast.

/30: 4 addresses · 2 usable · 2 wasted
/31: 2 addresses · 2 usable · 0 wasted

Verified fact RFC 3021 §2.1: with a 31-bit mask "the two addresses above MUST be interpreted as host addresses". A question that expects a /30 for a router-to-router link is using the older convention; if the question gives no hint, state the convention you use.

3. The failing /24: hosts fit, blocks do not

A /24 (192.168.10.0) has 254 usable addresses. Four LANs need 100, 60, 60 and 30 hosts.

hosts: 100 + 60 + 60 + 30 = 250 ≤ 254 ✓
blocks: 128 + 64 + 64 + 32 = 288 > 256 ✗

Mathematical derivation The four prefixes are /25, /26, /26 and /27, and their address totals exceed the parent by 32, so no placement exists and the page's allocator fails on the third block whatever order it tries. The host sum hid the gap because each block rounds up to a power of two and carries two reserved addresses.

4. Longest prefix match on the design's own table

The table has seven rows: the default route, the parent /22, and the five allocations. Each destination below is resolved by the rule, and the page also resolves it a second way — comparing the first p bits of the address with the first p bits of the route — and requires both to agree.

100.64.1.7   matches /0 /22 /23   → 100.64.0.0/23 (LAN-A)
100.64.2.7   matches /0 /22 /24   → 100.64.2.0/24 (LAN-B)
100.64.3.9   matches /0 /22 /25   → 100.64.3.0/25 (LAN-C)
100.64.3.150 matches /0 /22 /26   → 100.64.3.128/26 (LAN-D)
100.64.3.193 matches /0 /22 /31   → 100.64.3.192/31 (WAN)
100.64.3.201 matches /0 /22     → 100.64.0.0/22 (core)
100.64.9.9   matches /0 only    → 0.0.0.0/0 (ISP)

The sixth line is the interesting one. 100.64.3.201 is inside the parent block and outside every subnet, so the parent route catches it. The seventh line is outside the parent block, so only the default route matches: a longer prefix that does not match is not a candidate.

5. Aggregation, both ways

Four /24s that are siblings merge twice into one /22, and the page checks that the union of addresses is unchanged.

100.64.0.0/24 + 100.64.1.0/24 → 100.64.0.0/23
100.64.2.0/24 + 100.64.3.0/24 → 100.64.2.0/23
the two /23s → 100.64.0.0/22 · 1024 addresses

With a hole, the minimal set is two rows: 100.64.0.0/22 and 100.64.6.0/24, covering 1280 addresses. The /24 wins for its own addresses by longest match, and the /22 covers the rest.

Counterexample: the merge that looks right

100.64.4.0/24 and 100.64.6.0/24 look like a pair. Their addresses differ in the second bit of the third octet, so their longest common prefix is 22 bits, and the only block containing both is 100.64.4.0/22.

100.64.4.0/22 → 100.64.4.0 … 100.64.7.255
owned: 4.0/24 and 6.0/24 = 512 addresses
covered: 1024 addresses → 512 unowned

Mathematical derivation The rule is arithmetic, not judgement: siblings differ in exactly one prefix bit. Here they differ in one bit of the address but not at the last prefix position, which is a different thing.

Boundaries: where the formulas stop applying

Each case below is a place where a rule that works everywhere else gives a wrong answer if it is applied without its assumption.

/31 on a point-to-point link

$$2^1-2=0$$, yet the block is usable: RFC 3021 gives both addresses to the two ends. The assumption behind the subtraction is a broadcast medium, and a point-to-point link is not one.

If a question asks for the number of usable addresses on a router-to-router link and gives no convention, a /30 and a /31 are both defensible; say which you used.

/32, the host route

One address, no host bits, no network or broadcast address. The subtraction would go negative, which is the formula telling you it was not written for this case. Used for loopback addresses and for a single host that must be routed explicitly.

In a routing table a /32 is legal and very specific: it beats every other route for that one address.

An address given with a prefix that is not its network

100.64.0.48/26 is a legal way to name the block 100.64.0.0/26, because 64 does not divide 48. The canonical form clears the host bits.

Two different questions hide here: "what block is this address in" wants the cleared form, and "is this a valid subnet address" wants the alignment test. Read which one is asked.

A subnet equal to the parent

If one requirement needs the whole parent, the answer is the parent prefix with no split. This is not a degenerate case to be avoided: it is the correct answer, and any further allocation is then impossible.

In the design on this page, a 1000-host LAN would need a /22 and consume 100.64.0.0/22 entirely, leaving nothing for the other four.

The default route and the /0 test

0.0.0.0/0 matches every address, so it is always the shortest match and never the winner unless nothing else matches. Its network address is 0.0.0.0 and its mask is 0.0.0.0, so the alignment test is trivially true.

RFC 4632 §5.1 states that the default route must be accepted by all implementations, and that it should only be advertised into another routing domain when explicitly configured.

Overlapping aggregates

Two aggregate rows may overlap, and that is legal: the more specific one wins for the addresses they share. What is not legal is an aggregate that covers addresses the advertising domain cannot reach — RFC 4632 requires such an aggregate to be discarded, or the traffic loops.

So "minimal" here means "no row contained in another and no sibling pair left", not "the fewest rows at any cost".

Free space that is a single block, and free space that is not

In this lesson's design the free space is five blocks. In the same design with a /30 for the WAN it would be four, and in a design that allocated perfectly it can be one. The count is not a property of the parent; it is a property of the placements.

Decomposition is unique once the placements are fixed, which is why the page can check it: any different list of maximal blocks is wrong.

A requirement of exactly 2 hosts

Two hosts is the boundary where /30 and /31 both give 2 usable. The /31 is smaller, so a design that minimises addresses takes it; a design that follows an older textbook takes the /30 and wastes 2.

The same boundary appears at 1 host, where the answer is a /32 and there is no subnet at all, only a route.

Six beliefs that produce a wrong answer, each with the case that kills it

Each card states the belief, gives a minimal counterexample, and states the correction. The counterexamples are the ones this page computes.

1. "If the host counts fit, the design fits"

The belief: add the requirements and compare with the usable count of the parent.

The counterexample: 100, 60, 60 and 30 hosts sum to 250, under the 254 usable in a /24, but the blocks are 128 + 64 + 64 + 32 = 288 > 256.

The correction: compare block sizes with the parent's size. Hosts and addresses differ by two reserved per block and by rounding up to a power of two.

2. "62 free addresses means a /26 fits"

The belief: the free total is what growth can use.

The counterexample: after this design, 62 addresses are free in blocks of 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32. No free block is 64 long, so a /26 cannot be placed, although 62 > 32.

The correction: the largest free block is the answer to "what still fits". Decompose before you answer.

3. "A /31 has no usable addresses"

The belief: $$2^h-2$$ is the definition of usable, so 2−2 = 0.

The counterexample: RFC 3021 gives both addresses of a /31 to the two ends of a point-to-point link. A /31 holds 2 hosts.

The correction: the subtraction assumes a broadcast medium with a network address and a directed broadcast. State the assumption, or state which convention the question uses.

4. "The longest prefix in the table is the answer"

The belief: find the longest prefix and forward there.

The counterexample: 100.64.3.201 does not match the /31 or the /26 at all, so the longest prefix in the table is irrelevant; the /22 forwards it.

The correction: the rule is the maximum length among the matching routes. Filter first, then maximise.

5. "Any two blocks can be summarised into one prefix"

The belief: take the longest common prefix and advertise it.

The counterexample: 100.64.4.0/24 and 100.64.6.0/24 share 22 bits, so the summary is 100.64.4.0/22, which also covers 100.64.5.0/24 and 100.64.7.0/24 — 512 addresses nobody owns.

The correction: merge only siblings, that is, equal sizes differing in the last prefix bit. Anything else changes the set of addresses.

6. "Splitting a block creates addresses"

The belief: four /24s hold more than one /22 because there are four of them.

The counterexample: four /24s are 4 × 256 = 1024 addresses, exactly the /22 they came from. The reserved addresses double from 2 to 8, so splitting strictly reduces the usable total.

The correction: a split trades usable addresses for isolation. It never adds capacity.

What the questions look like, and the procedure that answers them

Four shapes account for almost every question in this topic. Each is a small amount of arithmetic on the 32-bit address, and each is answered by the same five steps.

Shape 1 — design

"A block of size X must serve LANs needing a, b, c hosts. Give the subnets." Or: "how many addresses remain free?"

Convert, sort, place, then decompose. The answer is a list of blocks at aligned addresses, and the free total is a subtraction of sizes.

Community observation Design questions are usually the multi-step ones, and the marking is on the addresses, so an unaligned block loses the whole item.

Shape 2 — longest prefix match

"Which next hop is chosen for destination D?" or "which prefixes match D?"

Convert D to bits, test each route's prefix, keep the matches, take the largest p. The page does this twice, once with masks and once bit by bit, and requires the two to agree.

A variant gives the table in a random order to see whether you apply the rule or the row order.

Shape 3 — aggregation

"What is the smallest number of prefixes that covers these networks?" or "which pair can be summarised?"

Drop contained blocks, merge sibling pairs, repeat. The check is that the union of addresses is unchanged; a summary that covers more is wrong even if it is tidier.

A hole in the range stays as its own more specific row.

Shape 4 — host and address counting

"How many hosts can this subnet hold?" "How many subnets of size s fit in block B?"

Use the table, and check the two exceptions. For "how many fit", the answer is $$\lfloor B/s \rfloor$$ only when B is the size of the parent and the subnets tile it; otherwise count the aligned starts, which is what the page's decomposition does.

Watch for the question that asks for addresses and the one that asks for usable hosts: they differ by two per subnet.

The five steps, as you would write them in an exam

  1. Convert. Each host requirement becomes the smallest prefix whose usable count covers it. Write the prefix and the address count side by side.
  2. Sort. Largest block first. Write the sizes in a column so the sum is visible.
  3. Place. Lowest aligned free address. Check the divisibility, then the overlap, then move on.
  4. Decompose. Read the free space as maximal aligned blocks and record the largest. Answer "does it still fit" from that list.
  5. Summarise. Merge siblings only; keep a more specific row for any hole; check that the address union did not change.

Two traps that cost a whole item

The units trap. Comparing host counts with usable addresses, or usable addresses with block sizes. Every comparison must be between the same kind of number.

The filter trap. Taking the longest prefix in the table instead of the longest among the matches. Filter, then maximise.

Where this connects

A routing protocol advertises these prefixes: distance vector and link state move them, and the table they build is read by the rule on this page.

NAT rewrites addresses at a boundary and depends on the inside being a block you control; IPv6 uses the same prefix arithmetic at 128 bits, where the same rules hold with a wider address.

NATOriginal practice

An organisation is given 100.64.0.0/22. It needs four LANs for 400, 200, 100 and 50 hosts. Using the smallest block for each need, allocated largest first, the number of addresses that remain unallocated is ______.

Answer: 64

The prefixes are /23, /24, /25 and /26, so the blocks are 512 + 256 + 128 + 64 = 960 addresses. The parent holds 1024, so 1024 − 960 = 64 remain, and they form exactly one block: 100.64.3.192/26.

Why 62 appears. Subtracting the usable counts instead of the block sizes. Why 60 appears. Using a /30 for a two-host link that was not asked for here. Why 256 appears. Stopping after the first two LANs.

MCQOriginal practice

172.16.8.0/24 is split for LANs needing 100, 50, 25 and 10 hosts, smallest block for each need, largest first. Which allocation for the 10-host LAN is correct?

  1. 172.16.8.192/27
  2. 172.16.8.224/28
  3. 172.16.8.240/28
  4. 172.16.8.224/27

Answer: B

The prefixes are /25, /26, /27 and /28. Largest first: /25 at .0 (0–127), /26 at .128 (128–191), /27 at .192 (192–223), so the /28 starts at .224 and runs to .239.

Why A. That is the 25-host LAN's /27. Why C. 240 is aligned for a /28, but it is the second half of the /27 at .224, which is already used. Why D. A /27 holds 30 hosts, which is more than the need and is not the smallest block.

NATOriginal practice

A router's table holds 10.0.0.0/8, 10.1.0.0/16, 10.1.2.0/24 and 0.0.0.0/0. For the destination 10.1.2.77, the prefix length of the chosen route is ______.

Answer: 24

The destination matches all four rows. The longest matching prefix is 10.1.2.0/24, so the prefix length is 24.

Why 8. The first matching row, which is what a first-match lookup would give. Why 16. Stopping at the second match. Why 0. The default route, which is the shortest, not the longest.

MCQOriginal practice

Which pair of prefixes can be replaced by a single prefix without covering any address that neither of them covers?

  1. 100.64.4.0/24 and 100.64.6.0/24
  2. 100.64.4.0/24 and 100.64.5.0/24
  3. 100.64.0.0/23 and 100.64.3.0/23
  4. 100.64.2.0/24 and 100.64.2.128/25

Answer: B

4.0/24 and 5.0/24 are siblings: equal size, differing only in the last prefix bit, so together they are 100.64.4.0/23 and cover exactly the same 512 addresses.

Why A. Their common prefix is 22 bits, so the only single prefix covering both is 100.64.4.0/22, which also covers 5.0 and 7.0. Why C. 100.64.3.0/23 is not a block at all: 3 is not a multiple of 2. Why D. The /25 is inside the /24, so the pair already covers only the /24; the merge is not a merge but a deletion.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. A /31 on a point-to-point link provides two usable host addresses.
  2. A /32 has a network address and a broadcast address.
  3. If 62 addresses are free after a design, a /26 can always be placed in them.
  4. A destination inside a parent block but outside every subnet is forwarded by the parent's route.
  5. Two prefixes of different lengths can never be merged into one prefix.

Answer: A and D

A is RFC 3021. D is the sixth line of the longest-prefix example: 100.64.3.201 is caught by the /22 because no subnet claims it.

Why B is false. A /32 has no host bits, so it has no network or broadcast address; it is a single host route. Why C is false. In this lesson's design, 62 addresses are free in blocks of 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32, and no block is 64 long. Why E is false. Merging needs equal sizes, but a /24 and its own /25 collapse into the /24 by containment, and a longer prefix inside an aggregate is exactly the "hole" case: the pair is represented by one row plus nothing else.

NATOriginal practice

A block 10.20.0.0/22 must be divided into the smallest blocks that hold 500, 250, 120 and 2 hosts, allocated largest first. The number of addresses in the largest block that remains free is ______.

Answer: 64

The needs give /23 (512), /24 (256), /25 (128) and /31 (2): 512 + 256 + 128 + 2 = 898, so 1024 − 898 = 126 addresses are free. Placement: /23 at 10.20.0.0, /24 at 10.20.2.0, /25 at 10.20.3.0, /31 at 10.20.3.128. Free: 10.20.3.130 to 10.20.3.255, which decomposes into 130/31 (2), 132/30 (4), 136/29 (8), 144/28 (16), 160/27 (32) and 192/26 (64). The largest free block is 64.

Why 126 appears. The free total, which is not a block. Why 32 appears. Stopping the decomposition at 160, the /27; 192 is aligned for a /26 and doubles it. A warning. Taking the largest power of two below the free total also gives 64 here, which is the right answer reached the wrong way: the free total is not what decides, the largest aligned block is. Write the decomposition, not just the answer.

Revision sheet

Definitions, formulas, the decision procedure, the numbers table and the five slips. This is the page to read the night before.

Definitions in one line each

  • Block — $$2^{32-p}$$ consecutive addresses starting at a multiple of that size.
  • Host bits — $$h = 32-p$$.
  • Usable — $$2^h-2$$, except /31 = 2 on a point-to-point link and /32 = 1.
  • VLSM — one parent, blocks of different prefix lengths.
  • Longest prefix match — the largest p among the routes that match.
  • Aggregate — a shorter prefix covering exactly several longer ones.
  • Siblings — equal prefixes differing in the last prefix bit.

Formulas

size(p) = 232−p
aligned(a,p) ⇔ a mod size(p) = 0
network(a,p) = a AND mask(p)
broadcast = network + size − 1
split into 2k: prefix p + k
hosts needed h → prefix = 32 − ⌈log2(h+2)⌉

Mathematical derivation The last line is the table in one expression: you need $$2^h \ge h+2$$, so h is the ceiling of $$\log_2(\text{hosts}+2)$$, and the prefix is 32 − h. For 400 hosts: $$\lceil\log_2 402\rceil = 9$$, so p = 23.

Third octet alignment shortcuts for a /24 and longer: a /25 starts at 0 or 128, a /26 at 0, 64, 128 or 192, a /27 at a multiple of 32, a /28 at a multiple of 16.

The decision procedure

  1. Convert each need: the smallest prefix that holds it.
  2. Sort largest first, and write the address sizes beside the prefixes.
  3. Place at the lowest aligned free address; check divisibility then overlap.
  4. Decompose the free space; answer "does it fit" from the largest block.
  5. Merge only siblings; keep a hole as its own more specific row.

The five slips

  • Adding host counts instead of block sizes.
  • Treating a free total as a free block.
  • Applying $$2^h-2$$ to a /31 or a /32.
  • Taking the longest prefix in the table instead of the longest match.
  • Merging non-siblings and covering addresses nobody owns.

One sentence

A subnet is a power-of-two block of addresses that starts at a multiple of its own size, and every question in this topic — design, forwarding, aggregation — is that sentence plus arithmetic.

Specifications

  • RFC 4632, "Classless Inter-Domain Routing (CIDR): an Address Assignment and Aggregation Strategy", August 2006. §5.1 supplies the longest-match forwarding rule, the requirement that an aggregate which covers unowned addresses be discarded, the re-homed "hole" case, and the contiguity of the mask.
  • RFC 3021, "Using 31-Bit Prefixes on IPv4 Point-to-Point Links", December 2000. §2.1: the two addresses of a /31 are host addresses; the limited broadcast replaces the directed broadcast.
  • RFC 950, "Internet Standard Subnetting Procedure", 1985. It allowed non-contiguous subnet masks, which RFC 4632 §5.1 later forbade. This is why the lesson treats the free bits as one run at the right-hand end.

What the research did not cover, and the assumptions the model makes

The design assumes a single contiguous parent block and a list of host requirements, with no constraint on which subnet must sit where; a real plan often has fixed placements, and then the arithmetic is a constraint check rather than a first-fit. The two-host link takes a /31 under RFC 3021; a question that follows the older /30 convention will differ by two addresses in the free total and by one block in the free decomposition, and both figures are stated in the Examples tab. The model checks that largest-first places this particular set of five requirements; it does not claim that largest-first first-fit succeeds for every requirement list whose total fits, and no such theorem is asserted. Aggregation is treated as "drop contained rows, then merge siblings", which gives the minimal set for the cases on this page but is not claimed as an algorithm for every routing table. The 32-step lookup bound belongs to a plain binary trie; compressed tries and TCAMs have different bounds and are not discussed. Nothing here was checked against a printed textbook page by page, and no claim is made about how often this material appears in GATE CSE papers — only that the CIDR clause it belongs to is in the syllabus.
