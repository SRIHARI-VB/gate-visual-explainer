Topic and objective

TOPIC: Group homomorphisms — maps between groups that respect the operation but need not be one-to-one — and the three objects they carry: the kernel, the image, and the quotient that joins them.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State a homomorphism as one equation with no bijection and derive its three consequences; compute the kernel as the preimage of the identity and see it is a subgroup; use the equivalence “injective exactly when the kernel is trivial” in both directions; read the size of the kernel as the exact measure of the collapse and use the product |G| = |ker| × |im|; show that a kernel is always normal and name a subgroup that can never be a kernel; build the quotient group and see why coset multiplication needs normality; state and verify the first isomorphism theorem; and count the homomorphisms between cyclic groups by enumerating maps.

I read the request as the map that is weaker than an isomorphism, and everything that weakness forces. Monoids and groups owns the axioms, subgroups, cosets and Lagrange; group isomorphism owns the bijective case and the invariant hunt. This page owns what both set aside: the non-bijective map, the kernel, normality, the quotient, and the theorem that ties them together.

GATE relevance: class B

Section 1 of the GATE 2027 Computer Science syllabus, as recorded in this repository’s coverage tracker, prints “Monoids, Groups.” The words homomorphism, kernel and quotient are not printed.

They are the vocabulary the printed word is examined with, and questions of the shape “how many homomorphisms from Zm to Zn” are standard. This page is classified B — the concept behind a named syllabus object. VERIFIED FACT for the syllabus wording.

Prerequisites

Needed: what a group is, the order of an element, subgroups, and cosets — all in Monoids and groups. Also a bijection and a function image, in Functions and their properties.

Helpful: isomorphism, because a homomorphism is that definition with one clause removed, and the contrast is the fastest way to see what the kernel measures.

Not needed: rings, fields, modules, Sylow theory, or the second and third isomorphism theorems. None is used or assumed here.

1. Intuition, and 2. the formal definition

Intuition. A twelve-hour clock and a twenty-four-hour clock. Reading the small clock means folding the big one: eight in the evening and eight in the morning both read “eight”. The fold loses information and it still respects addition — adding two hours on either clock gives the same answer. A homomorphism is that fold, done between groups. A homomorphism answers “what does this group look like from the point of view of that one”, where an isomorphism answers “are these the same group”. EDUCATIONAL INTERPRETATION

Formal. Let G and H be groups. A map φ : G → H is a homomorphism when φ(a∙b) = φ(a)∘φ(b) for every a, b in G. Read it as: combine first and then map, or map first and then combine — the same answer. There is no bijection clause. VERIFIED FACT

An isomorphism is exactly a homomorphism that is also a bijection. Everything on this page is about the case where that second clause is missing, because the missing clause is where all the structure lives.

3. Visual understanding: the kernel is a fibre

Draw the map as arrows from the elements of G to the elements of H. The kernel is the bundle of arrows that end at the identity of H. The image is the set of points that receive at least one arrow.

Once you see the kernel as one fibre, the counting is obvious: every other fibre is a translate of it, so all fibres have the same size, and the number of fibres is the size of the image. That picture is the proof of |G| = |ker| × |im|. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

4. Worked examples on this page

Five maps are built and measured: Z6 → Z3 by remainder (kernel 2, image 3); Z12 → Z4 by remainder (kernel 3, image 4); Z4 → Z4 by doubling (kernel 2, image 2, and the element 1 keeps order 4 while its image has order 2); the sign map S3 → Z2 (kernel 3, image 2); and the trivial map, whose kernel is the whole group.

Each is checked on every ordered pair of the source, so “is a homomorphism” is a computed verdict here, not an assertion.

5. Boundaries: what the definition does and does not say

Does: preserve the operation, so it carries the identity to the identity, inverses to inverses, subgroups to subgroups, and normality to normality. Does not: preserve order, preserve size, or have a trivial kernel. It does not even have to hit every element of the target.

The one direction that survives intact is the inequality: the order of φ(a) always divides the order of a. Shrinking is allowed, growing is not. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

6. Edge cases to hold on to

The trivial group. There is exactly one map out of a one-element group into any group, and it is a homomorphism. Counting by the formula gives n choices of φ(1) that all collapse to the same map, so the formula needs the case m = 1 handled separately.

The trivial map. Sending everything to the identity is always a homomorphism. Its kernel is the whole group and its image is one element.

Onto is a property of the image, not of the map’s formula. A map is onto exactly when its image equals the whole target; the same formula between different targets can be onto or not.

7–9. GATE, practice, consolidation

GATE application. Four shapes only: decide whether a formula is a homomorphism; count homomorphisms; name a kernel; decide injective or onto. The count for cyclic groups is the greatest common divisor, and the other three are answered from the kernel.

Practice. Seven questions, all original practice written for this page and labelled as such, plus one verified previous-year question used in the GATE tab for its adjacent idea.

Consolidation. The kernel is the collapse, the image is the survivor, and the quotient is the same information as the kernel. Name which of the three a question is about before computing anything.

Example 1: the fold everyone should be able to do in their head

Map. φ : Z6 → Z3, φ(x) = x mod 3. It sends 0,1,2,3,4,5 to 0,1,2,0,1,2.

Homomorphism? All 36 ordered pairs are tested against φ(a+b) = φ(a)+φ(b), with 0 violations. Kernel {0, 3}. Image {0, 1, 2}. Product 2 × 3 = 6. Quotient three cosets, {0,3}, {1,4}, {2,5}, and the induced map is a bijection onto Z3 that preserves the operation on all 9 pairs of cosets. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Why it is the reference example. Every quantity on this page is visible at once, and the kernel size 2 is exactly the number of elements merged at each value. If you can explain this map, you can explain the theorem.

Example 2: a bigger kernel, same mechanism

φ : Z12 → Z4, x mod 4. Kernel {0, 4, 8}, size 3. Image all four residues. 3 × 4 = 12.

Four cosets, each of size three, and the quotient is Z4. So the kernel size is not a measure of “how bad” the map is; it is exactly the fibre size, and the image tells you how many fibres there are.

Example 3: order is divided, not preserved

φ : Z4 → Z4, φ(x) = 2x mod 4, giving 0, 2, 0, 2. It is a homomorphism: 4 pairs, 0 violations. Kernel {0, 2}; image {0, 2}.

The element 1 has order 4. Its image 2 has order 2. Order shrank by a factor of 2 and nothing was broken. This single example kills the belief that homomorphisms preserve element order. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Example 4: the smallest non-abelian case

The sign map S3 → Z2 sends the three even permutations to 0 and the three transpositions to 1. Kernel: the identity and the two 3-cycles, size 3. Image: both values, size 2.

The two cosets are the rotations and the reflections, so S3 modulo its kernel is Z2. There are exactly two homomorphisms S3 → Z2: this one and the trivial map. Computed by enumerating all 64 maps.

Example 5: counting, including a non-cyclic answer

Z4 → Z4: 256 maps, 4 homomorphisms. Z6 → Z3: 729 maps, 3 homomorphisms. S3 → S3: 46656 maps, 10 homomorphisms. S3 → V4: 4096 maps, 4 homomorphisms.

The ten from S3 to itself split into one trivial map, six automorphisms, and three maps whose kernel is the rotations. There is no formula here; the count comes from testing every map, which is exactly what an exam asks you to reason about. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Counterexample: a subgroup that is nobody’s kernel

Take H = {identity, (2 3)} in S3. It is a subgroup: it contains the identity and is closed. It is not normal, because conjugating (2 3) by the rotations gives (1 2) and (1 3), which are outside H.

So H is the kernel of no homomorphism out of S3. The proof is one line: every kernel is closed under conjugation. The computation is in the animation: conjugating H gives three distinct subgroups, so no single one is closed.

This is the sharpest boundary on the page. It is not a technicality — the quotient S3/H does not exist as a group, and the next scene shows the multiplication failing on a concrete pair.

The product is not well defined without normality

Multiply the identity by (1 2): the answer is (1 2). Multiply (2 3) by (1 2): the answer lies in a different coset. Both inputs came from the same coset, so the “product of cosets” depends on the representative and is not a function at all.

The page finds this witness by search and prints the two permutations. For the kernel {0, 3} in Z6 the same search returns nothing, which is the normality of the kernel seen as a computation.

The trivial map, and the group of one element

Sending every element to the identity is always a homomorphism. Its kernel is the whole group, its image is one element, and the quotient is the trivial group. Both product checks hold: |G| = |G| × 1.

For the one-element group there is exactly one map to any group, and it is a homomorphism. A count that treats every choice of φ(1) as a different map gets n instead of 1 here, which is why the enumeration on this page handles m = 1 separately.

Onto, injective, and neither

A homomorphism can be injective but not onto (the identity of a proper subgroup is not a homomorphism, but Z2 → Z4 sending 1 to 2 is), onto but not injective (Z6 → Z3 by remainder), both (an isomorphism), or neither (the trivial map into a group with more than one element).

The four cases are separated by two numbers: kernel size and image size. Nothing else is needed.

Where the size product stops

|G| = |ker| × |im| is a statement about finite groups. For infinite groups the first isomorphism theorem still holds as an isomorphism of groups, but multiplying cardinalities is a different statement and this page does not use it.

For the exam this matters only in that every question on this topic is set in a finite group, and the product is then a legitimate free check.

1. “A homomorphism preserves the order of every element”

False, and the smallest counterexample is on this page: Z4 → Z4 by doubling sends 1, of order 4, to 2, of order 2. What is true is that the image order divides the original order.

The belief usually comes from the isomorphism lesson, where order really is preserved. The fix: order preservation is a consequence of injectivity, not of the equation.

2. “Every subgroup is the kernel of some homomorphism”

False. Kernels are normal, and S3 has three subgroups of order 2 that are not. In D4, six of the ten subgroups are normal and four are not, so four subgroups are excluded.

The converse is true: every normal subgroup N is the kernel of the quotient map G → G/N. Both halves are worth knowing, and only one is the direction students guess.

3. “The kernel is whatever maps to zero”

The kernel is the preimage of the identity only. The preimage of any other element is a coset of the kernel, not a kernel. Confusing the two is what makes “the kernel of the map x → 2x mod 4 is {2}” look plausible: 2 maps to 0, but so does 0, and the kernel is {0, 2}.

Check: a kernel always contains the identity. Any candidate set that does not is wrong immediately.

4. “There are n homomorphisms from Zm to Zn”

There are n choices for φ(1), and only the greatest common divisor of m and n of them give a homomorphism. For Z6 → Z3 that is 3 out of 3 choices, which is a coincidence of this pair; for Z6 → Z4 it is 2 out of 4.

The condition is m·k ≡ 0 (mod n), not “anything goes”.

5. “A homomorphism is onto unless it is obviously not”

Onto means the image is the whole target, and that is a computation, not an impression. S3 → Z3 has exactly one homomorphism and it is trivial, so nothing is onto there, even though both groups are small.

For cyclic groups the test is arithmetic: onto is possible only when n divides m, and then there are φ(n) of them.

6. “The quotient exists for any subgroup”

The set of cosets always exists; the group structure does not. Coset multiplication is well defined exactly for normal subgroups, and the page exhibits the failing pair in S3.

So “quotient by H” is only meaningful after normality is established. Skipping that check is the most common lost mark in this topic.

The four shapes, and the procedure for each

Shape 1 — is this formula a homomorphism? Test the one equation on a small generating set, or use the shortcut: the order of φ(a) must divide the order of a. A single violation ends the question. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

Shape 2 — how many homomorphisms are there? For Zm → Zn the answer is gcd(m, n), because φ is fixed by φ(1) = k and the condition is m·k ≡ 0 (mod n). For non-cyclic sources there is no formula; reason from generators and the kernels they can have.

Shape 3 — what is the kernel? Solve φ(x) = e. For x → kx mod n on Zm the kernel is the multiples of n/gcd(k, n) inside Zm; for a permutation group, collect the permutations that act as the identity on the relevant structure.

Shape 4 — injective or onto? Injective exactly when the kernel is {e}. Onto exactly when |im| = |H|, which for finite groups follows from |ker| × |im| = |G|.

Worked exam reasoning, one line each

“How many homomorphisms Z12 → Z18?” gcd(12, 18) = 6. The page’s enumeration gives 6 of the 18 candidate values of k.

“G has 24 elements, a homomorphism has an image of 6; what is the kernel?” 24 / 6 = 4. The product forces it; no formula for the map is needed.

“How many onto homomorphisms Z12 → Z4?” 4 divides 12, so the answer is φ(4) = 2, and the page finds exactly 2.

How to use this set

MCQ original practice

How many group homomorphisms are there from Z12 to Z18?

  1. 2
  2. 6
  3. 12
  4. 18

Answer: (2), that is 6. The map is fixed by φ(1) = k, and it is a homomorphism exactly when 12k ≡ 0 (mod 18), that is when 3 divides 2k, that is when 3 divides k. The values are 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15: six of them, and gcd(12, 18) = 6.

Option 4 is the count of choices for φ(1) before the condition is applied. Option 1 is a decoy with no derivation behind it: the count of onto maps here is 0, because 18 does not divide 12, so it cannot be 2 either. Option 3 confuses the size of the source with the number of maps.

NAT original practice

Let φ : Z20 → Z8 be a group homomorphism. How many possible values of φ(1) give a homomorphism?

Answer: 4. The condition is 20k ≡ 0 (mod 8), which reduces to 5k ≡ 0 (mod 2), so k must be even: 0, 2, 4, 6. Four values, and gcd(20, 8) = 4.

The trap is answering 8, the number of elements of the target. The other trap is answering 2, which is the kernel size of the map with k = 4, not the count of maps.

MCQ original practice

Let φ : Z6 → Z6 be given by φ(x) = 2x mod 6. Which statement is correct?

  1. φ is injective.
  2. The kernel of φ is {0, 3} and the image is {0, 2, 4}.
  3. φ is not a homomorphism.
  4. The kernel of φ is {0} and the image is {0, 2, 4}.

Answer: (2). The map sends 0,1,2,3,4,5 to 0,2,4,0,2,4. It respects addition, so it is a homomorphism. The elements sent to 0 are those with 2x ≡ 0 (mod 6), namely 0 and 3, so the kernel is {0, 3}. The image is {0, 2, 4}, and 2 × 3 = 6 confirms the product.

Option 1 fails because 0 and 3 collide. Option 3 fails because the equation holds on all 36 pairs. Option 4 is the classic error: it keeps only the element 0 in the kernel and forgets that 3 also maps to 0.

MSQ original practice

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. Every kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup.
  2. Every normal subgroup of a group G is the kernel of some homomorphism with domain G.
  3. Every subgroup of an abelian group is the kernel of some homomorphism out of that group.
  4. Every subgroup of S3 is the kernel of some homomorphism out of S3.

Answer: (1), (2) and (3). (1) is the theorem proved in the animation. (2) is the converse: the quotient map G → G/N has kernel exactly N when N is normal. (3) holds because every subgroup of an abelian group is normal, so (2) applies.

(4) is false and it is the counterexample on this page: the three subgroups of order 2 in S3 are not normal, so none of them is a kernel. Option 4 is the option that looks like a harmless generalisation of option 3, and the difference is exactly commutativity.

MCQ original practice

A group G has 24 elements. φ : G → H is a homomorphism whose image has 6 elements. What is the size of the kernel?

  1. 2
  2. 4
  3. 6
  4. 18

Answer: (2), that is 4. |G| = |ker| × |im| gives 24 = |ker| × 6, so |ker| = 4.

Option 4 of the list, 18, is 24 − 6, which is the subtraction reflex and has no meaning here. Option 6 forgets that the kernel is the thing being divided out. Option 2 is 24 / 12, the answer to a different question where the image has 12 elements.

NAT original practice

How many onto (surjective) group homomorphisms are there from Z12 to Z4?

Answer: 2. An onto map must send 1 to a generator of Z4, that is to 1 or to 3, and both give a homomorphism because 12 is a multiple of 4. So the answer is φ(4) = 2, and the page’s enumeration finds exactly 2 onto maps out of the 4 homomorphisms.

The trap is answering 4, the total number of homomorphisms, or gcd(12, 4) = 4, which counts all of them rather than the onto ones.

MCQ original practice

Which one of the following is NOT a group homomorphism?

  1. φ : Z → Z, φ(x) = 2x
  2. φ : Z4 → Z4, φ(x) = x + 1 mod 4
  3. φ : S3 → Z2, the sign map
  4. φ : Z6 → Z3, φ(x) = x mod 3

Answer: (2). φ(0) = 1, and a homomorphism must send the identity to the identity, so it fails before any product is checked. Concretely, φ(0 + 0) = 1 but φ(0) + φ(0) = 2.

The other three pass. Option 1 is multiplication by a constant on an infinite cyclic group, which is always a homomorphism. Option 3 and option 4 are the two maps built and verified in the animation. The trap in option 2 is that it “looks like addition”; the identity test kills it in one line.

The six facts to carry

1. A homomorphism is one equation, φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b), with no bijection clause.

2. The kernel is the preimage of the identity. It is always a normal subgroup, and it always contains e.

3. Injective ⇔ kernel = {e}. This is the most used line in the topic.

4. |G| = |ker| × |im| for finite G. Both sizes divide |G|.

5. A quotient exists only for a normal subgroup; the kernel is normal, so G/ker always exists and is isomorphic to the image.

6. The order of φ(a) divides the order of a. Divided, not preserved.

Normality, by the numbers

S3: 6 subgroups, 3 normal.

D4: 10 subgroups, 6 normal, 4 not.

Every kernel found by enumerating the maps S3→S3, D4→Z2 and D4→V4 is normal.

The transposition pairs of S3 and the reflection pairs of D4 are the standard non-normal examples.

One line to carry into the exam

The kernel measures the collapse, the image is what survives, and the quotient by the kernel is the image. Any question on this topic is asking about one of those three, and naming which one tells you the method.
