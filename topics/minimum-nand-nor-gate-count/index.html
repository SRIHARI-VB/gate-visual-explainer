Topic and objective

TOPIC: The minimum number of two-input NAND or NOR gates needed to build a given Boolean function — and why the answer changes when the question changes.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State the four settings that a gate count depends on (gate type, fan-in limit, which signals are supplied, whether a signal may be reused). Compute the exact minimum for any function of one or two variables from the lesson’s own exhaustive search, and read the minimum off a table for the standard functions. Build a sum of products from NAND gates and a product of sums from NOR gates, and expand every gate wider than the stated fan-in before counting it. Explain why XOR costs four NAND gates without complements and three with them. Explain why a full adder costs nine gates together and fourteen apart. Apply the four-step procedure to a previous-year question.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class A — explicitly in the syllabus, under Section 2, “Design of combinational and sequential circuits”.

PREREQUISITES: reading a truth table and minimising a function by K-map or algebra (Boolean algebra and minimisation); knowing that NAND and NOR are each functionally complete (Combinational circuits); De Morgan’s two laws.

VISUALIZATION PLAN: twelve scenes. The same function is priced under three cost models, so the number visibly moves while the function does not. Then the four settings of a cost model, the one-gate test, and a bar chart of all sixteen two-variable functions by their exact minimum. Then the two XOR circuits side by side, the sum-of-products count, the shared full adder, and the three previous-year questions solved from their own K-maps. Every number on the canvas is read from the page’s model, which searches every circuit up to the stated depth.

I read this topic as a cost model and its consequences, not as a table to memorise. The table is here, computed, but the lesson is about the clause in the question that decides which table row applies.

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A truth table, and that a function of $$n$$ variables has $$2^n$$ rows.
  • De Morgan: $$\overline{p \cdot q} = \bar p + \bar q$$ and $$\overline{p + q} = \bar p \cdot \bar q$$.
  • That NAND alone, and NOR alone, can build every function.
  • Minimising to a sum of products or a product of sums.

Helpful, not required: having seen the “4 NAND gates for XOR” line somewhere, so that the third scene has something to correct.

Approximate learning time: 45–60 minutes with the animation, or 15 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. A gate count belongs to a cost model. Gate type, fan-in limit, supplied signals, reuse. Change one and the number changes.
  2. One gate computes $$\overline{p\cdot q}$$ or $$\overline{p+q}$$, nothing else. That is the whole one-gate test.
  3. A wide gate is not one gate. A $$k$$-input NAND is $$2k-3$$ two-input gates; a $$k$$-input AND is $$2k-2$$.
  4. Supplied complements change the table. XOR: 4 NAND gates without them, 3 with. XNOR: 4 NOR gates without, 3 with.
  5. Shared signals are counted once. The full adder is 9 gates together, not $$8+6$$.

Legend: green marks a construction that was simulated and passed; amber marks a trap, a clause, or a wide gate; red would mark a failing check, and none is failing on this page.

Intuition: pricing a journey

“How much does it cost to get from here to there?” is not a question until you say how you are travelling and what you are allowed to take with you. By train it is one price; by taxi another; and if a friend drives you part of the way, a third.

The destination is the function. The fare is the gate count. The mode of travel is the cost model: which gates you may use, how many inputs each may take, and what arrives at the start already assembled.

Educational interpretation The analogy is exact about the part that matters — the destination does not determine the fare, the mode does — and loose about the rest. There is no “cheapest” in the abstract, only cheapest for a stated mode.

Formal definition

A function of $$n$$ variables is a map from $$\{0,1\}^n$$ to $$\{0,1\}$$. A circuit over a gate type is a finite sequence of gates, each taking two signals, where a signal is either a supplied input or the output of an earlier gate. The size of the circuit is its number of gates.

The minimum for a function $$f$$ under a cost model is the smallest size of any circuit over that model whose last gate computes $$f$$.

size(C) = |C|,   min(f) = min { |C| : C computes f }

The size is how many gates the circuit has. The minimum is the smallest size among all circuits that compute $$f$$ — and it is only defined once the gate type, the fan-in and the supplied signals are fixed.

Mathematical derivation Everything the lesson computes is this definition applied exhaustively: for one and two variables the search visits every circuit up to the stated depth, so the smallest size it finds is the minimum.

The four settings, stated once

Every number on this page is computed under a cost model, and the model is stated before the number. These are the four settings.

  • Gate type. NAND, or NOR. They are not interchangeable in cost.
  • Fan-in limit. Two inputs per gate. A wider gate is expanded before it is counted.
  • Supplied signals. Either the literals only, or each literal and its complement.
  • Reuse. A signal computed once may feed any number of gates, and is charged once.

Educational interpretation These four are the convention this lesson uses. A different convention gives different numbers, and every such number is correct for its own convention. A GATE question fixes them in its wording; a bare “minimum number of gates” fixes only some of them.

The one-gate test

A single NAND gate outputs $$\overline{p\cdot q}$$. A single NOR gate outputs $$\overline{p+q}$$. So a function is one gate exactly when it is the negation of a product, or of a sum, of two signals you already have.

That test catches three things at once: a function that is one gate (answer 1), a function that is already a supplied signal (answer 0), and a function that is not one gate and must be built.

NAND(p,q) = ¬(p·q)    NOR(p,q) = ¬(p+q)

Both gates negate. That is why a sum of products maps onto NAND-NAND directly: the gate supplies the inversion the sum needs.

Mathematical derivation Tying both inputs of a NAND gives $$\overline{p\cdot p} = \bar p$$, so an inverter is one gate and never free — unless the complement was supplied.

For one and two variables the page searches every circuit. The result is a complete classification: every one of the sixteen functions of two variables, with its exact minimum under each of four cost models.

Read it as four columns: NAND with literals only, NAND with complements, NOR with literals only, NOR with complements. The Animation tab draws it with every cell filled from the search.

Two rows carry the whole exam: AND (2 / 2 / 3 / 1) and XOR (4 / 3 / 5 / 3). The first shows a gate type that cannot be helped by complements; the second shows one that can.

Verified fact The “literals only” columns agree with the standard realisation table used in textbooks and in coaching material (AND 2, OR 3, XOR 4, XNOR 5 in NAND; OR 2, AND 3, XOR 5, XNOR 4 in NOR). They are reproduced here by search rather than quoted.

Boundaries: what the count is not

A gate count says nothing about the three things a designer also cares about.

  • Delay. The four-gate XOR is deeper than the three-gate one; a smaller circuit can be a slower one.
  • Fan-out load. Reuse is free in this model, and is not free in silicon: a signal feeding many gates is a real electrical load.
  • Wiring. Two circuits with the same gate count can differ in how many wires cross.

Educational interpretation The lesson counts gates only, because that is what the question asks. It does not model delay, and claims no timing number anywhere.

Example 1 — NOT, AND and OR from NAND

The three constructions everything else is built from, with only the literals available.

1. $$\overline{x}$$ is one gate: tie both inputs of a NAND, since $$\overline{x\cdot x} = \bar x$$.

2. $$x\cdot y$$ is two: one gate for $$\overline{xy}$$, one to invert it.

3. $$x+y$$ is three: build $$\bar x$$ and $$\bar y$$, then $$\overline{\bar x \cdot \bar y}$$, which is $$x+y$$ by De Morgan.

4. So one gate, two gates, three gates: 1, 2, 3.

5. The search confirms no shorter circuit exists for any of the three.

Under NOR the same three come out 1, 3, 2 — the pair swaps, because NOR contains an OR and NAND contains an AND.

Example 2 — XOR, twice

The same function, two supplied-signal settings.

1. Literals only. Gate 1 makes $$\overline{xy}$$. Gates 2 and 3 push $$x$$ and $$y$$ against it. Gate 4 combines: 4 gates.

2. Complements supplied. Gate 1 is $$\overline{\bar x \cdot y}$$, gate 2 is $$\overline{x \cdot \bar y}$$, gate 3 combines them: 3 gates.

3. Both were simulated on all four rows; both give 0110.

4. The search proves two gates cannot do it in either setting.

The difference is exactly the two inverters the first circuit has to build and the second is handed.

Example 3 — a sum of products, counted properly

$$AB + CD$$ and then $$AB + CD + EF$$, in two-input NAND gates.

1. Each product term is one NAND gate, because the gate already negates.

2. $$AB+CD$$: two product gates plus one combine = 3. Verified on all sixteen rows.

3. $$AB+CD+EF$$: three product gates, then a three-input combine.

4. A three-input NAND is $$2(3)-3 = 3$$ two-input gates, so the total is $$3+3 = 6$$.

The tempting answer is 4 — three products plus “the final gate”. The final gate is only one gate when the sum has two terms.

Example 4 — the full adder, shared

Two outputs from one circuit, in nine two-input NAND gates.

1. Gates 1 to 4 make $$A\oplus B$$ by the four-gate construction, and gate 1 leaves $$\overline{AB}$$ on the way.

2. Gate 5 is $$\overline{(A\oplus B)\cdot C}$$. Gates 6, 7 and 8 turn that into the sum, $$A\oplus B\oplus C$$: four more gates.

3. Gate 9 combines gate 1 with gate 5: $$\overline{AB + (A\oplus B)C}$$, the carry.

4. Nine gates for both outputs; all eight rows simulated, both outputs correct.

The sum alone is 8 gates and the carry alone 6, so separately they are 14. Sharing two signals takes it to 9.

Example 5 — GATE CSE 2009, worked

$$AB + C$$ in two-input NOR gates only.

1. A NOR gate negates a sum, so a NOR-NOR circuit builds a product of sums. Distribute: $$AB + C = (A+C)(B+C)$$.

2. Gate 1: $$\overline{A+C}$$. Gate 2: $$\overline{B+C}$$. Gate 3: $$\overline{\bar{(A+C)} + \bar{(B+C)}}$$, which is the product.

3. Three gates, no complement of any input needed.

4. Two gates cannot do it: 42 two-gate NOR circuits with literals only, 128 with complements, none works.

The official key is 3, and it does not depend on the complements clause here: the search says 2 is impossible in both settings.

Example 6 — GATE CSE 2019, worked

A four-variable function in two-input NOR gates, complements available.

1. Group the ones: $$f = \Sigma(0,2,5,7,8,10,13,15)$$ collapses to $$B\odot D$$. Variables $$A$$ and $$C$$ drop out.

2. So the four-variable question is a two-variable one.

3. Gate 1: $$\overline{\bar B + D} = B\bar D$$. Gate 2: $$\overline{B + \bar D} = \bar B D$$. Gate 3 combines them into $$B\odot D$$.

4. Three gates, and the search says XNOR in NOR cannot be done in two.

The folklore figure of four for XNOR in NOR assumes the complements are not supplied. Here the question supplied them.

Edge case 1 — the answer can be zero

If the function simplifies to a single supplied literal, no gate is needed at all. $$A + AB' + AB'C$$ absorbs twice to $$A$$, and the answer is 0.

Zero is a legitimate answer, and it is the one most often missed because the arithmetic starts at one.

Mathematical derivation $$A + AB' = A$$ by absorption, and $$A + A(B'C) = A$$ again. The function is the input $$A$$, so the “circuit” is a wire.

Edge case 2 — the answer can be one, on a function you did not expect

$$x \odot y$$ (XNOR) looks complicated and costs 5 NAND gates with literals only. But $$\overline{\bar a \cdot c}$$ is one NOR gate, and it is a perfectly ordinary function.

The one-gate test is therefore worth running before any minimisation: is the function the negation of a product, or of a sum, of two supplied signals?

Mathematical derivation $$\overline{a + \bar c} = \bar a \cdot c$$ by De Morgan, so one NOR gate computes it whenever $$\bar c$$ is supplied.

Edge case 3 — the constants are not free

With only the literals supplied, the constant functions are not available for nothing: the page’s search puts them at 2 and 3 gates. A circuit output that must be permanently 0 or permanently 1 is a wire to a rail, and a rail is a supplied signal.

So “how many gates for the function 0” has no single answer either: it is 0 if the rail is supplied, and 2 or 3 if it must be built from literals.

Mathematical derivation The classification covers all sixteen two-variable functions, constants included; two of them cost nothing only because they are supplied as $$x$$ and $$y$$.

Edge case 4 — the same function, two gate types, four answers

$$x \odot y$$ costs 5 in NAND with literals, 3 in NAND with complements, 4 in NOR with literals, 3 in NOR with complements. Four numbers, one function, no contradiction.

$$x \cdot y$$ is the opposite case: 2 in NAND either way, but 3 in NOR with literals and 1 with complements. A gate type that contains an AND cannot be helped by supplying complements for an AND; one that contains an OR can.

Mathematical derivation NAND contains AND, so AND needs an extra inversion that complements cannot remove. NOR contains OR, so $$\bar x \cdot \bar y$$ is one NOR of the supplied complements.

Edge case 5 — “gates” without “two-input”

If the question says “2-input NAND gates”, a three-input NAND is three gates. If it says only “NAND gates” with no fan-in limit, a three-input NAND is one gate and the whole count changes.

$$AB+CD+EF$$ is 6 gates under a two-input limit and 4 gates if a three-input NAND is one gate.

Educational interpretation Both numbers are correct for their own reading of the question. The fan-in phrase is part of the cost model, not decoration.

Edge case 6 — a don’t care is only free where the input cannot occur

GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 gives a map with don’t-care cells, and the collapse to two literals — and then to one gate — uses them. That is legitimate because the question states those input combinations do not arise.

Spending a don’t care on a combination that can arise produces a circuit that is wrong on that row, and the count is meaningless.

Mathematical derivation A don’t care is a row on which the specification has no value, so any output there satisfies the specification. A row with a specified value does not have that freedom.

“XOR always needs four NAND gates”

Why it tempts. It is true under the commonest convention, it is in every textbook table, and it is the answer to a real previous-year question.

Minimal counterexample. With $$\bar x$$ and $$\bar y$$ supplied, three gates suffice: $$\overline{\bar x y}$$, $$\overline{x \bar y}$$, and one gate to combine them. The page’s exhaustive search finds 3 and proves 2 impossible.

The correct rule. The count is four when the complements must be built, three when they are supplied. Read the clause before you answer.

Mathematical derivation Both circuits are in Example 2 and both were simulated on all four rows.

“Minimise the literals, then count the gates”

Why it tempts. Minimisation is the topic just before this one, and it produces a smaller expression, which feels like a smaller circuit.

Minimal counterexample. The full adder’s sum, $$A\oplus B\oplus C$$, has a four-term sum of products. The shared circuit is 9 gates for both outputs; the minimal-cover route gives 8 for the sum alone.

The correct rule. Literal count, term count and gate count are three different costs. Minimise the one the question names.

Educational interpretation The existing lesson on minimisation says the same thing in one line and does not compute the gate count; this lesson is that computation.

“A gate is a gate, whatever its width”

Why it tempts. The schematic shows one symbol, and the symbol is one gate.

Minimal counterexample. $$AB+CD+EF$$: three product gates plus “one” final gate is 4 by this belief, and 6 in fact, because a three-input NAND is three two-input gates.

The correct rule. Expand every gate wider than the stated fan-in before counting: $$2k-3$$ for a $$k$$-input NAND, $$2k-2$$ for a $$k$$-input AND.

Mathematical derivation The formula is verified by construction for $$k = 3$$ (3 gates) and $$k = 4$$ (5 gates) on the page.

“Count each output separately, then add”

Why it tempts. Each output is a function, and each function has a minimum.

Minimal counterexample. The full adder: 8 for the sum, 6 for the carry, 14 apart, 9 together.

The correct rule. When the question asks for a circuit with two outputs, the two minima are not additive. A signal computed once may feed both.

Mathematical derivation The saving of five is exactly the gates shared: $$\overline{AB}$$ and $$A\oplus B$$.

“NAND and NOR are dual, so the counts match”

Why it tempts. Duality is real: swap AND with OR, and NAND with NOR, and a true statement stays true.

Minimal counterexample. The duality maps the statement “AND needs 2 NAND gates” to “OR needs 2 NOR gates” — not to “AND needs 2 NOR gates”, which is false (it needs 3).

The correct rule. Duality swaps the function as well as the gate. Read the dual row, not the same row in the other column.

Mathematical derivation The four columns of the table on the Animation tab show all four readings; no two of them are equal column-wise.

“The table is the answer”

Why it tempts. The table is short, memorable and usually right.

Minimal counterexample. GATE CSE 2019 asks for XNOR in two-input NOR gates. The table says 4. The key is 3, because the question supplies the complements.

The correct rule. Use the table only after checking which of its assumptions the question preserved. The clause is the question; the table is a lookup.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2019, a numerical-answer item: $$f=\Sigma(0,2,5,7,8,10,13,15)$$, “assume that all the inputs and their complements are available”, key 3.

Pattern A — a function and a gate type, count the gates

The commonest shape. “The minimum number of 2-input NAND gates required to implement $$f$$ is ______.”

What it demands: simplify; run the one-gate test; match the form (sum of products to NAND-NAND, product of sums to NOR-NOR); expand wide gates; count.

The common wrong approach: minimise the literal count and report the number of terms plus one.

Seen in 2009 ($$AB+C$$ in NOR, key 3) and in 2019 (a four-variable function in NOR, key 3).

Pattern B — the clause about complements

“Assume that all the inputs and their complements are available”, or its absence. The clause moves the answer by one or two gates.

What it demands: before counting, decide whether a complemented literal is a wire or a gate.

The common wrong approach: answering from the memorised table, which assumes the complements are absent.

Seen in 2017 Set 1 (complements available, key 1) and 2019 (complements available, key 3 — the table would say 4).

Pattern C — a K-map with don’t cares, then a gate type

The map does two jobs: it simplifies, and it may collapse the function to one or two literals, which is where a count of one comes from.

What it demands: group with the don’t cares, read the surviving literals, then apply the one-gate test to the result.

The common wrong approach: minimising without the don’t cares, giving a larger function and a larger count.

GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 is exactly this, and the collapse to $$\bar a c$$ is what makes the answer 1.

Pattern D — a named block

“Minimum number of 2-input NAND gates for a 2-input XOR”, or for a half adder, a full adder, a 2-to-1 multiplexer.

What it demands: the named constructions. XOR 4 (or 3 with complements); half adder 5 for both outputs; full adder 9 for both outputs.

The common wrong approach: adding the two output minima, which overcounts shared gates.

The full-adder figure of 9 is a construction this lesson verifies; it does not claim to have proved it minimal.

Pattern E — the answer is zero or one

A function that absorbs to a literal costs nothing; a function that is the negation of a product or a sum costs one.

What it demands: the one-gate test, run before any other counting.

The common wrong approach: building the expression as written, which charges gates for terms that absorption removes.

Absorption to a single literal appears in several papers; on this page it is Edge case 1.

What the papers actually say, and what they do not

Verified as GATE CSE, this run: 2009 Q6 ($$AB+C$$ in two-input NOR, key 3); 2017 Set 1 Q21 (K-map with don’t cares, two-input NOR, complements available, key 1); 2019 (four-variable function in two-input NOR, complements available, key 3).

Q1 — the clause decides

The minimum number of two-input NAND gates required to implement $$x \oplus y$$, when only $$x$$ and $$y$$ are available, is:

(a) 3   (b) 4   (c) 5   (d) 6

Answer: (b), 4

Gate 1 makes $$\overline{xy}$$; gates 2 and 3 push $$x$$ and $$y$$ against it; gate 4 combines. The page’s exhaustive search confirms 4 and proves 3 and 2 impossible when the complements are absent.

(a) is the answer when the complements are supplied — the trap in the other direction. (c) is XNOR’s figure. (d) has no construction.

Trap: if the question had said the complements were available, (a) would be right. Read that clause first.

Q2 — the wide gate

Using only two-input NAND gates, the minimum number needed to implement a four-input NAND gate is ______.

Answer: 5

A $$k$$-input NAND costs $$2k-3$$ two-input gates, so $$2(4)-3 = 5$$. The construction: two NAND gates make $$\overline{ab}$$ and $$\overline{cd}$$; two inverters turn them into $$ab$$ and $$cd$$; one gate combines them into $$\overline{abcd}$$.

The common wrong answer is 3 — a balanced tree of NAND gates, which computes $$ab + cd$$, not $$\overline{abcd}$$.

MCQ original

Q3 — the shared output

A full adder is built with two-input NAND gates. The sum alone needs 8 gates and the carry alone 6. The minimum this lesson’s construction uses for both outputs together is:

(a) 8   (b) 9   (c) 14   (d) 12

Answer: (b), 9

Gates 1 to 4 make $$A\oplus B$$ and leave $$\overline{AB}$$; gate 5 makes $$\overline{(A\oplus B)C}$$; gates 6 to 8 turn that into the sum; gate 9 combines gate 1 with gate 5 into the carry. Both outputs were checked on all eight rows.

(c) is the separate-build total, 14. (a) cannot give both outputs. (d) is a plausible-looking number with no construction.

9 is a verified construction, not a proved minimum; the page says so rather than borrowing a claim.

MSQ original

Q4 — which statements hold

Select every statement that is true of gate counting under the cost model of this lesson.

(a) A $$k$$-input AND costs $$2k-2$$ two-input NAND gates.
(b) With complements supplied, AND in two-input NOR costs one gate.
(c) With complements supplied, AND in two-input NAND costs one gate.
(d) Two outputs always cost at least the sum of their separate minima.
(e) A function that simplifies to one literal needs no gate.

Answer: (a), (b) and (e)

(a) holds: $$2k-2$$, verified for $$k=3$$ at 4 gates. (b) holds: $$\overline{\bar x + \bar y} = x\cdot y$$ is one NOR gate. (e) holds: it is a wire.

(c) fails: NAND contains AND, so AND still needs an extra inversion — 2 gates either way. (d) fails: sharing makes the pair cheaper, 9 against 14.

(c) is the interesting one: supplying complements helps the gate type that lacks your operation, not the one that has it.

Q5 — the 2009 item

What is the minimum number of gates required to implement the Boolean function $$(AB + C)$$ if we have to use only 2-input NOR gates?

Answer: 3

$$AB + C = (A+C)(B+C) = \overline{\overline{(A+C)} + \overline{(B+C)}}$$. Two NOR gates make the inverted sums; the third combines them. No complement of any input is needed.

Two gates cannot do it: the page searches every two-gate NOR circuit in three variables, with and without complements, and finds none.

Provenance: GATE CSE 2009, Question 6, one mark, single-correct. The official key is (B) 3.

Q6 — the 2019 item

What is the minimum number of 2-input NOR gates required to implement a 4-variable function expressed in sum-of-minterms form as $$f = \Sigma(0,2,5,7,8,10,13,15)$$? Assume that all the inputs and their complements are available.

Answer: 3

The map collapses to $$B \odot D$$, so only two variables survive. Gate 1: $$\overline{\bar B + D} = B\bar D$$. Gate 2: $$\overline{B + \bar D} = \bar B D$$. Gate 3 combines them.

Provenance: GATE CSE 2019, numerical-answer type. Key 3. The memorised table gives 4 for XNOR in NOR, because it assumes the complements are not supplied.

Q7 — the 2017 item, in the abstract

A four-variable function has exactly four 1-cells, at $$m_2, m_3, m_{10}, m_{11}$$, and the rest are 0. Complements of all inputs are available. Implemented using 2-input NOR gates only, the minimum number of gates is ______.

Answer: 1

Grouping gives $$\bar a c$$, and $$\bar a c = \overline{a + \bar c}$$, which is one NOR gate because $$\bar c$$ is supplied.

This is the same shape as the 2017 Set 1 question, restated with explicit minterms; the original gives the same cells as a map with don’t cares. The original’s official key is 1.

MCQ original

Q8 — the counterexample

Which statement is FALSE?

(a) XOR needs more NAND gates than AND does, under every one of the four cost models in this lesson.
(b) A circuit with fewer gates is always faster.
(c) The minimal sum of products always gives the minimal gate count.
(d) All three are false.

Answer: (d)

(a) is true, and it is worth checking rather than assuming: the table gives XOR as 4, 3, 5, 3 and AND as 2, 2, 3, 1, so XOR is larger in all four columns.

(b) is false. The four-gate XOR is deeper than the three-gate one, so a smaller circuit can be the faster one and a larger circuit the slower — gate count and delay are different measures.

(c) is false. The full adder shares $$\overline{AB}$$ and $$A\oplus B$$ across two outputs; no single-output cover can express that sharing, and the pair costs 9 rather than $$8+6$$.

Two statements are false, so (d) is the answer. The point of the question: a gate count is neither a delay nor a cover size.

The procedure, four steps

  1. Read the clause. Gate type, fan-in limit, complements supplied, reuse.
  2. Simplify, then test one gate. Is it a literal (0 gates)? The negation of a product or a sum (1 gate)?
  3. Match the form. Sum of products to NAND-NAND; product of sums to NOR-NOR.
  4. Count, expand wide gates, simulate. $$2k-3$$ for a $$k$$-input NAND.

Every previous-year question on this page was decided by step 1.

The named constructions

  • NOT: 1 gate (tie the inputs), or 0 if the complement is supplied.
  • AND: 2 NAND, 3 NOR (or 1 NOR with complements).
  • OR: 3 NAND, 2 NOR (or 1 NAND with complements).
  • XOR: 4 NAND, 5 NOR (3 of either with complements).
  • XNOR: 5 NAND, 4 NOR (3 of either with complements).
  • $$AB+CD$$: 3 NAND. $$AB+CD+EF$$: 6 NAND.
  • Half adder, both outputs: 5 NAND.
  • Full adder, both outputs: 9 NAND (sum 8, carry 6 separately).

The half-adder figure of 5 is the standard construction and is not proved minimal on this page; neither is the 9.

Four traps, one line each

  • Counting a wide gate as one gate — expand it first.
  • Answering from the table when the question supplied complements.
  • Adding two output minima instead of sharing.
  • Forgetting that the answer can be 0 or 1.

And one assumption: every number here assumes two inputs per gate and free reuse. Change either and the numbers move.

What was not verified, and what is a construction of this lesson

The exhaustive search is run for one and two variables, where it visits every circuit up to the stated depth. Every minimum in the four-column table is therefore a proved minimum. For three variables and more, the lesson builds and simulates circuits but does not claim minimality: the 9-gate full adder, the 5-gate half adder and the 3-gate and 4-gate wide-gate expansions are constructions, verified by simulation, and labelled as such.

The only three-variable minima claimed are those where a bounded search to one less than the claimed count was exhaustive: $$AB+C$$ in two-input NOR cannot be done in 2 (42 circuits searched with literals only, 128 with complements), and $$\bar a c$$ in two-input NOR cannot be done in 0.

The cost model itself — two inputs per gate, free reuse, complements either supplied or not — is stated in the Build-up tab and applied identically to every number.

What was computed rather than quoted

Every number on this page was computed at load by the page’s own model: four exhaustive breadth-first searches over two-variable circuits (both gate types, both supplied-signal settings), a complete classification of all sixteen functions, fourteen constructions each re-simulated on every row, and six bounded searches for lower bounds.

The model re-checks itself in 63 named checks, which run on every page load and are reported on the Animation tab. They compare every table cell against the search, every construction against its function row by row, every gate count against the circuit that produced it, and every claimed lower bound against a search that was exhaustive.

If a check ever fails, the Animation tab says so in words rather than drawing a number that is not true.
