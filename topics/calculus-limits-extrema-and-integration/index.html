Topic and objective

TOPIC: Calculus — limits, continuity, differentiability, the mean value theorem, maxima and minima, and integration. Section 1, Engineering Mathematics, GATE 2027 CS syllabus.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide what a limit is by substituting first and recognising the indeterminate forms, and know which two forms license L'Hopital's rule. Test continuity at a join of a piecewise definition and solve for the unknown constant. Test differentiability by comparing two one-sided slopes, and find every point where a piecewise function has none. Use the mean value theorem as an inequality to bound a function's value at an endpoint. List every candidate for an extremum on a closed interval — critical points, corners, endpoints — and classify by the sign of the first derivative. Evaluate a definite integral by substitution, carrying the sign and changing the limits, and recognise the symmetry that makes an integral zero without any antiderivative.

I read this clause as four decisions and one habit: is the limit a number, where is the function broken, where does the graph turn, and what does the area come to. The habit is the candidate list — every question in this topic is settled by writing down all the candidates before computing any of them.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A limit is about a neighbourhood. Substitute first; a 0/0 answer means substitution failed, not that the limit is missing.
  • L'Hopital has two entry conditions. Only 0/0 and ∞/∞. Everything else is rewritten into one of those.
  • Differentiable means continuous; the converse fails. Compare the two one-sided slopes to find the failure points.
  • Extrema need a candidate list. Critical points, corners, and both endpoints — then compare values, never slopes.
  • Integrals are decided before they are computed. Odd over a symmetric interval is zero, and a substitution must carry its minus sign and its new limits.

Level 1 — Intuition, before any notation

A limit is what a value is heading towards. Stand at the edge of a road that has a hole in it: the cars come from both sides and pass close to the hole without ever falling in. The limit is where they pass; the hole is why the function is undefined there. That picture is the whole definition, and it is why the value at the point never appears in it.

A derivative is the steepness of that road at one place. At a sharp corner the road has two steepnesses, one from each side, so there is no single answer and we say the derivative does not exist. An integral is the area under the road, counted negative below the axis. Once you have those three pictures, the rules below are bookkeeping.

Level 2 — The formal definitions

Limit. The limit of f at a is L when, for every tolerance, there is a small distance such that every x within that distance of a — but not equal to a — has f(x) within the tolerance of L. The phrase "not equal to a" is the entire content: the definition is deliberately blind to the value at a.

Continuity. f is continuous at a when the limit exists, f(a) exists, and the two are equal. One equation, three requirements.

Derivative. f′(a) is the limit of ( f(a+h) − f(a) ) / h as h tends to 0. Because it is a limit, it is two-sided.

Definite integral. The signed area between the curve and the axis, defined as a limit of sums of thin rectangles. It is a number, not a function.

Level 3 — What each theorem actually assumes

L'Hopital. Both pieces differentiable near a; both limits zero, or both infinite; and the quotient of derivatives has a limit. Drop the last condition and the rule says nothing — it does not say the original limit fails.

Mean value theorem. Continuous on the closed interval, differentiable on the open one. That is all, and both are used: continuity at the ends makes the average slope exist, differentiability inside supplies the tangent.

Extreme value theorem. A continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains both a maximum and a minimum. This is why the endpoint is always a candidate, and why an open interval can fail to have a maximum at all.

Fermat. An interior local extremum of a differentiable function forces the derivative to vanish there. It is one-directional: vanishing does not force an extremum.

Level 4 — Four worked examples, one line each

Limit. (√(2x+22) − 4)/(x+3) at x = −3 gives 0/0; the derivative quotient is 1/√(2x+22), which is 1/4 at −3.

Continuity. c1ex − c2 ln(1/x) must meet 3 at 0; the logarithm diverges, so c2 = 0 and then c1 = 3.

Extrema. x3 + 15x2 − 33x − 36 has f′ = 3(x+11)(x−1): a maximum at −11 and a minimum at 1.

Integral. ∫ cos(1/x)/x2 dx with u = 1/x becomes −∫ cos u du, and the limits and the sign together give −1.

Level 5 — Boundaries and constraints

Each tool has a domain of validity, and the GATE question is usually built by stepping just outside it. L'Hopital needs a quotient and an indeterminate form; applying it to a product is not a rule at all. The mean value theorem needs continuity at the two endpoints, which is why 1/x on the interval from −1 to 1 is the standard counterexample: the average slope is +1 while every actual slope is negative. The extreme value theorem needs the interval closed and bounded, so 1/x on the open interval from 0 to 1 has no maximum. The symmetry rule for integrals needs both an odd integrand and an interval symmetric about zero; an interval from 0 to 2 satisfies neither.

A useful habit: before using any theorem, say its hypotheses out loud and check them against the question. Half the marks in this topic are lost by using a true theorem in a place where its assumptions are false, and the other half by not using one where they hold.

Level 6 — The counterexample to carry into the exam

Take f(x) = 1/x on the interval from −1 to 1. Its average slope is ( f(1) − f(−1) ) / 2 = ( 1 − (−1) ) / 2 = 1. Its derivative is −1/x2, which is negative everywhere it exists. So no interior point has slope 1, and the mean value theorem's conclusion is false here.

Nothing is wrong with the theorem: the function is not continuous at 0, so a hypothesis fails. Mathematical derivation This is the fastest way to see why continuity on the closed interval is not decoration, and it is the answer to any option of the form "the theorem holds for every differentiable function".

Levels 7 and 8 — How it is tested, and where to practise

The one-line limit. A numerical answer, usually with a rounded value, and the mark turns on whether you used the right form.

The inequality. A theorem is stated in words and one bound is asked for; the trap is a bound that is valid but not tight.

The classification. A multiple-select item on a cubic; the trap is stopping after the first critical point.

The symmetry. An integral or a triple integral that is zero, where the arithmetic punishes anyone who expands it. Practice is the Practice tab: twelve items, answers hidden until you ask, each with the reason the wrong options are wrong.

Level 9 — Consolidation

Four habits cover this topic. Substitute first, and treat 0/0 as a signal rather than an answer. Compare two sides whenever a definition mentions a limit — that is what continuity, differentiability and one-sided limits all have in common. Write the candidate list before computing anything, because a missing endpoint or a missing corner costs the whole mark. Look before you integrate, because symmetry and an inner derivative are both visible in the integrand and neither requires an antiderivative.

A limit where substitution fails — GATE CSE 2021, Set 1 (1-mark numerical)

PYQ Consider limx→−3 ( √(2x+22) − 4 ) / ( x+3 ). The value, rounded to two decimal places, is ______.

Step 1. Substitute. The numerator is √16 − 4 = 0 and the denominator is 0. The form is 0/0, so substitution has failed.

Step 2. L'Hopital, because the form is one of the two that license it. The derivative of the numerator is (1/(2√(2x+22)))·2 = 1/√(2x+22); the derivative of the denominator is 1.

Step 3. Substitute again: 1/√16 = 1/4 = 0.25.

The alternative. Multiply top and bottom by √(2x+22) + 4. The numerator becomes 2x + 22 − 16 = 2(x+3), so the quotient is 2/(√(2x+22) + 4), and the factor x+3 has cancelled. At x = −3 that is 2/(4+4) = 2/8 = 0.25. The page's model computes both routes and both give 0.25; the seven-point approach table on the canvas is the third check, and it costs nothing.

The mean value theorem as an inequality — GATE CSE 2021, Set 2 (1-mark numerical)

PYQ f is continuous on [−3, 3] and differentiable on (−3, 3), with f′(x) ≤ 2 there. If f(−3) = 7, then f(3) is at most ______.

By the mean value theorem there is an interior c with ( f(3) − f(−3) ) / 6 = f′(c). Every interior slope is at most 2, so the left side is at most 2.

Hence f(3) − 7 ≤ 12, that is f(3) ≤ 19.

Why 19 is the answer and not just an estimate. The line 2x + 13 meets every hypothesis and reaches 19 at x = 3. A bound that is attained cannot be improved, which is what "at most" asks for. The width 6 is essential: it is what turns a bound on a slope into a bound on a value.

The classification that needs both points — GATE CSE 2023 (1-mark MSQ)

PYQ For f(x) = x3 + 15x2 − 33x − 36, which statements are true? A: no local maximum. B: has a local maximum. C: no local minimum. D: has a local minimum.

f′(x) = 3x2 + 30x − 33 = 3(x+11)(x−1), so the critical points are −11 and 1. The sign of f′ is +, then −, then +: climb, fall, climb.

A climb then a fall is a local maximum at −11; a fall then a climb is a local minimum at 1. The second derivative, 6x+30, is −36 and +36 at those points, which confirms it. Answer: B and D. Answering only B because you stopped at the first critical point is the standard half-mark loss.

The integral with a sign — GATE CSE 2015, Set 1 (2-mark numerical)

PYQ Evaluate ∫ from 1/π to 2/π of cos(1/x)/x2 dx.

Put u = 1/x, so du = −dx/x2, that is dx/x2 = −du. The limits become π and π/2, and the minus sign reverses them into π/2 up to π.

So the integral is ∫π/2π cos u du = sin π − sin(π/2) = 0 − 1 = −1.

The page's model integrates the original integrand by Simpson's rule on 4000 panels and gets −1 to nine decimal places, which is the check that the sign was carried correctly. Dropping the minus produces +1, and that is exactly the distractor the paper offered.

Symmetry instead of arithmetic — GATE CSE 2023 (1-mark numerical)

PYQ The value of the triple integral of (4x2y − z3) over x ∈ [−3, 3], y ∈ [−2, 2], z ∈ [−1, 1], rounded to the nearest integer, is ______.

The first term is odd in y over a symmetric interval, so its y-integral is 0. The second term is odd in z over a symmetric interval, so its z-integral is 0.

Both vanish, so the answer is 0. A symmetric midpoint grid in the page's model cancels to zero as well, which is the numerical confirmation of the argument. Nobody who spots this needs an antiderivative.

Continuity fixing a constant — GATE CSE 2026, Set 1 (1-mark numerical)

PYQ f(x) = c1ex − c2 ln(1/x) for x > 0, and 3 otherwise. If f is continuous at 0, then c1 + c2 = ______.

As x → 0+, ex → 1 while ln(1/x) = −ln x → +∞. A term that diverges cannot be matched to the finite value 3, so its coefficient must be zero: c2 = 0.

Then the limit is c1, and continuity needs c1 = 3. So c1 + c2 = 3. No derivative is involved anywhere: continuity is a statement about values.

A set, not a number — GATE CSE 2024, Set 1 (1-mark MCQ)

PYQ Where is f(x) = max{x, x3} not differentiable?

The two expressions cross where x3 = x, that is x(x−1)(x+1) = 0, so at −1, 0 and 1. At each crossing the winning branch changes, so each is a candidate.

The one-sided slopes there are (1, 3) at −1, (0, 1) at 0 and (1, 3) at 1. No pair agrees, so all three are corners and the answer is the set {−1, 0, 1}. Everywhere else the function agrees with a polynomial on a neighbourhood, so it is smooth. The page's model recomputes all three slope pairs numerically.

The endpoint that beats the vertex — GATE CSE 1997 (2 marks)

PYQ What is the maximum value of f(x) = 2x2 − 2x + 6 on the interval [0, 2]?

f′(x) = 4x − 2 vanishes at x = 1/2, where f = 5.5. The parabola opens upward, so this interior point is the minimum, not the maximum.

The endpoints give f(0) = 6 and f(2) = 10. Comparing 6, 5.5 and 10, the maximum is 10, at the right endpoint. The question is testing exactly the habit of writing all candidates down.

Two more limits, with the form named first — GATE CSE 2015 Set 1 and 2022

PYQ GATE CSE 2015 Set 1: the value of limx→∞ x1/x is (A) ∞ (B) 0 (C) 1 (D) not defined. The form is ∞0, which is not a quotient, so take logarithms: ln y = (ln x)/x, which is ∞/∞. One application of L'Hopital gives (1/x)/1 → 0, so y → e0 = 1. Answer C.

PYQ GATE CSE 2022: the value of limx→0+ √x/(1 − e2√x) is ______. Substitute t = √x → 0+: the expression is t/(1 − e2t), a 0/0 form, and L'Hopital gives 1/(−2e2t) → −1/2. The page's model reproduces −0.5 at x = 10−8.

Three small ones worth one line each

PYQ GATE CSE 2016 Set 1: limx→4 sin(x−4)/(x−4) = 1. The standard limit, with the shift absorbed into the variable.

PYQ GATE CSE 2019: limx→3 (x4−81)/(2x2−5x−3). Both vanish at 3; L'Hopital gives 4x3/(4x−5) = 108/7 ≈ 15.43. Cancelling instead, (x−3)(x3+3x2+9x+27) over (2x+1)(x−3), gives the same 108/7.

PYQ GATE CSE 2018: ∫0π/4 x cos(x2) dx with π = 3.14. Put u = x2, so du = 2x dx, and the integral is ½ sin(π2/16) = 0.289 to three decimals. No minus sign appears, because the inner derivative is positive here — which is why the sign must be derived each time rather than remembered.

What these eight items have in common

Every one of them is decided before it is computed. The limit questions are decided by naming the form; the continuity question by noticing which term diverges; the differentiability question by solving one cubic equation; the extremum question by writing the candidate list; and the integral questions by looking for symmetry or an inner derivative. The arithmetic is short in all eight. Educational interpretation That is the shape of the section in the paper: the marks are for the decision, and the computation is a formality.

Limits: the four ways a limit can fail to be a number

It exists while the function does not. (√(2x+22)−4)/(x+3) at x = −3. The limit is 1/4; the function is undefined. Both facts are true at once.

It does not exist: a jump. The sign function at 0 has left limit −1 and right limit 1. Two different numbers means no two-sided limit.

It does not exist: an oscillation. sin(1/x) at 0 never settles, so no tolerance can be met.

It is infinite. 1/x2 at 0 has no finite limit; writing ∞ is a description of the failure, not a value.

Continuity and differentiability: the boundary between them

Continuous, not differentiable: a corner. |x| at 0, slopes −1 and +1.

Continuous, not differentiable: a vertical tangent. The cube root at 0; the difference quotients diverge rather than disagree.

Differentiable but the derivative is not continuous. A function can have a derivative everywhere and a derivative that jumps; differentiability of f says nothing about the continuity of f′.

Neither. A jump discontinuity destroys continuity and therefore the derivative, because differentiability implies continuity.

The theorem that fails when a hypothesis is dropped

f(x) = 1/x on [−1, 1]. Average slope (1 − (−1))/2 = 1. Derivative −1/x2 < 0 wherever it exists. No interior point has slope 1, so the mean value theorem's conclusion fails.

The hypothesis that failed is continuity on the closed interval, at x = 0. Mathematical derivation Any option claiming the theorem applies to "every differentiable function" is false, and this is the one-line refutation.

Extrema: four boundary cases

Derivative zero, no extremum. x3 at 0. The sign of f′ does not change.

Extremum, no derivative. 1 − |x| at 0. A corner can be the peak.

No maximum at all. 1/x on the open interval (0, 1). It is unbounded above, which is why the extreme value theorem asks for a closed interval.

Maximum at an endpoint. 2x2−2x+6 on [0, 2] peaks at x = 2, while its only critical point is the minimum.

Integration: when the shortcuts do not apply

Not symmetric. ∫02 x3 dx is not zero, although x3 is odd. The interval decides the rule, not the function.

Symmetric but not odd. An even integrand over a symmetric interval doubles the half-interval; it does not vanish.

Improper. ∫01 1/x dx diverges, so there is no area to report. Bounded integrand is a hypothesis, not a courtesy.

Definite, not indefinite. A definite integral is a number. Writing "+ C" in a definite answer is a category error, and the constant cancels in the subtraction anyway.

Two pairs that are constantly confused

Local versus global. A local maximum is the largest value in some small neighbourhood; a global maximum is the largest on the whole interval. GATE CSE 2023 asks about local ones, and the two answers differ: the cubic's global maximum on the real line does not exist at all.

The point versus the value. "Where does it attain the maximum" asks for an x; "what is the maximum" asks for f(x). GATE CSE 2015 Set 2 (aptitude) offered both as separate numbers in one option, and mixing them up was the whole trap. Read the last four words of the question.

“0/0 means the limit does not exist”

Why it feels right. Dividing by zero is forbidden, so a 0/0 answer looks like a breakdown.

What is actually true. 0/0 means the substitution rule does not apply, because the function is not continuous at that point in the form you substituted into. The limit may well exist: the 2021 question has limit 1/4.

The correction. Treat 0/0 as an instruction: factor, rationalise, or differentiate. It is the start of the work, not the end of it.

“L'Hopital works on any limit”

Why it feels right. It works on almost every example in the textbook, and the textbook examples are chosen to be indeterminate.

What is actually true. It is a theorem about a quotient whose two parts both tend to zero or both to infinity. Applied to a product, there is no quotient to differentiate. Applied where the form is 1/2, it gives the wrong number.

The correction. Name the form in writing before differentiating. If the name is not "0/0" or "∞/∞", rewrite first.

“If the derivative is zero, it is an extremum”

Why it feels right. Every worked example of a maximum in a first course comes from setting f′ = 0.

What is actually true. x3 at 0 has f′ = 0 and no extremum. Fermat's theorem is one-directional: an interior extremum forces a zero derivative, not the other way round.

The correction. Always finish with a sign change of f′ or the sign of f″. A zero derivative is a candidate, and a candidate is not a conclusion.

“The critical point is where the maximum is”

Why it feels right. On an open interval it often is, and on a parabola opening downward it always is.

What is actually true. On a closed interval the maximum can be at an endpoint — 10 at x = 2 for 2x2−2x+6 on [0, 2], while the critical point gives 5.5. And on a parabola opening upward the critical point is the minimum.

The correction. Evaluate at every candidate and compare values. The candidate list is critical points, corners and both endpoints; the answer is the largest of the values.

“Differentiable and continuous are the same thing”

Why it feels right. Every function drawn in a calculus book is smooth, and smooth functions are both.

What is actually true. Differentiable implies continuous; the converse fails at every corner. |x| at 0 is continuous and has no derivative, and max{x, x3} has three such points.

The correction. To disprove differentiability, produce two unequal one-sided slopes. To prove continuity, produce three equal numbers. They are different tests.

“An odd function always integrates to zero”

Why it feels right. The picture of the two cancelling areas is memorable and it is correct — on a symmetric interval.

What is actually true. ∫02 x3 dx = 4, not 0. Oddness alone does nothing; the interval must be symmetric about zero. An even function on a symmetric interval doubles instead of vanishing.

The correction. Check two things before invoking symmetry: the interval is [−a, a], and the integrand changes sign under x → −x. Then the answer is 0, and the reason is a cancellation, not a coincidence.

“Substitution is just renaming”

Why it feels right. The variable is a dummy, so renaming it should not matter.

What is actually true. In a definite integral, substituting changes the limits, and the differential brings a factor that may be negative. GATE CSE 2015 Set 1 is worth 2 marks and the entire difference between −1 and +1 is that minus sign.

The correction. Write three lines every time: the substitution, the differential with its sign, and the new limits. If the limits are still in x, the substitution is incomplete.

“A negative integral means I made a mistake”

Why it feels right. Areas feel positive, and a negative area is unsettling.

What is actually true. A definite integral is a signed area. Where the curve lies below the axis the contribution is negative, and the total can be negative — as it is in the 2015 question, where cos(1/x) is negative on part of the interval.

The correction. Before doubting the arithmetic, check the sign of the integrand. If it is negative somewhere on the interval, a negative answer is available and may well be right.

Pattern 1 — the one-line limit

Shape. A single quotient, a point where both parts vanish, and a numerical answer to two decimals or a choice among four constants.

Procedure. Name the form; if it is 0/0 or ∞/∞, differentiate both parts; substitute; round as asked.

The traps. Using the quotient rule instead of differentiating the parts separately; rounding a fraction to the wrong number of decimals; reporting the limit of a logarithm rather than of the original expression.

Pattern 2 — the theorem as an inequality

Shape. A theorem is described in words, one hypothesis gives a bound on a derivative, and one value of the function is asked for as "at most" or "at least".

Procedure. Write the conclusion with the interval width, substitute the bound, solve the inequality. Then ask whether the bound is attained, because "at most" wants the tight one.

The traps. Reversing the inequality; using the width of the wrong interval; giving a valid but loose bound.

Pattern 3 — the classification MSQ

Shape. A cubic, and four statements of the form "has / does not have a local maximum / minimum".

Procedure. Factor the derivative, list every critical point, read the sign chart, and answer for all of them at once.

The traps. Stopping after the first critical point; forgetting that a cubic always has at least one of each unless it is monotone; choosing a statement about global behaviour when the question says local.

Pattern 4 — the integral that is zero

Shape. A definite or multiple integral whose limits are symmetric about zero, with an integrand containing an odd power.

Procedure. Take each term separately and name the variable in which it is odd. If every term is odd in some variable over a symmetric interval, the answer is 0.

The traps. Expanding the integral and losing a sign; applying the rule to an interval that is not symmetric; missing that one term is odd and the other is not.

Pattern 5 — the piecewise constant

Shape. A function defined by one formula above a point and another below, with one or two unknown constants, and a condition of continuity or differentiability.

Procedure. Continuity gives one equation (the two limits and the value agree). Differentiability gives a second (the two slopes agree). Solve them in that order, because the slope equation needs the value equation to have been solved first.

The traps. Matching values but not slopes when differentiability is asked for; letting a divergent term survive with a free coefficient.

What a "best possible bound" question is really asking

Two claims, and both are needed. An upper bound: here is the inequality the theorem gives. Sharpness: here is a function meeting every hypothesis that attains it.

The 2021 mean value theorem item is exactly this, and the line 2x+13 is the sharpness witness. Without it you have shown 19 is allowed; with it you have shown nothing smaller is.

NATMediumPYQ: GATE CSE 2021 Set 2

f is continuous on [−3, 3] and differentiable on (−3, 3), and f′(x) ≤ 2 for every x there. If f(−3) = 7, then f(3) is at most ______.

The mean value theorem gives an interior c with ( f(3) − 7 )/6 = f′(c) ≤ 2, so f(3) ≤ 19.

Sharp, because 2x+13 satisfies both hypotheses and equals 19 at x = 3. A larger number would also be an upper bound but would not answer "at most".

MCQEasyPYQ: GATE CSE 2015 Set 1

limx→∞ x1/x is

  1. ∞
  2. 0
  3. 1
  4. not defined

Answer: C — 1

The form is ∞0, which is not a quotient. Take logs: ln y = (ln x)/x is ∞/∞, and one application of L'Hopital gives (1/x)/1 → 0. So y → e0 = 1.

A mistakes the slow growth for divergence; B reports the limit of the logarithm; D confuses "no elementary antiderivative" thinking with limits. The model evaluates 1.0000138 at x = 106.

NATMediumPYQ: GATE CSE 2026 Set 1

f(x) = c1ex − c2 ln(1/x) for x > 0, and f(x) = 3 otherwise. If f is continuous at x = 0, then c1 + c2 = ______.

Answer: 3

ln(1/x) → +∞ as x → 0+, so a finite limit forces c2 = 0. Then the limit is c1, which must equal 3.

Setting the limit to 3 while leaving c2 free gives one equation in two unknowns and cannot be solved; the vanishing coefficient is a separate requirement.

MCQMediumPYQ: GATE CSE 2024 Set 1

For f(x) = max{x, x3} on the real line, the set of points where f is NOT differentiable is

  1. {0}
  2. {−1, 1}
  3. {−1, 0, 1}
  4. the empty set

Answer: C — {−1, 0, 1}

The branches cross where x3 = x, that is at −1, 0 and 1. The one-sided slopes there are (1, 3), (0, 1) and (1, 3): no pair agrees.

A is the |x| reflex; B misses the crossing at 0; D assumes a maximum of two polynomials must be smooth.

MCQEasyPYQ: GATE CSE 1997

What is the maximum value of f(x) = 2x2 − 2x + 6 on the interval [0, 2]?

  1. 6
  2. 10
  3. 12
  4. 5.5

Answer: B — 10

The critical point is x = 1/2 with value 5.5, and the parabola opens upward, so that is the minimum. The endpoints give 6 and 10.

D is the vertex trap; A checks only the left endpoint; C is the value at the vertex of a differently scaled parabola.

NATMediumPYQ: GATE CSE 2022

limx→0+ √x / (1 − e2√x) = ______.

Answer: −0.5

Put t = √x, so the expression is t/(1 − e2t), a 0/0 form. L'Hopital gives 1/(−2e2t) → −1/2.

The sign surprises people because both pieces look positive; the denominator is negative for t > 0. The model reproduces −0.49995 at x = 10−8.

MSQMediumPYQ: GATE CSE 2020

Which of these functions is/are increasing everywhere on [0, 1]? I. e−x II. x2 − sin x III. √(x3 + 1)

  1. II and III only
  2. III only
  3. II only
  4. I and III only

Answer: B — III only

I: derivative −e−x < 0, decreasing. II: derivative 2x − cos x, which is −1 at x = 0, so it decreases near 0 before rising. III: derivative 3x2/(2√(x3+1)) ≥ 0 on [0, 1], and only 0 at x = 0.

The trap is II: it is increasing on [0.5, 1] but not on the whole interval, and "everywhere" is the word that decides it. A is the option for people who check only the right half.

NATMediumOriginal practice

How many points in the interval (−2, 2) are points where f(x) = |x2 − 1| is NOT differentiable?

Answer: 2

Inside (−2, 2) the inner expression x2 − 1 changes sign at x = −1 and x = 1, and nowhere else. At each, the one-sided slopes are −2x and +2x, which are 2 and −2 at x = −1, and −2 and 2 at x = 1.

This is an original question written for this lesson, not a previous-year item. It is the same construction as the 2024 question with a quadratic inside the absolute value, and it tests whether you find the sign changes rather than remembering the answer to the earlier one.

The six statements to know word for word

limx→a f = LFor every tolerance there is a distance; the point a itself is excluded.

continuous at a ⟺ lim = f(a)Three requirements in one equation.

f′(a) = limh→0 ( f(a+h) − f(a) ) / hTwo-sided, so two one-sided slopes must agree.

MVT: f′(c) = ( f(b) − f(a) ) / ( b − a )Needs continuity on [a,b] and differentiability on (a,b).

∫−aa odd = 0, ∫−aa even = 2∫0aThe interval must be symmetric; the parity decides which rule.

∫ f(g(x))g′(x)dx = ∫ f(u)duChange the limits and keep the sign that du brings.

The five indeterminate forms and their moves

0/0 and ∞/∞. L'Hopital directly — differentiate numerator and denominator separately, never by the quotient rule.

0 × ∞. Put one factor underneath: f g = f / (1/g).

∞ − ∞. One fraction over a common denominator, or rationalise.

1∞, ∞0, 00. Take logarithms, evaluate the exponent, then exponentiate. Never report the exponent's limit as the answer.

The three standard limits. sin x/x → 1; (1 − cos x)/x2 → 1/2; (1 + 1/n)n → e.

The candidate list, which is the whole of extrema

  1. Interior points where f′ = 0.
  2. Interior points where f′ does not exist.
  3. Both endpoints, if the interval is closed.
  4. Evaluate f at every candidate.
  5. Compare the values — never the slopes.

Classification, if a local question is asked, comes from the sign change of f′ or the sign of f″. A zero derivative is a candidate and nothing more.

Numbers worth remembering

1/√16 = 0.25 (2021 Set 1). 7 + 6·2 = 19 (2021 Set 2). c1+c2 = 3 (2026 Set 1). 2x+13 attains the MVT bound.

108/7 ≈ 15.43 (2019). 0.5 sin(π2/16) ≈ 0.289 (2018). 0.99 = 1 − 1/100 (2015, telescoping).

∫π/2π cos u du = −1. Symmetric odd integrals are 0. A parabola's vertex is the minimum when it opens upward — always check the endpoints.

The four-step procedure

1. Substitute. A number means the answer, because the function is continuous there.

2. Name the form. Only 0/0 and ∞/∞ license L'Hopital; rewrite the rest.

3. Write the candidate list. Critical points, corners, endpoints; compare values.

4. Look before integrating. Symmetry, an inner derivative, and the sign of du.

The one counterexample to keep

f(x) = 1/x on [−1, 1]: average slope +1, every actual slope negative, so the mean value theorem's conclusion fails — because f is not continuous at 0.

And x3 at 0 for the other direction: derivative zero, no extremum. Between them these two refute "the theorem holds for every differentiable function" and "a zero derivative is an extremum".
