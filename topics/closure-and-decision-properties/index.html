Topic and objective

TOPIC: Closure and decision properties of regular and context-free languages — which operations keep a language inside its class, which questions an algorithm can answer about a machine or grammar in that class, and the two pumping lemmas, which are the tool for proving that a language is outside a class. The five previous-year questions that sit on this clause are named on this page, each with its year, paper and keyed answer.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given two languages and an operation, say whether the result is guaranteed to stay in the class, and justify it with a construction or a witness. Given a language, decide which lemma applies and produce a witness that defeats every legal split. Given a proposed pumping length, decide whether it is too small. Given a grammar or automaton, name the procedure that answers emptiness, finiteness, membership, universality and equivalence, or say that no procedure exists.

I read the topic as two questions, two lemmas, one table. Closure is answered by a construction or a counterexample; decidability by a procedure or a theorem; and the pumping lemmas are the one-directional bridge between them. Prerequisites

  • DFA, NFA and regular expressions, and the subset construction — the regular languages lesson.
  • Context-free grammars, derivations and pushdown automata — the CFG and PDA lesson.
  • Basic set algebra: union, intersection, complement, difference, and De Morgan's laws.
  • The idea of an algorithm that always halts, which is the whole content of "decidable".

Approximate learning time: 75 to 100 minutes, including the practice set.

The five ideas this lesson turns on

  1. Closure is a claim about every pair in the class. Decidability is a claim about one object and one question.
  2. The pumping lemma is necessary and never sufficient. Failing it proves exclusion; passing it proves nothing.
  3. The pumping length is a property of the language. Any value at least as large as the smallest one also works.
  4. Context-free languages are closed under intersection with a regular language, and not with themselves. That asymmetry is the workhorse.
  5. Universality, equivalence and ambiguity are the three questions that separate the two classes.

Intuition: the club with a dress code

Educational interpretation A class of languages is a club. Closure asks: if two members walk in holding hands and you apply an operation to them, is the result still allowed in? Some operations are allowed for everyone, some for nobody, and some only when the partner is a particular kind of member.

Decidability is a different question about the same club: standing at the door with one member and one question, is there a procedure that always ends with a yes or a no? A club can be generous about operations and still have questions nobody can answer.

The pumping lemma is the bouncer's tape measure. If a member is too tall for the door, they are definitely not a member of that club. Being short enough does not get you in — the bouncer only ever rejects.

Formal: closed under an operation

A class C of languages is closed under an operation op when, for every choice of languages L1, L2 in C, the language L1 op L2 is also in C. Every word matters: "every choice", not "some choices".

A class is not closed when there exists at least one pair in the class whose result is outside it. One pair is a proof of non-closure; one construction that works for an arbitrary pair is a proof of closure.

Mathematical derivation Closure is inherited in one direction only. If C is closed under intersection and under complement, it is closed under difference, because L1 − L2 = L1 ∩ complement(L2). The converse fails: a class can be closed under difference with regular languages and not under difference in general, which is exactly the context-free case.

Formal: the pumping lemma for regular languages

If L is regular, there exists a number p — the pumping length — such that every w in L with length at least p can be written as w = x y z with:

|y| ≥ 1 (the pumped piece is not empty), |xy| ≤ p (the pumped piece is found near the front), and x yi z in L for every i ≥ 0 (pumping up and down stays inside).

The symbol |y| means the number of symbols in y; x yi z means x, then y written i times, then z. At i = 0 the piece y is deleted.

Mathematical derivation Why it holds: a DFA with p states that reads a word of length at least p visits some state twice, so the symbols between the two visits form a loop. Running the loop once more, or skipping it, ends in the same state, so the word is still accepted.

Formal: the pumping lemma for context-free languages

If L is context-free, there exists n such that every w in L with length at least n can be written as w = u v w x y with:

|vwx| ≤ n (the window is short), |vx| ≥ 1 (at least one of the two pumped pieces is non-empty), and u vi w xi y in L for every i ≥ 0.

Note that v and x are pumped together, with the same count. That is what makes this lemma weaker than the regular one: it can only stretch two pieces at once.

Mathematical derivation Why it holds: a long derivation in a grammar with finitely many nonterminals repeats one nonterminal on a single root-to-leaf path. The two pieces grown between the repetitions are v and x; the piece between them is w.

Formal: what "decidable" means

A question about a machine or grammar is decidable when some algorithm always halts and answers it correctly for every input in the class. It is undecidable when no such algorithm exists — not "nobody has found one", but "one cannot exist".

Six questions are asked on this clause: emptiness (does it generate anything), finiteness (is the language finite), membership (is this word in it), universality (is it everything), equivalence (do two of them agree), ambiguity (does a grammar have two trees for one word).

Mathematical derivation A question that quantifies over all words is much harder than one that asks about a single word. Membership is one word, and it is decidable for grammars. Equivalence is every word at once, and it is not.

Two conditions people forget

Complement needs a total DFA. Flipping the accepting states of an NFA complements nothing at all; flipping them on a partial DFA accepts words that used to crash. Determinise and add a sink first.

The CFL lemma needs |vx| ≥ 1, not |v| ≥ 1. Either piece may be empty. A proof that assumes both are non-empty is proving something stronger than the lemma, and it will not apply to grammars with unit rules.

Example 1 — anbn is not regular

1. Suppose it is regular, with pumping length p. Justification: proof by contradiction.

2. Take w = apbp. Justification: it is in the language and has length 2p ≥ p, so the lemma must apply to it.

3. The first p symbols of w are all a, so |xy| ≤ p forces y to be a non-empty block of a's. Justification: xy is a prefix of w of length at most p.

4. Then x y2 z has more a's than b's, so it is outside the language. Justification: pumping adds at least one a and no b.

5. No split survives, for any p. Contradiction, so the language is not regular. Justification: step 4 never used the value of p.

The page runs the same argument mechanically for p = 1 … 8 and finds no surviving split in any of the eight cases.

Example 2 — a pumping length that is too small

For L = {a2+3k} ∪ {b10+12k}, the 2019 question offers 3, 5, 9 and 24.

1. The shortest b-string in L is b10. Justification: k = 0 gives 10.

2. If p ≤ 10 then b10 is long enough to be tested, so it must be pumpable with |y| ≤ p < 10. Justification: the lemma applies to every word of length at least p.

3. Pumping gives length 10 + |y|, which must again be 10 + 12k, so |y| would have to be a positive multiple of 12. Justification: membership in the second set.

4. No positive multiple of 12 is below 10. So 3, 5 and 9 are all too small. Justification: arithmetic.

5. 24 is at least the minimum, which is 12, so 24 is a pumping length. Justification: any value at least the minimum works.

Example 3 — anbncn is not context-free

1. Suppose context-free, with constant n. Take w = anbncn, length 3n ≥ n.

2. The window vwx has length at most n, so it can cover symbols from at most two of the three blocks of n identical symbols.

3. Pumping changes the count of at most two of the three letters, since only v and x change.

4. The three counts were equal and are now not, so the pumped word is outside the language.

5. No split survives, for any n. The language is not context-free.

The page tries 6, 37, 121, 295, 605 and 1106 legal splits for n = 1 … 6; none survives.

Example 4 — two context-free languages whose intersection is not

L1 = {anbncm} is context-free: one stack matches the a's against the b's and the c's are unconstrained. L2 = {ambncn} is context-free for the mirror reason.

A word in both must have as many a's as b's (from L1) and as many b's as c's (from L2), so all three counts are equal:

L1 ∩ L2 = {anbncn}, which Example 3 shows is not context-free.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q38 asks exactly this pair and keys "I only": the union is context-free because the class is closed under union, and the intersection is not.

Example 5 — the product construction, counted

Take A with 3 states (number of a's divisible by 3) and B with 3 states (contains the substring ab), both over {a,b}.

1. States are pairs, so at most 3 × 3 = 9. Justification: run both machines at once.

2. Only pairs reachable from the start pair are built: the page keeps 7 of the 9. Justification: the construction explores by reachability.

3. Accepting is "either accepting" for union, "both" for intersection, "first yes and second no" for difference. Justification: the three definitions.

4. Minimisation then shows 5 states suffice for each. Justification: table filling on distinguishable pairs.

5. All three were compared with their definitions on 255 strings and agreed. Justification: finite but exhaustive up to length 7.

Example 6 — the [A,p,q] construction, counted

Grammar S → aSb | bSa | SS | ε (equal counts of a and b); automaton: even length, 2 states.

1. One nonterminal becomes |Q|² = 4 nonterminals, S[p,q].

2. Add a new start symbol S0 → S[q0,q] for each accepting q: 5 nonterminals in all.

3. For S → aSb, the terminal a forces the state step and the two nonterminals thread the rest: S[p,q] → a S[r,q] for every r.

4. The result has 19 productions and generates exactly the strings with equal counts and even length.

5. Checked on all 127 strings of length at most 6: no disagreement.

Because the output is a grammar, the result is context-free by definition — no stack argument is needed.

Counterexample: a language that passes the lemma and is not regular

L = {aibjck : i = 1 implies j = k} satisfies the pumping lemma from p = 2 upward — the page finds no failing member among 129, 125, 117 and 106 tried — and is not regular.

Proof of non-regularity. L ∩ a b*c* = {a bjcj}, which fails the lemma at every p with witness a bpcp. Regular languages are closed under intersection, so if L were regular this intersection would be too. It is not. So the lemma cannot be used to prove regularity.

Counterexample: a proposed pumping length that fails

For the same language, p = 1 fails on 24 of 132 members. The only legal split at p = 1 is y equal to the first symbol; pumping aab down gives ab, which has one a and therefore needs j = k.

This is a failed value of p, not a counterexample to the lemma. The lemma says some p exists; p = 2 works and every larger value works too.

Boundary: finite languages

Every finite language is regular, and for a finite language the pumping lemma is vacuous: choose p larger than the longest word and no word is long enough to be tested. The condition holds with nothing to check.

This is not a hole in the lemma — it is the lemma being satisfied by a language that really is regular. It is a reminder that the lemma only ever speaks about long words, which is why it can never distinguish a finite language from an infinite one.

Trap: complementing an NFA

Flipping the accepting states of an NFA does not complement its language. A word may have several runs, some ending in an accepting state and some not; flipping the set changes which runs count, not which words are accepted.

Determinise first, and add a sink state for missing transitions. On this page the two example automata are already total, so complement is a flip of the accepting set and costs no states.

Trap: the empty string in closure constructions

Concatenation and star both introduce ε-moves, and ε-moves are what make the subset construction necessary. Forgetting to close over ε-moves produces an automaton that misses exactly the words that use the seam.

The page's star construction for the "divisible by 3" automaton returns 3 states and the same language, because that language is already closed under concatenation with itself. A construction being correct does not mean it changes anything.

Beyond the syllabus: deterministic context-free languages

Community observation The deterministic context-free languages are closed under complement but not under union — the mirror image of the full class. This is class D for GATE CSE 2027: it is not named in Section 6, and it is mentioned here only so that a half-remembered fact does not get applied to the wrong class.

If a question says "context-free", answer for the full class: not closed under complement, closed under union.

Boundary: ambiguity is a property of the grammar

A language is unambiguous when some grammar for it has one tree per word; a grammar is ambiguous when it has two trees for some word. Every regular language has an unambiguous grammar, because its DFA gives one derivation per word.

Under S → S S | a, the word a8 has 429 parse trees — Catalan(7), which the page computes. That shows this grammar is ambiguous. Deciding whether a given grammar is ambiguous has no algorithm at all.

Boundary: what the lemmas cannot see

The regular lemma cannot distinguish anbn from any other non-regular language by inspection; it only ever produces a no. Neither lemma can prove that a language is in a class, and neither can prove that a language is not in a larger class: failing the regular lemma says nothing about context-freeness, because anbn fails it and is context-free.

The correct reading of "fails the regular lemma" is exactly "not regular". Nothing more.

1. "It passes the pumping lemma, so it is regular"

Why it tempts. The lemma is taught as a test, and a test that a language passes looks like a certificate.

Minimal counterexample. {aibjck : i = 1 implies j = k} passes from p = 2 and is not regular, as the intersection with a b*c* shows.

Correct rule. The lemma is an implication in one direction: regular implies pumpable. Failing proves not regular; passing proves nothing.

2. "Context-free languages are not closed under intersection, so L1 ∩ L2 is not context-free"

Why it tempts. The non-closure fact is true and is the most examined fact on the clause.

Minimal counterexample. L1 = anbn and L2 = a*b*: their intersection is anbn, which is context-free. Non-closure is a statement about the class, not about a pair.

Correct rule. A closure theorem can prove a yes and can never prove a no for a specific instance. For a specific instance, build the grammar or find the witness.

3. "A pumping length that fails means no pumping length exists"

Why it tempts. A failed split feels like a counterexample.

Minimal counterexample. p = 1 fails on the worked language and p = 2 works, so the language is pumpable with p = 2. Any larger value also works.

Correct rule. The lemma asserts existence. To prove non-regularity you need a failure at every p, symbolically in p.

4. "L1 − L2 is context-free because L1 − R is"

Why it tempts. Both are written with a minus sign, and the regular case is a named fact.

Minimal counterexample. Take L1 = Σ* (regular, so context-free) and L2 a context-free language whose complement is not. Then L1 − L2 = complement(L2), outside the class.

Correct rule. The second operand decides. Minus a regular language: always context-free. Minus a context-free language: no guarantee.

5. "Membership is decidable, so equivalence is too"

Why it tempts. Both are questions about the same object, and one of them is decidable.

Minimal counterexample. There is no algorithm at all for equivalence of context-free languages, or for universality, or for ambiguity — although each of those is expressible in terms of questions that are decidable.

Correct rule. Membership quantifies over one word; equivalence and universality quantify over all words. That is the whole difference, and it is a difference in kind.

6. "Closure under complement is the same for both classes"

Why it tempts. Complement feels like the simplest operation, so it looks safe for everyone.

Minimal counterexample. Regular languages are closed under complement; context-free languages are not. If they were, closure under union would give closure under intersection, which fails.

Correct rule. For the two classes the complement column differs, and it is one of the three places the decision table differs as well.

Two checks, then the arithmetic

Every question on this clause reduces to two checks made before any construction. Check 1: does the claim say "always" (every pair in the class) or "this language" (one instance)? Check 2: is the other operand a regular language, or a member of the same class? Doing these first removes most wrong options without any computation.

The five questions below were checked against answer archives during this run. Question numbering can differ by one between archives; the year, the set and the keyed answer are what this page relies on.

Pattern 1 — which of these is "not necessarily" in the class

Four expressions built from a regular and a context-free language; exactly one is not guaranteed. The reasoning is a table lookup: intersection, union and concatenation with a regular language are all safe; difference is the one that is not.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2021 Set 1: L1 regular, L2 context-free; the keyed answer is L1 − L2.

Common wrong approach. Choosing the intersection because "context-free languages are not closed under intersection" — true of two context-free languages, false when one side is regular.

Pattern 2 — which statements are "always true"

A list of statements about regular and non-regular languages, with "always" doing the work. One counterexample kills each false option, and the standard counterexample is the universal language Σ*, which is regular and swallows any language in a union.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q23: the keyed answer is the complement of a non-regular language is non-regular, and the union of two complemented regular languages is regular.

Common wrong approach. Accepting "a regular union a non-regular is non-regular" because it sounds monotone. Σ* ∪ L3 = Σ*.

Pattern 3 — is this specific language context-free

A single language given by a pattern of exponents. Here closure can only ever answer yes: intersect with a regular language, concatenate, substitute — each gives a context-free result. To answer no you need the CFL pumping lemma, or a reduction to something already known not to be context-free.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q38: L1 ∪ L2 is context-free, L1 ∩ L2 is not; the keyed answer is "I only".

Common wrong approach. Answering "neither" because intersection is not closed. The union is fine, and the union is half the question.

Pattern 4 — which of four statements about two context-free languages holds

Union, complement, difference with a regular language, intersection. Two are true and two are false, and the two false ones are exactly the two operations the class does not keep.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 Q4: the keyed answer is "I and III only" — union and difference with a regular language.

Common wrong approach. Including the complement because the complement of a context-free language is recursive, which is true and irrelevant.

Pattern 5 — a pumping length or a pumping constant

Either "which of these can be a pumping length" or "which of these is a pumping length for the given language". Both are answered by finding the minimum and taking any value at least as large. The distractors are always just below the shortest word in the language.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2019 Q15: the keyed answer is 24, and the minimum for that language is 12.

Common wrong approach. Computing the minimum, finding it is not an option, and concluding the question is broken. Any value at least the minimum is a valid answer.

Solving procedure for the whole clause

  1. Identify the class each language is in, and whether the claim is about all pairs or one.
  2. If it is a closure claim about all pairs, name the construction for a yes, or one pair for a no.
  3. If it is about one language, try to build a grammar (yes) before trying to fail the lemma (no).
  4. If a pumping length is asked for, find the shortest word in each branch and take any value at least the largest of them.
  5. If a decision question is asked, check the three: universality, equivalence, ambiguity — those are the ones with no algorithm.

MSQGATE CSE 2024 Set 1, Q23

Let L1, L2 be two regular languages and L3 a language which is not regular. Which of the following statements is/are always TRUE?

  1. L1 = L2 if and only if L1 ∩ complement(L2) = ∅
  2. L1 ∪ L3 is not regular
  3. complement(L3) is not regular
  4. complement(L1) ∪ complement(L2) is regular

Answer: C and D (keyed)

C. Regular languages are closed under complement. If complement(L3) were regular, its complement L3 would be regular too, which it is not.

D. Both complements are regular, and the union of two regular languages is regular.

Why A is wrong. L1 ∩ complement(L2) = ∅ says only L1 ⊆ L2. Equality needs both directions.

Why B is wrong. Take L1 = Σ* and L3 = anbn: the union is Σ*, which is regular.

This is check 1 in action: "always" means one counterexample ends the option, and Σ* is the counterexample that ends B.

MCQGATE CSE 2021 Set 1

Suppose that L1 is a regular language and L2 is a context-free language. Which one of the following languages is NOT necessarily context-free?

  1. L1 ∩ L2
  2. L1 · L2
  3. L1 − L2
  4. L1 ∪ L2

Answer: C — L1 − L2

L1 − L2 = L1 ∩ complement(L2). Take L1 = Σ*, which is regular, and L2 a context-free language whose complement is not. Then the difference is complement(L2), outside the class.

Why A, B and D are safe. Intersection with a regular language is context-free by the [A,p,q] construction. Concatenation is closed for two context-free languages, and a regular language is context-free. Union likewise.

This is check 2 in action: the second operand is context-free here, and only the difference cares.

MCQGATE CSE 2017 Set 2, Q4

Let L1, L2 be any two context-free languages and R be any regular language. Then which of the following is/are CORRECT?

I. L1 ∪ L2 is context-free   II. complement(L1) is context-free   III. L1 − R is context-free   IV. L1 ∩ L2 is context-free

  1. I, II and IV only
  2. I and III only
  3. II and IV only
  4. I only

Answer: B — I and III only (keyed)

I holds: closed under union. III holds: L1 − R = L1 ∩ complement(R), and the complement of a regular language is regular, so this is a context-free intersection with a regular language.

II fails: the class is not closed under complement. IV fails: not closed under intersection with itself, with anbncm and ambncn as the witness pair.

Note that II and IV fail for the same reason: closure under complement plus closure under union would give closure under intersection.

MCQGATE CSE 2019, Q15

For Σ = {a, b}, let us consider the regular language L = {x | x = a2+3k or x = b10+12k, k ≥ 0}. Which one of the following can be a pumping length (the constant guaranteed by the pumping lemma) for L?

  1. 3
  2. 5
  3. 9
  4. 24

Answer: D — 24 (keyed)

The shortest b-string in L is b10. Any p ≤ 10 would force b10 to be pumpable with |y| ≤ p < 10, but pumping gives length 10 + |y|, which must again be of the form 10 + 12k — so |y| would have to be a positive multiple of 12. No such multiple is below 10.

The minimum pumping length for this language is 12, and any value at least 12 works, so 24 is correct and 3, 5, 9 are not.

The trap: computing the minimum, not finding it among the options, and answering "none". The question asks for a pumping length, not the minimum.

MSQOriginal practice question

Let L = {anbn : n ≥ 0}. Which of the following languages are context-free?

  1. L · {cm : m ≥ 0}
  2. L ∩ {w : |w| is even}
  3. L ∩ {aibici : i ≥ 0}
  4. complement(L) restricted to a*b*

Answer: A, B and D

A. Concatenation of two context-free languages, which is closed.

B. Intersection with a regular language, which is closed — the [A,p,q] construction gives a grammar directly.

C. The intersection equals {aibici}, which fails the CFL pumping lemma. Non-closure under intersection, in the one direction it can be used: a specific pair, not the class.

D. Inside a*b*, the complement of anbn is {aibj : i ≠ j}, which is context-free: guess whether there are too many a's or too many b's and check with the stack. This does not contradict non-closure under complement — it is one instance, not the class.

D is the option that separates a student who knows the closure table from one who knows what it means.

NATOriginal practice question

Let A be a DFA over {a, b} with 4 states and B a DFA over {a, b} with 6 states, both total. The product construction for L(A) ∩ L(B) builds states as pairs. What is the largest number of states the product automaton can have?

Answer: 24

States are pairs, so the product has at most 4 × 6 = 24. The construction explores by reachability, so the actual count can be smaller, and the minimal DFA for the result can be smaller still.

Why 10 appears. That is the sum, which is the state count of a union built by running the two machines side by side in a different construction. The product is a product, not a sum.

On this page the same arithmetic gives 9 possible pairs for two 3-state machines, of which 7 are reachable and 5 suffice after minimisation.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following questions has an algorithm that always halts with the correct answer, for every input grammar G over a fixed alphabet?

  1. Does G generate the empty string?
  2. Is L(G) empty?
  3. Is G ambiguous?
  4. Does L(G) = Σ*?

Answer: A and B

A. Membership is decidable for context-free grammars: parse the word, in cubic time.

B. Emptiness is decidable: compute the productive nonterminals as a fixed point and test the start symbol. The page runs this on S → AB | a, A → aA, B → bB and gets the productive set {S}.

C. Ambiguity of a grammar is undecidable. Counting trees for one grammar is easy — a8 has 429 under S → S S | a — but deciding it in general is not possible.

D. Universality is undecidable for context-free grammars.

Note that A being decidable and D being undecidable is not a paradox: A quantifies over one word, D over all words.

The closure table, one screen

Regular: closed under union, intersection, complement, difference, concatenation, star, reversal, homomorphism, and intersection with anything.

Context-free: closed under union, concatenation, star, reversal, homomorphism, intersection with a regular language, and difference with a regular language. Not closed under intersection, complement, or difference in general.

The one-line reason complement fails: union plus complement would give intersection, and intersection already fails.

Definitions worth writing out

Regular pumping lemma. ∃p. ∀w ∈ L, |w| ≥ p ⇒ ∃ x,y,z: w = xyz, |y| ≥ 1, |xy| ≤ p, ∀i ≥ 0: xyiz ∈ L.

CFL pumping lemma. ∃n. ∀w ∈ L, |w| ≥ n ⇒ ∃ u,v,w,x,y: w = uvwxy, |vwx| ≤ n, |vx| ≥ 1, ∀i ≥ 0: uviwxiy ∈ L.

Product construction. States are pairs; |Q| = |Q1| × |Q2| at most. Union: either accepts. Intersection: both accept. Difference: first accepts, second does not.

The decision table

Both classes, decidable: emptiness, finiteness, membership. Procedures: reachability; a live state on a cycle; run or parse.

Regular only: universality (complement empty), equivalence (symmetric difference empty).

Neither: universality and equivalence for context-free languages, and ambiguity of a grammar.

Traps, in the order they cost marks

  • Passing the pumping lemma is not a proof of regularity.
  • A failed pumping length is too small, not a counterexample.
  • Non-closure under intersection cannot be used to answer "no" for one specific pair.
  • L1 − R is context-free; L1 − L2 is not guaranteed.
  • Complement needs a deterministic, total automaton.
  • The CFL lemma needs |vx| ≥ 1, not |v| ≥ 1.
  • Ambiguity belongs to a grammar; a language can have an unambiguous grammar and an ambiguous one.

Witnesses to memorise

Not regular: anbn, aq with q prime, ww, balanced parentheses.

Context-free but not regular: anbn, wwR, palindromes.

Not context-free: anbncn, ww, anbncndn.

The pair that proves non-closure under intersection: anbncm and ambncn.

Thirty-second self-test

1. Is {aibj : i ≠ j} regular? 2. Is L ∩ Σ* context-free when L is? 3. Can a regular language's complement be non-regular? 4. Is ambiguity decidable for regular grammars? 5. Does p = 100 prove anything about {anbn}?

Answers: no; yes, it is L itself; no; the question does not arise, since a regular language has an unambiguous grammar; no — you need a failure at every p, given symbolically.
