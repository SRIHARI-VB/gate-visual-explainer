Topic and objective

TOPIC: Resolution refutation — clausal form, the resolution rule, the empty clause — and the Horn fragment with unit propagation.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Convert a formula to a set of clauses and say what a set of clauses means. State the resolution rule exactly: one pivot, everything else kept, tautologies dropped. Prove that the rule is sound, by the two cases on the pivot, and say why adding a resolvent never changes satisfiability. Read a derivation as a tree that ends at the empty clause, and say what the empty clause means. Separate refutation-completeness from the false claim that resolution derives every consequence. Recognise a Horn clause, read it as a rule, run unit propagation on it, and know that the same procedure stalls on general clauses. Refute a previous-year item option by option, and name the one unsound shortcut that produces a plausible contradiction.

I read this topic as one method and one fragment rather than as two topics: the fragment exists only because the method is expensive, so the two halves are taught together.

Prerequisites

  • Propositional logic — the five connectives, truth tables, logical equivalence, tautology and contradiction, and validity as a tautology test. Every clause in this lesson is read with that vocabulary.
  • De Morgan's laws and distribution — the same lesson gives them; the conversion to clausal form is those two laws applied until nothing is left to apply.
  • Boolean algebra and minimisation — the same two shapes appear there as sum-of-products and product-of-sums. A clause is one product-of-sums term; clausal form is the whole product.
  • P, NP and NP-complete — needed only for the claim that deciding satisfiability of general clauses is NP-complete, which is why the Horn fragment earns its own lesson.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A set of clauses is an AND. Each clause is an OR; the set is true only when every clause is.
  • One pivot per step. Cancel exactly one complementary pair and keep everything else. Two pivots at once is unsound.
  • The step is sound and preserves satisfiability. So a satisfiable set can never reach the empty clause.
  • Complete for refutation, not for derivation. From (P) nothing follows by resolution; entailment is tested by adding the negated goal.
  • Horn buys linear time. At most one positive literal per clause, and unit propagation decides it without search.

1. Intuition — two witnesses who cannot both be right

You want to show that a statement must be true. Instead of building it, you invite its opposite into the room along with everything you already believe, and you show that the two cannot sit together. That is refutation, and it is the whole idea. Educational interpretation

The engine of the argument is a single observation. If one witness says "P or Q" and another says "not P", then between them Q must hold: the first witness has two ways to be right, the second rules one of them out. Resolution is that observation, written as a rule, applied until nothing is left. When nothing is left — when the last pair cancels and produces a clause with no literals at all — the company of statements has been shown to be impossible, and the original claim follows.

2. Formal definitions

A literal is a variable or its negation. A clause is a disjunction of literals; a clause with one literal is a unit clause, and a clause with none is the empty clause, written □. A formula is in clausal form (conjunctive normal form) when it is a conjunction of clauses, and from then on it is written as a set of clauses, because a conjunction is true exactly when every member is.

The resolution rule. From a clause containing the literal p and a clause containing its negation ¬p, infer the resolvent: the union of the two clauses with p and ¬p removed, repeated literals collapsed. The variable p is the pivot. The resolvent is a logical consequence of its two parents.

A refutation of a set S is a finite sequence of clauses in which each clause is either a member of S or a resolvent of two earlier clauses, and the last clause is □. Verified fact This is Robinson's definition and his theorem: a finite set of clauses is unsatisfiable if and only if it has a refutation (J. A. Robinson, A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle, JACM, 1965).

3. Visual understanding — what the drawing has to show

A refutation is not a wall of text: it is a tree. The original clauses are the leaves, each step is a node with exactly two parents, and the empty clause is the single root. The animation draws that tree as it grows, and labels each edge with the pivot it cancelled, because the pivot is the only choice a solver makes.

The other three scenes are drawn from the same principle: the truth table shows the rows where the parents are true, so soundness is visible as "no row escapes"; the Horn scene shows marks arriving one at a time, so the order is visible; the trap scene puts the illegal step and the legal one side by side, so the difference is a picture rather than a sentence.

4. Worked example — the four clauses

The standard first refutation: (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q), (¬P ∨ ¬Q). The first pair differs only on Q, so resolve on Q and get (P). The second pair differs only on Q as well, so resolve on Q and get (¬P). Then (P) and (¬P) differ only on P, so resolve on P and get □. Three steps, all four leaves used once.

The page computes this tree itself and checks its shape rather than printing a remembered answer: four leaves, three steps, one root, and the root empty. It also checks that the two middle clauses are exactly the units (P) and (¬P).

5. Boundaries — what the theorem does and does not promise

Completeness is a promise about the empty clause. It says: if the set is unsatisfiable, some refutation exists. It does not say that every clause entailed by the set can be derived, and it is false that it can: from the single clause (P) the closure is just (P), with nothing derived, although (P ∨ Q) is entailed.

It is also not a promise about size. Haken proved in 1985 that the pigeonhole clauses need exponentially many resolution steps, and the page measures the beginning of that growth: 6 clauses, then 73, then past 900. Completeness guarantees existence, never a practical search.

6. Edge cases and the counterexample to memorise

The counterexample that matters most is the one that kills the shortcut. Take (P ∨ Q) and (¬P ∨ ¬Q). They are complementary on P and also on Q, so a rule that cancelled both pairs at once would produce □ — and would be wrong, because P true with Q false satisfies both parents. Done legally, one pivot at a time, the two resolvents are (Q ∨ ¬Q) and (P ∨ ¬P), both tautologies, both dropped, and the closure is the two original clauses.

Two boundary conventions go with it. A resolvent that contains a variable and its negation is a tautology and is deleted, because it is true everywhere and carries nothing. And the empty set of clauses is trivially true, which is the opposite of the empty clause.

7. GATE application — what the question looks like

The examined questions are small and structural: which clause is a resolvent of these two, which statement about the rule is false, is this set satisfiable, is this clause Horn, how many steps does the refutation take. The arithmetic is trivial; the marks sit on the definition of the step and on the difference between entailment and equivalence.

The 2003 item quoted in this lesson is exactly that shape: it states the rule, then offers four statements, three of which are true. The three true ones are soundness, satisfiability preservation, and the meaning of an unsatisfiable disjunction — the three properties this lesson proves.

Example 1 — converting one formula, step by step

Take P → (Q ∧ R). Remove the implication using the equivalence P → X ≡ ¬P ∨ X, giving ¬P ∨ (Q ∧ R). Distribute the disjunction over the conjunction, giving (¬P ∨ Q) ∧ (¬P ∨ R). Read the conjunction as a list, and each disjunct as one clause: {¬P ∨ Q, ¬P ∨ R}. Two clauses, and the original formula is true exactly when both are.

Why distribution is the step that matters. Before it, the expression still has a conjunction inside a disjunction, so it is not a set of clauses and resolution has nothing to resolve. After it, there is one AND at the top and only ORs below, which is exactly the shape the rule consumes.

Example 2 — negating a goal the right way

To prove P → Q by refutation, negate it. The negation of an implication is not another implication: ¬(P → Q) ≡ P ∧ ¬Q, which is the one assignment that makes the implication false. That conjunction is two unit clauses, {P} and {¬Q}.

Consequence. Add both clauses, not one. A learner who adds only (¬Q) has refuted a different set and will usually conclude "not entailed" when the goal does follow. The page checks the negation by truth table whenever it is used, and the four-clause refutation in the animation is exactly this shape with P and Q swapped for whole formulas.

Example 3 — the four-clause refutation, read as a tree

Clauses (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q), (¬P ∨ ¬Q). Resolve the first pair on Q to get (P); resolve the second pair on Q to get (¬P); resolve those on P to get □. The derivation has three steps, and each original clause is used exactly once.

Read backwards. The tree has the four clauses as leaves, two intermediate nodes carrying the units, and one root carrying □. That tree is the refutation; the linear sequence of clauses is just one order in which it can be written down. The page computes the tree from its own engine and checks the shape: four leaves, three steps, one empty root.

Example 4 — a set that does derive something

From (P ∨ Q) and (P ∨ ¬Q) resolve on Q: the two clauses agree on P and disagree on Q, so cancelling Q leaves (P) alone. The closure is three clauses: the two originals and the unit.

The contrast with the next example. Resolution does derive consequences when the clauses disagree on something. What it cannot do is derive a consequence that is weaker than what it already has: from the unit (P) it produces nothing, because a unit has no pivot to offer. Deriving (P ∨ Q) from (P) would need a weakening rule, and resolution has none.

Example 5 — a Horn program, and its smallest model

Facts (A) and (C). Rules (¬A ∨ ¬C ∨ B), read as "A and C give B", and (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ D), read as "A and B give D". Goal (¬B ∨ ¬E). Propagation marks A and C from the facts, fires the first rule to mark B, then the second to mark D. E is never marked, because no clause concludes E.

Verdict and model. The goal needs B and E; E never arrives, so no goal clause is violated, the set is satisfiable, and {A, B, C, D} is its smallest model. Every mark is forced, which is why the marked set is a model at all and why it is the smallest one: an atom marked this way is true in every model of the set.

Example 6 — the same four clauses, decided twice

On the four general clauses, unit propagation cannot start: every clause has two literals, so there is no unit clause, and the procedure reports no conflict after zero steps. Resolution refutes the same set in five derived clauses, and the truth table finds no satisfying row among the four assignments.

What that shows. Propagation is a sound refuter — a conflict it reports is real — and an incomplete decision procedure outside the Horn fragment. "No conflict found" is not "satisfiable"; it is "no refutation was found by this method". That distinction is the whole reason the Horn fragment gets its own linear-time algorithm.

The empty clause against the empty set

A set with no clauses has nothing to satisfy, so it is trivially true and satisfiable. A clause with no literals has nothing to make it true, so it is false under every assignment and is the verdict of a refutation. These two are opposites and are confused constantly, because both are written as nothing at all. The page prints □ for the clause and never prints an empty set as a result.

A unit clause has no pivot

Resolution needs two parents and a complementary pair. A single unit clause offers neither, so the closure of {(P)} is exactly {(P)} with zero derived clauses. This is not a failure of the method: it is the method reporting that nothing has been refuted. Two units that disagree, (P) and (¬P), do have a pivot and do produce □, which is the smallest possible refutation.

Tautologies are deleted, and that is safe

If the union of the parents brings in a variable and its negation, the resolvent is true under every assignment. Deleting it cannot lose a refutation, because a clause that is true everywhere can never be part of the step that produces a clause that is true nowhere. Of the 1,998 resolvents the page computes over all pairs of clauses on four variables, 1,022 are tautologies and are dropped.

Duplicate and subsumed clauses

A resolvent that repeats a clause already present adds nothing, and neither does one that is subsumed: if C ⊆ D then D follows from C, so D is redundant. The page keeps a set, not a list, so a repeated resolvent is never added twice. A subsumption test is the third filter a real implementation applies, after the tautology test and the duplicate test.

Several complementary pairs in one pair of clauses

Two clauses can disagree on more than one variable. Then there is one legal resolvent per pivot, not one per pair. With (P ∨ Q) and (¬P ∨ ¬Q) there are two legal steps, on P and on Q, and both give tautologies. The illegal move is to cancel both pairs at once, which is the counterexample this lesson returns to three times.

Horn with no positive literal at all

A clause such as (¬B ∨ ¬E) has zero positive literals, which satisfies "at most one" and is therefore Horn. It is a goal clause: it forbids a combination rather than asserting anything. A set of goal clauses alone is satisfiable, because nothing forces their premises. That is why a refutation of a Horn set always needs at least one fact to start the propagation.

The clause (P) and the clause (¬P)

Both are Horn: each has at most one positive literal. Together they are unsatisfiable, and the refutation is one step. A learner who checks only "does it have a positive literal" will call (¬P) non-Horn; the definition counts positive literals and allows zero. The page's own filter is written that way, and the check on 1,159 Horn sets depends on it.

Refutation-complete, not decision-complete

For a finite propositional set the closure always terminates, because there are finitely many clauses over finitely many variables, so the method does decide satisfiability. What it does not do is enumerate the entailed clauses: it decides one yes-or-no question about the set, and every other use — entailment, consistency, a model — is that question in disguise. In first-order logic the termination guarantee is lost, and that is a different lesson.

1. "Resolution proves the goal"

Why it feels right: every worked example in a textbook ends with the thing you wanted, so the goal seems to be what comes out.

Counterexample: what comes out is □, which contains no variable and says nothing about the goal. The goal appears only in the input, negated, and it is the knowledge base plus that negation that is refuted. Repair: say "refute KB with ¬φ" and never "derive φ".

Cost: a learner who forgets to negate runs the method on a satisfiable set, finds nothing, and reports "not entailed" for a goal that does follow.

2. "Complete means every consequence can be derived"

Why it feels right: the word complete suggests nothing is missing, and consequences feel like the obvious thing to be missing.

Counterexample: from the single clause (P), the closure is {(P)} with zero derived clauses, although (P ∨ Q) is entailed. Repair: the theorem is about the empty clause. Resolution is refutation-complete, and that is a strictly weaker promise.

Where it is tested: exactly here. The GATE 2003 item offers "the resolvent is logically valid" and "satisfiability is preserved" as separate statements, and a learner holding this belief reads them as the same statement.

3. "Cancel every complementary pair at once"

Why it feels right: two clauses that contradict each other in two places feel more contradictory, not less.

Counterexample: (P ∨ Q) and (¬P ∨ ¬Q) give □ by the double cancel, and P true with Q false satisfies both. Repair: one pivot per step, always. The rule removes one literal from each parent, not a set of them.

Cost: this is the only error here that produces a false verdict, so it is the one that turns a satisfiable set into "unsatisfiable" and loses the mark with no warning.

4. "The resolvent is equivalent to its parents"

Why it feels right: the rule is taught next to the equivalence laws, and it looks like one more rewriting step.

Counterexample: (P) follows from (P ∨ Q) and (P ∨ ¬Q), and (P) does not imply (P ∨ Q). Repair: the resolvent is a consequence — one direction only. That asymmetry is why the method can only refute.

Useful consequence: because the resolvent is weaker, adding it never removes a model, so satisfiability is preserved. That is the fact the next misconception attacks.

5. "No conflict from propagation means satisfiable"

Why it feels right: on Horn sets it does mean that, and most examples in a first course are Horn.

Counterexample: the four clauses (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q), (¬P ∨ ¬Q) have no unit clause, so propagation does nothing and reports no conflict, and the set has no model. Repair: propagation is a sound refuter and an incomplete decision procedure.

Correct reading: "no conflict" means "this method found none", which is a statement about the method, not about the set.

6. "A Horn clause has few literals"

Why it feels right: the examples are short, and "Horn" sounds like a small subclass.

Counterexample: (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ ¬C ∨ ¬D ∨ ¬E) has five literals and is Horn, with zero positive ones. (P ∨ Q) has two literals and is not Horn. Repair: count positive literals, not literals, and allow zero.

Why the definition is drawn that way: one positive literal is one conclusion. A clause with two conclusions is a choice, and a choice is what makes general satisfiability hard.

7. "A complete method is a practical one"

Why it feels right: completeness is stated as a guarantee, and guarantees sound like something you can rely on at the keyboard.

Counterexample: the pigeonhole clauses. The page's closure is 6 clauses for two pigeons, 73 for three, and past its cap of 900 for four, and Haken proved the growth is exponential for every resolution refutation. Repair: separate existence from size, and quote both.

Consequence: this is why SAT solvers add heuristics and learning, and why the Horn fragment — decided in linear time — is worth a separate lesson.

8. "A set of clauses is a disjunction"

Why it feels right: the notation {C1, C2, C3} looks like a list of alternatives, and set-builder braces are used for unions elsewhere.

Counterexample: the four-clause set is unsatisfiable although every one of its clauses is true in three of the four assignments. Read as a disjunction it would be a tautology. Repair: a set of clauses is a conjunction: one false clause falsifies the set.

The distinction to keep: within a clause the literals are alternatives; between clauses they are all required. The whole difficulty of the topic lives in that switch.

GATE CSE 2003 — the resolution rule, and which statement is false

"The following resolution rule is used in logic programming. Derive clause (P ∨ Q) from clauses (P ∨ R), (Q ∨ ¬R). Which of the following statements related to this rule is FALSE? (A) ((P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R)) ⇒ (P ∨ Q) is logically valid. (B) (P ∨ Q) ⇒ ((P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R)) is logically valid. (C) (P ∨ Q) is satisfiable if and only if (P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R) is satisfiable. (D) (P ∨ Q) ⇒ FALSE if and only if both P and Q are unsatisfiable."

(A) holds. This is soundness, stated for one pair: the resolvent is entailed by the conjunction of its parents, so the implication is true in every row. The page checks all eight rows.

(B) fails. The implication runs the wrong way. Take P true, Q false, R true: then P ∨ Q holds, P ∨ R holds, and Q ∨ ¬R is false — so the conjunction is false while the antecedent is true. One row is enough, and the page finds exactly this assignment as its witness.

(C) holds. Both formulas are satisfiable, so the biconditional of their satisfiability is true. The forward direction is the interesting one: if P ∨ Q holds, choose R to satisfy the other clause — R false if P holds, R true if Q holds. The page checks satisfiability of both sides directly.

(D) holds. P ∨ Q is unsatisfiable exactly when P is false in every assignment and Q is false in every assignment, which is what "both P and Q are unsatisfiable" says. That is the definition of an unsatisfiable disjunction, read backwards.

Answer: B. Note what the three true options are: soundness, satisfiability preservation, and the meaning of an unsatisfiable disjunction. Those are the three properties this lesson proves, and the one false option is the converse of the first — which is exactly the "resolvent is equivalent to its parents" misconception. Verified fact Statement and options read from two independent reproductions of the paper (GATE Overflow and ExamSIDE), which agree on the wording and on answer (B) and disagree on the question number. The verdicts here were recomputed, not copied.

The four question patterns

  • Produce the resolvent. Find the complementary pair, cancel it, keep the rest, collapse repeats, and check whether the answer is a tautology. One pivot only.
  • Decide satisfiability. Either run the refutation, or find one satisfying assignment. A single row is a complete answer for "satisfiable"; a refutation is a complete answer for "not".
  • Classify a clause. Horn means at most one positive literal. Then read it as a fact, a rule or a goal, and say what unit propagation would mark.
  • Judge a statement about the rule. The statements are always one of: soundness (true), equivalence (false), satisfiability preservation (true), or the meaning of an unsatisfiable clause (true).

The procedure, and where each step is tested

  1. Negate the goal and convert it. Add every clause it produces — not one.
  2. Look for a complementary pair: one positive, one negative, same variable.
  3. Cancel that one pair and keep everything else. If the result is a tautology, drop it.
  4. Repeat. The empty clause means the goal is entailed; a closed set with no empty clause means it is not.
  5. If the set is Horn, replace all of that with unit propagation and read the marks: a violated goal means unsatisfiable, and the marks themselves are the smallest model.

MCQGATE CSE 2003

The following resolution rule is used in logic programming: derive clause (P ∨ Q) from clauses (P ∨ R) and (Q ∨ ¬R). Which of the following statements related to this rule is FALSE?

  1. ((P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R)) ⇒ (P ∨ Q) is logically valid.
  2. (P ∨ Q) ⇒ ((P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R)) is logically valid.
  3. (P ∨ Q) is satisfiable if and only if (P ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ ¬R) is satisfiable.
  4. (P ∨ Q) ⇒ FALSE if and only if both P and Q are unsatisfiable.

Answer: B

Option B asserts the converse of soundness, and the converse is false. Witness: P true, Q false, R true. Then P ∨ Q holds, P ∨ R holds, and Q ∨ ¬R is false, so the conjunction is false while the antecedent is true. The page finds this row by truth table.

Why A is true. Soundness: the resolvent is entailed by the conjunction of the parents. Why C is true. Both sides are satisfiable; and if P ∨ Q holds you can choose R to satisfy the other clause. Why D is true. P ∨ Q is unsatisfiable exactly when P and Q are each false everywhere.

Shortcut and its assumption. "Resolution gives a consequence, never an equivalent" settles A and B in one line. The assumption is that the step cancelled exactly one pivot, which it did — R.

MCQOriginal practice question

Which one of the following is the resolvent of (P ∨ Q ∨ ¬R) and (R ∨ S) on the pivot R?

  1. P ∨ Q ∨ S
  2. P ∨ Q ∨ ¬R ∨ R ∨ S
  3. P ∨ Q ∨ S ∨ ¬R
  4. No resolvent exists, because the clauses share no variable in opposite signs.

Answer: A

R appears negatively in the first clause and positively in the second, so R is the pivot. Remove both and keep the rest: P, Q, S. Repeated literals collapse, so the answer is P ∨ Q ∨ S.

Why B fails. It keeps the cancelled pair, which is the commonest slip: the pair disappears, it does not become a tautology inside the answer. Why C fails. It keeps ¬R as well, which is the same error with one literal. Why D fails. The pivot is there; it is R.

Check the answer is weaker. P ∨ Q ∨ S is entailed by the two parents and does not entail them, which is exactly what a resolvent should be.

NATOriginal practice question

Take the three clauses (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q) and (¬P ∨ Q). Saturate them: keep every new non-tautological resolvent, and drop duplicates and tautologies. How many clauses are in the closure, counting the three originals?

Answer: 5

Resolving the first two on Q gives (P). Resolving the first and third on P gives (Q). The other two pairs — first with second on P, first with third on Q — produce (¬Q ∨ Q) and (P ∨ ¬P), both tautologies, both dropped. Nothing further can be derived, so the closure is {P ∨ Q, P ∨ ¬Q, ¬P ∨ Q, P, Q}: five clauses.

Why 4 appears. It forgets that (P) and (Q) are two separate derivations, not one. Why 7 appears. It keeps the two tautologies. Why 6 appears. It counts a duplicate: (P) can be reached only once, and a set has no repeats.

What the answer means. The set is satisfiable, with the single model P true and Q true, so no refutation exists — and the page's own engine reports exactly that.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements are true for every finite set S of propositional clauses?

  1. Every resolvent of two clauses in S is entailed by S.
  2. If S is satisfiable, no sequence of resolution steps from S reaches the empty clause.
  3. If S is unsatisfiable, some sequence of resolution steps from S reaches the empty clause.
  4. Every clause entailed by S can be derived from S by resolution.

Answer: A, B and C

A is soundness. B follows from A plus the fact that a resolvent preserves satisfiability: a satisfiable set never produces a clause with no model. C is Robinson's completeness theorem for refutation.

Why D fails. From the single clause (P), the entailed clause (P ∨ Q) is not derivable: a unit clause offers no pivot. Resolution derives resolvents, which is a smaller set than the set of consequences.

Distinction being tested. B and C are the two halves of the refutation theorem. D is the completeness theorem misread as being about consequences rather than about the empty clause — the single most common overstatement on this topic.

MCQOriginal practice question

Which one of the following clauses is NOT a Horn clause?

  1. ¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C
  2. ¬A ∨ ¬B
  3. A ∨ B
  4. ¬A

Answer: C

A Horn clause has at most one positive literal. Options A, B and D have one, zero and zero positive literals respectively, so all three qualify. Option C has two positive literals, A and B, so it is not Horn.

Why B is not the answer. Zero positive literals is allowed; a clause with none is a goal clause. Counting all literals instead of positive ones is the error this question is built to catch. Why D is not the answer. One negative literal, zero positive: a goal clause of one literal.

Why the definition matters. A ∨ B offers two ways to be true and no conclusion, and that choice is exactly what makes general satisfiability hard. Restricting to one conclusion at most is what makes linear-time propagation possible.

NATOriginal practice question

The clauses (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q) and (¬P ∨ ¬Q) form an unsatisfiable set. What is the smallest number of resolution steps in any refutation of it?

Answer: 3

Resolve the first pair on Q to get (P), resolve the second pair on Q to get (¬P), then resolve the two units on P to get □. Three steps, and each of the four original clauses is used exactly once.

Why 2 is impossible. One step produces a single clause from two parents, so after one step there are five clauses; the empty clause would have to be the resolvent of two original clauses, and no two of the four resolve directly to nothing — each pair leaves one literal behind. Why 4 appears. Some learners resolve the first clause with the third and get a tautology, then need an extra step to recover.

Reading the tree. Three steps means three internal nodes, four leaves and one root. The page checks that shape rather than trusting the printed sequence.

MCQOriginal practice question

A procedure runs unit propagation on a set of clauses, finds no unit clause at all, and stops. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The set is satisfiable.
  2. The set is unsatisfiable.
  3. Nothing is concluded about the set; only that this procedure found no conflict.
  4. The set is Horn.

Answer: C

Propagation is a sound refuter: a conflict it reports is real. It is not a decision procedure outside the Horn fragment, so the absence of a conflict says only that this method found none. The four clauses (P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q), (¬P ∨ ¬Q) are the counterexample: no unit clause, no conflict, and no model.

Why A fails. That is the counterexample above. Why B fails. Reporting no conflict cannot establish unsatisfiability; the method has not run. Why D fails. Horn-ness is a property of the clauses, not something a stalled run establishes — and those four clauses are not Horn.

Correct reading of a stall. "No refutation found by propagation" is a statement about the search, not about the formula.

MCQOriginal practice question

A learner resolves (P ∨ Q) with (¬P ∨ ¬Q) by cancelling both P and ¬P and both Q and ¬Q at once, obtaining the empty clause, and concludes the pair is unsatisfiable. What is wrong?

  1. Nothing; the pair really is unsatisfiable.
  2. The rule allows one pivot per step, and P true with Q false satisfies both parents.
  3. The resolvent should have been (P ∨ ¬P), which is a tautology.
  4. The empty clause may only be derived from unit clauses.

Answer: B

Cancelling two pairs at once is not the resolution rule. Under P true and Q false, (P ∨ Q) is true through P and (¬P ∨ ¬Q) is true through ¬Q, so the pair has a model and the derived □ would be false. One pivot per step, always.

Why C fails. Resolving on P alone gives (Q ∨ ¬Q), not (P ∨ ¬P); resolving on Q alone gives (P ∨ ¬P). Both are tautologies and both are dropped, which is why the legal closure adds nothing. Why D fails. Nothing restricts the rule to unit clauses; the four-clause refutation's last step is the only unit-on-unit step in it, and the first two are not.

The general lesson. A derivation that cancels more than one pair can turn a satisfiable set into a false contradiction. It is the only error in this lesson that changes the verdict.

The rule, in one line

From (A ∨ p) and (B ∨ ¬p) infer (A ∨ B): one pivot, both parents must disagree on it, everything else is kept, repeats collapse, and a resolvent containing a variable and its negation is a tautology and is dropped.

The four properties, and what each is for

  • Sound — the resolvent is entailed by its parents. Proved by the two cases on the pivot.
  • Satisfiability-preserving — adding a resolvent removes no model, so a satisfiable set never reaches □.
  • Refutation-complete — an unsatisfiable set always has a refutation (Robinson, 1965).
  • Not derivation-complete — from (P) nothing follows; (P ∨ Q) is entailed and underivable.

The Horn fragment

At most one positive literal per clause. One positive literal is a rule or a fact; zero is a goal. Unit propagation marks the facts, then fires every rule whose premises are all marked, and the marks are the smallest model. A violated goal means unsatisfiable. Dowling and Gallier, 1984: linear in the literal occurrences. The restriction costs expressiveness — (P ∨ Q) is not Horn — and buys a decision procedure with no search.

The procedure

  1. Negate the goal, convert it, add every clause.
  2. Find one complementary pair.
  3. Cancel that one pivot, keep the rest, drop tautologies and duplicates.
  4. Repeat until □ appears, or nothing new can be made.
  5. □ means entailed; a closed set without it means not entailed. Horn: propagate instead.

The five traps

  • Reading a set of clauses as a disjunction. It is a conjunction.
  • Adding the goal instead of its negation.
  • Cancelling two pivots in one step — the only error here that changes the verdict.
  • Keeping tautological resolvents, then reading a busy closure as a contradiction.
  • Reading a stalled propagation as a model.

The one counterexample to keep

(P ∨ Q) with (¬P ∨ ¬Q). The illegal double cancel gives □; P true with Q false satisfies both parents; the two legal steps give (Q ∨ ¬Q) and (P ∨ ¬P), both tautologies, both dropped, so nothing is derived and the pair is correctly reported satisfiable. Three facts in one line: the rule is one pivot, tautologies carry nothing, and a derivation that looks like a contradiction can be unsound.

The resolution principle

J. A. Robinson, A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle, Journal of the ACM, 1965. Source for: the resolution rule, the definition of a refutation as a sequence ending in the empty clause, and the Resolution Theorem — a finite set of clauses is unsatisfiable if and only if it has a refutation.

Completeness, constructively

J. Gallier, The Completeness of Propositional Resolution: A Simple and Constructive Proof, Logical Methods in Computer Science, 2006. Source for: the resolution DAG and the refutation as a DAG with a single root labelled □; the four-clause example {(P ∨ Q), (P ∨ ¬Q), (¬P ∨ Q), (¬P ∨ ¬Q)} used in this lesson's fifth scene; and the observation that the standard completeness proofs are existence proofs.

Horn clauses and linear time

W. F. Dowling and J. H. Gallier, Linear-time algorithms for testing the satisfiability of propositional Horn formulae, Journal of Logic Programming, 1984. Source for: Horn satisfiability decided in time proportional to the literal occurrences, and for the positive-unit propagation rule that the animation runs.

Unit propagation, taught

D. Mitchell, Linear Time: Unit Propagation and Horn-SAT, course notes, Simon Fraser University, CMPT 827. Source for: the definition of a Horn clause as at most one positive literal, the unit clause rule, the claim that propagation settles Horn sets in linear time, and the observation that the fixpoint of propagation is unique.

Search strategies, and where unit resolution fails

D. Bridge, Automating Resolution Refutation, course notes, University College Cork. Source for: the distinction between the search space, the derivation graph and the refutation tree; and for the statement that unit resolution and input resolution are complete for Horn clauses but not complete in general — the claim the eighth scene demonstrates with the four-clause set.

The exponential worst case

A. Haken, The intractability of resolution, Theoretical Computer Science 39, 1985. Source for: every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole clauses requires exponentially many steps.
