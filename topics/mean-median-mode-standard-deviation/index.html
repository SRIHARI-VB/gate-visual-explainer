Topic and objective

TOPIC: Mean, median, mode and standard deviation — the four numbers that summarise a list, on raw data, on grouped (frequency) data, and as parameters of a random variable. This is the statistics clause of Section 1 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Compute the three averages and the spread on a list and on a frequency table; say which of them a given question is really asking about; state the exact rule by which each one moves when the data are shifted or scaled; give the mean, median and mode of the five named distributions where they exist; and name the cases where a widely quoted shortcut or ordering rule is simply false.

I read this topic as three different answers to the question "where is the middle?" plus one answer to "how wide is it?". The averages differ because they measure different things: the mean measures mass, the median measures position, the mode measures frequency. Almost every exam item on this topic is decided by noticing which of the three the wording describes — so that distinction is the spine of the lesson, and every number below is computed on the page.

Prerequisites

  • Probability, random variables and distributions — this lesson assumes you know what a random variable is and what a PMF and a density are.
  • Arithmetic with fractions and square roots; summing a short list.
  • Nothing from algorithms. The mean of a list is a sum, not a data-structure operation.

Five ideas that decide almost every question here

  1. Mean is mass, median is position, mode is frequency. One outlier moves the mean and leaves the median alone; it moves the mode only if it becomes the most frequent value.
  2. Two exact minimisation facts. The mean is the unique point minimising the sum of squared deviations; the median minimises the sum of absolute deviations. These are theorems, provable in one line each, and they explain why the two averages differ.
  3. Shift and scale. Adding $$b$$ to every value adds $$b$$ to mean, median and mode and leaves the standard deviation alone. Multiplying by $$a$$ multiplies mean, median, mode and standard deviation by $$a$$ — but only the first three keep their sign; the standard deviation picks up $$|a|$$.
  4. Standard deviation is not the average absolute deviation. On this lesson's data those are 19.79 and 12.30. Squaring before averaging is what makes the variance add over independent variables, and that is the only reason for it.
  5. Ordering rules are not theorems. "Right-skewed means mode < median < mean" fails on a two-peaked list, which this page exhibits and computes.

Intuition first: one number for a crowd

Nine people in a small office are asked their monthly salary, in thousands of rupees: 22, 25, 25, 28, 30, 30, 30, 32, 90. Someone asks "what does a person here earn?" There are three honest answers and they are different numbers.

The mean asks: if all the money were pooled and shared out equally, what would each person get? That is the total divided by nine, and it gives 34.67. The median asks: line everyone up from poorest to richest and take the person in the middle. That is 30. The mode asks: what is the most common salary? That is also 30, appearing three times.

Educational interpretation The pooling image is an aid to memory, not a definition. The formal statements are below, and the animation draws all three on the same number line so you can see that they disagree.

Mean (arithmetic mean)

For values $$x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$$:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

In words: add every value, divide by how many there are. For grouped data, where value $$x_i$$ occurs with frequency $$f_i$$:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$$

For a random variable, the mean is the expectation $$E[X] = \sum x\,p(x)$$ or $$\int x f(x)\,dx$$.

Median

Sort the values into $$x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$$. Then

$$\text{median} = \begin{cases} x_{((n+1)/2)} & n \text{ odd} \\ \tfrac{1}{2}\left(x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2+1)}\right) & n \text{ even}\end{cases}$$

For a random variable, a median is any $$m$$ with $$P(X \le m) \ge \tfrac12$$ and $$P(X \ge m) \ge \tfrac12$$. When there is an atom — a value with positive probability — this definition can accept a whole interval, and the answer may be a value that the variable takes with probability one half.

Sorting is part of the definition. The median of an unsorted list is the median of its sorted version, and of nothing else.

Mode

The mode is a value that occurs with the highest frequency. It is not required to be unique: a list can have one mode, several modes, or none by the usual convention when every value occurs equally often.

For a discrete random variable it is a point maximising $$p(x)$$; for a continuous one, a point maximising the density $$f(x)$$. The mode is the only one of the three that makes sense for unordered categories such as blood group or city, which is why it exists at all.

Standard deviation

Variance is the mean squared deviation from the mean; the standard deviation is its square root:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$

Every symbol: $$x_i - \bar{x}$$ is how far the $$i$$-th value sits from the mean; squaring removes the sign; dividing by $$n$$ averages those squares; the square root returns the answer to the original units, so a salary spread of 19.79 means 19.79 thousand rupees, not a squared quantity.

Population against sample. Dividing by $$n$$ describes the list you have. Dividing by $$n-1$$ is the unbiased estimator of the variance of a larger population the list was drawn from, and it is larger by the factor $$n/(n-1)$$: on this list 20.99 against 19.79. GATE asks about a given finite list, so divide by $$n$$ unless the question says the list is a sample.

The two minimisation facts, and why they matter

For any constant $$c$$:

$$\sum (x_i - c)^2 \text{ is smallest at } c = \bar{x}; \qquad \sum |x_i - c| \text{ is smallest at } c = \text{median}$$

Mathematical derivation For the first: write $$\sum (x_i - c)^2 = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2 + n(\bar x - c)^2$$, which is the first expression plus a square, so it is minimal exactly when $$c = \bar x$$. For the second: moving $$c$$ right by a small amount decreases the sum by (number of values above $$c$$) and increases it by (number below), so the minimum is where the two counts balance — the median.

These are theorems with hypotheses: both hold for any finite list of real numbers, and neither needs any distributional assumption. They are the reason the mean is the right summary when errors are squared, and the median when they are absolute.

Grouped data: three interpolation formulas

With classes of common width $$h$$, lower boundaries $$L$$ and frequencies $$f$$:

$$\bar x = \frac{\sum f_i m_i}{\sum f_i}$$ ($$m_i$$ = class midpoint)

$$\text{median} = L_m + \frac{\tfrac{N}{2} - F}{f_m}\,h$$

$$\text{mode} = L_o + \frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2}\,h$$

$$F$$ is the cumulative frequency of the classes before the median class; $$f_1$$ is the modal frequency, $$f_0$$ and $$f_2$$ its neighbours (zero if there is none). All three are estimates: they assume the values inside each class are spread evenly, which is an assumption about data nobody saw.

Example 1 — the nine salaries, all four numbers

Data, in thousands of rupees: 22, 25, 25, 28, 30, 30, 30, 32, 90. Every figure below is computed on this page and printed on the canvas in scene 1.

Mean

Sum $$= 22+25+25+28+30+30+30+32+90 = 312$$, and $$n = 9$$, so $$\bar x = 312/9 = 34.667$$.

Check that the deviations cancel. The nine deviations from 34.667 add to 0.0000 — not approximately, exactly, up to the floating-point rounding the page reports. That is not luck: $$\sum (x_i - \bar x) = \sum x_i - n\bar x = 0$$.

Median and mode

Already sorted, nine values, so the median is the 5th: 30. The value 30 appears three times, more than any other, so the mode is 30.

Mean 34.67 against median 30: the single value 90 is dragging the mean up by 55.33/9 ≈ 6.15. Remove 90 and the mean of the remaining eight is 27.75, below the median of the original list. One observation moved the mean by nearly seven units and moved the median by nothing at all.

Standard deviation

Squared deviations from 34.667: 160.44, 93.44, 93.44, 44.44, 21.78, 21.78, 21.78, 7.11, 3061.78. Their mean is $$\sigma^2 = 391.778$$, so $$\sigma = 19.793$$.

The single value 90 contributes 3061.78 of the 3526 total — 87 per cent of the variance comes from one observation out of nine. Compare the mean absolute deviation, 12.296: the standard deviation is 61 per cent larger, because it squares.

The shortcut that is not available

$$\sigma = \text{mean absolute deviation}$$ is false. $$\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$ where $$\sigma^2$$ is the mean of the squared deviations. On these data 19.793 against 12.296. If a question gives you the deviations and asks for the spread, squaring before averaging is the whole computation.

Example 2 — a frequency table, all three interpolation formulas

Fifty observations in six classes of width 10. This is the example the animation builds in scene 5.

Classes and frequencies: 0–10: 4, 10–20: 8, 20–30: 15, 30–40: 12, 40–50: 6, 50–60: 5. $$N = 50$$.

Mean by midpoints

Midpoints 5, 15, 25, 35, 45, 55. Weighted sum $$= 4(5)+8(15)+15(25)+12(35)+6(45)+5(55) = 1480$$. Divide by 50: 29.6.

This is an approximation: it treats all 15 observations in 20–30 as sitting exactly at 25. It is exact only if the values really are the midpoints.

Median by cumulative frequency

$$N/2 = 25$$. Cumulative frequencies: 4, 12, 27, 39, 45, 50. The first to reach 25 is the third class, 20–30, so $$L_m = 20$$, $$F = 12$$, $$f_m = 15$$, $$h = 10$$.

Median $$= 20 + \frac{25 - 12}{15}\times 10 = 20 + 8.667 = 28.667$$. Notice it lands inside the median class, as it must: the fraction $$\frac{N/2 - F}{f_m}$$ lies between 0 and 1 by the choice of class.

Mode by the modal class

The largest frequency is 15, in 20–30, so $$L_o = 20$$, $$f_1 = 15$$, $$f_0 = 8$$, $$f_2 = 12$$.

Mode $$= 20 + \frac{15-8}{30-8-12}\times 10 = 20 + \frac{7}{10}\times 10 = 27.0$$. The formula pulls the estimate towards the neighbouring class that is larger — here 12 beats 8, so 27 sits above the class midpoint 25.

What the table already tells you

Mode 27.0 < median 28.667 < mean 29.6. The tail runs to the right (the 50–60 class is far out and still holds 5 observations), so the mean is the largest of the three. You could have predicted the order from the shape without computing any of them.

Example 3 — shift and scale, which is GATE CSE 2011

Take the nine salaries and form $$y_i = 3x_i + 100$$. The paper's own version uses $$y_i = a x_i + b$$ with $$a, b$$ positive.

What happens, computed

New mean: 204.0, and $$3(34.667) + 100 = 204.0$$. New standard deviation: 59.380, and $$3(19.793) = 59.380$$. New median 190 = $$3(30)+100$$; new mode 190 likewise.

The page checks all four equalities on the actual transformed list rather than asserting them.

Why each rule holds

Mathematical derivation Mean: $$\frac{1}{n}\sum (ax_i+b) = a\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i + b$$. Median: multiplying by positive $$a$$ preserves order, so the middle index is unchanged and the value there is mapped by the same rule. Mode: equal values stay equal, so the most frequent value maps to the most frequent value.

Variance: $$y_i - \bar y = a(x_i - \bar x)$$, so $$\sigma_y^2 = a^2\sigma_x^2$$ and $$\sigma_y = |a|\sigma_x$$. The $$b$$ cancels because it shifts the mean by exactly as much as it shifts every value.

GATE CSE 2011, and the option that is wrong

The paper offers four statements and asks which is INCORRECT: mode index position preserved, median index position preserved, $$\mu_y = a\mu_x + b$$, and $$\sigma_y = a\sigma_x + b$$.

The fourth is the answer. $$\sigma_y = a\sigma_x$$ with no $$b$$: adding a constant cannot change a spread. Note also that the paper says $$a$$ is positive; had it been negative, $$\sigma_y = |a|\sigma_x$$ and the sign would matter for the mean and median directions but still not for the spread.

Example 4 — the exponential distribution's three middles

With rate $$\lambda = 0.5$$: mean $$1/\lambda = 2$$, median $$\ln 2/\lambda = 1.386$$, mode 0 (the density $$0.5e^{-0.5x}$$ is largest at $$x = 0$$).

The page evaluates the CDF at the median and gets exactly 0.5. Here the three averages are three different numbers, and the gap between mean and median is a signature of the right skew — the long tail pulls the mean past the median, while the peak stays at the boundary.

Even $$n$$: the median need not be a value in the list

For 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 6 the median is $$(2+3)/2 = 2.5$$, and 2.5 is not one of the six observations. Any question that asks "which observation is the median" is therefore ill-posed for even $$n$$; a question asking for the median as a number is fine.

For a continuous random variable this is the normal case: with probability one the median is not a value the variable can take at all.

Mode: zero, one, or several

1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — every value occurs once, so by convention there is no mode. 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4 — two values tie at the top, so the list is bimodal, and any statement of "the mode" must name both.

A uniform continuous distribution is the extreme case: every point in the support attains the same maximum density, so the mode is not unique and no single number is the mode. A question with "the mode of U(a,b) is ___" has no answer unless it says "any point of (a,b)".

The skew ordering rule fails on a two-peaked list

On 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 6: mean 3.0, median 2.5, mode 6. So the mean exceeds the median — the sign usually read as "right-skewed" — while the mode sits far above the mean.

The rule "mode < median < mean when skewed right" is an empirical description of single-peaked, moderately skewed data, not a theorem. Here the second peak at 6 supplies the mode and a gap in the middle supplies the skew, and the two pull in opposite directions.

Grouped formulas assume what nobody checked

Both the median and the mode formulas interpolate linearly inside a class. Two different underlying data sets can produce the same frequency table and genuinely different medians.

The formulas also require classes of equal width. Feed a table with unequal widths into the mode formula and the number it returns is meaningless, because the largest frequency then reflects the widest class rather than the densest one.

When the mean does not exist

The mean requires $$\sum |x|\,p(x)$$ or $$\int |x| f(x)\,dx$$ to converge. The Cauchy distribution has a perfectly good density and no mean; a heavy-tailed distribution can have a finite mean and an infinite variance, in which case the standard deviation does not exist either.

Every distribution named in the syllabus — uniform, normal, exponential, Poisson, binomial — has finite mean and variance, so this trap is about reading definitions carefully, not about the named five.

$$n$$ or $$n-1$$ changes the answer

On the nine salaries, dividing by 9 gives $$\sigma = 19.793$$; dividing by 8 gives $$s = 20.994$$. Both are computed on the page.

GATE gives you a finite list and asks for its standard deviation: divide by $$n$$. The $$n-1$$ version belongs to a sample used to estimate a population, and a question will say so if it wants it. Answering with the wrong divisor is a silent one-mark loss, since both numbers look plausible.

A single outlier can dominate the variance

In the nine salaries, 87 per cent of the total squared deviation comes from the value 90. Drop it and $$\sigma$$ falls from 19.79 to 7.4 on the remaining eight values.

This is why robust statistics prefers the median and the interquartile range: they are built from positions, so one arbitrary value cannot move them by more than one position.

Order matters for the median, not for the mean

The mean is symmetric: permuting the list leaves it unchanged, because addition commutes. The median needs the sorted order, so the index of the median is preserved only under order-preserving maps — which is exactly what GATE CSE 2011's options (A) and (B) assert, and why they are true for positive $$a$$.

With negative $$a$$ the order reverses, the median value maps correctly, but its index position moves from $$k$$ to $$n+1-k$$. The paper avoided that case by saying $$a$$ is positive.

"The mean is the typical value"

Why it tempts: it is the average everyone learns first, and for symmetric data it is typical. What breaks it: the nine salaries have mean 34.67 and no one earns between 32 and 90; eight of the nine earn below the mean.

Correct rule: the mean is a balance point, not a representative member. If the question says "typical", check the median before answering.

"The median is always one of the data values"

Why it tempts: for odd $$n$$ it is, and most classroom examples use odd $$n$$. Counterexample: 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 6 has median 2.5, which appears nowhere in the list.

Correct rule: for even $$n$$ the median is the average of the two middle values, and that average may be a value the data never take.

"Every list has exactly one mode"

Why it tempts: the word "the" suggests uniqueness. Counterexamples: 1,2,3,4,5 has none; 1,1,2,2,3,4 has two.

Correct rule: the mode is the set of maximally frequent values. It can be empty, a singleton, or larger — and for a continuous distribution with a flat density it is the whole support.

"Standard deviation is the average distance from the mean"

Why it tempts: the words "mean squared deviation" get shortened in memory. What breaks it: on the salaries the mean absolute deviation is 12.296 and the standard deviation is 19.793 — a 61 per cent gap.

Correct rule: square, average, then take the root. Only the squaring makes the variance of a sum of independent variables add, which is the property the rest of probability depends on.

"Adding a constant shifts the standard deviation too"

Why it tempts: mean, median and mode all shift, so it feels uniform. What breaks it: GATE CSE 2011 offered exactly this option as the incorrect statement, and the transformed list here gives $$\sigma_y = 59.380 = 3\sigma_x$$ with the 100 absent.

Correct rule: $$\sigma(ax+b) = |a|\,\sigma(x)$$. Shifts never move a spread; only scaling does, and only by its magnitude.

"Mode < median < mean whenever the data are skewed right"

Why it tempts: it holds on almost every textbook picture. Counterexample: 0,1,2,3,6,6 has mean 3.0 > median 2.5 but mode 6, far above both.

Correct rule: treat the ordering as a description of single-peaked, moderately skewed data. Use it to sanity-check an answer, never to derive one.

"mode = 3·median − 2·mean is a formula"

Why it tempts: it is printed in textbooks with an equals sign. What breaks it: it is an approximation for moderately skewed unimodal data. On the frequency table it gives 26.8 against a computed mode of 27.0 — good here, and easily off by several units elsewhere.

Correct rule: use it only when a question supplies two of the three and asks for the third, and say it is approximate. Never use it to check a mode you can compute directly.

"The sample formula with $$n-1$$ is the more correct one"

Why it tempts: it is "unbiased", which sounds strictly better. What breaks it: they answer different questions. For a list that is the whole population, dividing by $$n-1$$ understates the spread of that population; on the salaries it gives 20.994 instead of 19.793.

Correct rule: follow the question's wording. A finite list described as the data → divide by $$n$$. A sample used to estimate a population → divide by $$n-1$$.

Five shapes this clause takes

Every verified item below is quoted on the Examples or Practice tab with its paper. The list is a description of question shapes, not a claim about how many will appear in 2027.

1. Transformation of a sequence

Shape: a list is mapped by $$y = ax + b$$ and you must say which statement about mean, median, mode and standard deviation is false.

Reasoning demanded: know that the three averages are linear and the spread is homogeneous of degree one, with $$|a|$$.

Common wrong approach: apply the same $$+b$$ to the spread, which is exactly the distractor GATE CSE 2011 built.

2. A statement battery (MSQ-style)

Shape: four assertions about adding 7 and doubling, choose the correct pair. This is GATE CSE 2012's General Aptitude item.

Reasoning demanded: two rules — shift moves the mean not the spread, scale moves both — applied four times, quickly.

Common wrong approach: assuming doubling leaves the standard deviation unchanged because it "rescales everything equally".

3. Standard deviation of a named distribution

Shape: a binomial or Poisson model described in words, a numerical answer for the standard deviation. GATE CSE 2021 Set 2 asked for $$\sqrt{np(1-p)}$$ on a biased coin with $$n = 1000$$, $$p = 0.4$$.

Reasoning demanded: recognise the model, recall $$\mathrm{Var} = np(1-p)$$, and remember the root — the variance is 240, the answer is 15.49.

Common wrong approach: reporting 240, or computing $$\sqrt{1000 \times 0.4}$$ and forgetting the $$(1-p)$$.

4. Median of a transformed or truncated variable

Shape: $$Y = \max(X,0)$$ for a symmetric $$X$$, or an atom placed at zero; find the median. GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 asked exactly this and the answer is 0.

Reasoning demanded: use the definition $$P(Y \le m) \ge \tfrac12$$ and $$P(Y \ge m) \ge \tfrac12$$; notice that half the mass sits on a single point.

Common wrong approach: computing the mean of $$Y$$, which is $$\sigma/\sqrt{2\pi}$$ and not the median at all.

5. Reading a frequency table

Shape: a grouped table, then a mean, median or mode computed by interpolation. This is the shape most often mis-answered, because three formulas compete.

Reasoning demanded: pick the right formula from the wording, find the median class by cumulative frequency, and respect the equal-width assumption.

Common wrong approach: using the modal class midpoint as the mode, or the median class midpoint as the median.

The 20-second triage

Before computing anything: is the question about a list or a distribution? If a list — raw or grouped? If a transformation is involved, apply the two rules and never recompute from scratch. If a distribution is named, use its parameters. Then check whether the answer should be a value from the data (mean and mode: maybe; median: only for odd $$n$$).

MCQGATE CSE 2011, 2 marks

Consider a finite sequence of random values $$X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$$. Let $$\mu_x$$ be the mean and $$\sigma_x$$ the standard deviation of $$X$$. Let another finite sequence $$Y$$ of equal length be derived as $$y_i = a x_i + b$$, where $$a$$ and $$b$$ are positive constants. Let $$\mu_y$$ and $$\sigma_y$$ be the mean and standard deviation of $$Y$$. Which one of the following statements is INCORRECT?

  1. Index position of mode of $$X$$ in $$X$$ is the same as the index position of mode of $$Y$$ in $$Y$$
  2. Index position of median of $$X$$ in $$X$$ is the same as the index position of median of $$Y$$ in $$Y$$
  3. $$\mu_y = a\mu_x + b$$
  4. $$\sigma_y = a\sigma_x + b$$

Answer: D

Because $$a > 0$$, the map is strictly increasing and one-to-one: equal values stay equal, so the most frequent value maps to the most frequent value at the same index (A holds), and the ordering is preserved, so the middle element stays the middle element (B holds). The mean is linear (C holds). The standard deviation satisfies $$\sigma_y = a\sigma_x$$, because $$y_i - \mu_y = a(x_i - \mu_x)$$ and the constant $$b$$ cancels; adding $$b$$ cannot change a spread.

Why D is the answer: it adds $$b$$ to the spread. On this page's data: with $$a=3$$, $$b=100$$ on the nine salaries, $$\sigma_x = 19.793$$ and $$\sigma_y = 59.380 = 3\sigma_x$$; $$3\sigma_x + 100 = 159.4$$ is not the standard deviation of anything.

Assumption worth naming: $$a$$ positive. With $$a<0$$, option B would become false in the index sense (the order reverses), so the paper's positivity is load-bearing.

NATGATE CSE 2021 Set 2, 1 mark

For a given biased coin, the probability that the outcome of a toss is a head is 0.4. This coin is tossed 1,000 times. Let $$X$$ denote the random variable whose value is the number of times that head appeared in these 1,000 tosses. The standard deviation of $$X$$ (rounded to 2 decimal places) is ______.

Answer to two decimal places.

Answer: 15.49

$$X$$ counts successes in 1,000 independent tosses with the same probability, so $$X \sim \text{Binomial}(n=1000, p=0.4)$$. Then $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p) = 1000 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 240$$ and $$\sigma = \sqrt{240} = 15.4919\ldots$$, which rounds to 15.49. The official key accepts a band around it; the computed value on this page is 15.4919.

Traps: reporting the variance 240; or forgetting the $$(1-p)$$ and answering $$\sqrt{400} = 20$$; or computing the standard error $$\sqrt{p(1-p)/n} = 0.0155$$, which describes the proportion, not the count.

NATGATE CSE 2017 Set 1

Let $$X$$ be a Gaussian random variable with mean 0 and variance $$\sigma^2$$. Let $$Y = \max(X, 0)$$, where $$\max(a,b)$$ is the maximum of $$a$$ and $$b$$. The median of $$Y$$ is ______.

Answer as a number.

Answer: 0

Half the mass of a symmetric variable lies below 0, and on that half $$Y = 0$$ exactly. So $$P(Y = 0) = \tfrac12$$, hence $$P(Y \le 0) = \tfrac12 \ge \tfrac12$$ and $$P(Y \ge 0) = 1 \ge \tfrac12$$. By the definition of a median for a variable with an atom, 0 is a median; it is the only one, since any $$m>0$$ has $$P(Y \le m) = \tfrac12 + P(0 < X \le m) > \tfrac12$$ but $$P(Y \ge m) = P(X \ge m) < \tfrac12$$.

Trap: computing the mean of $$Y$$, which is $$\sigma/\sqrt{2\pi}$$. The mean and the median of a truncated variable are different numbers, and the question asked for the median. This is the atom case the definition was written to handle.

MCQGATE CSE 2012 (General Aptitude), 2 marks

Which of the following assertions are CORRECT?

P: Adding 7 to each entry in a list adds 7 to the mean of the list.
Q: Adding 7 to each entry in a list adds 7 to the standard deviation of the list.
R: Doubling each entry in a list doubles the mean of the list.
S: Doubling each entry in a list leaves the standard deviation of the list unchanged.

  1. P, Q
  2. Q, R
  3. P, R
  4. R, S

Answer: C — P and R

P is true and Q is false: a shift moves the mean by the same amount and leaves every deviation $$x_i - \bar x$$ untouched, so the standard deviation does not change. R is true and S is false: doubling multiplies each deviation by 2, so the standard deviation doubles too.

Computed on this page: the nine salaries give $$\sigma = 19.793$$; adding 7 to each entry leaves it at 19.793, and doubling gives 39.587. Only the second is a change.

Why the other options fail: A includes Q, which is the classic error; B drops P and includes Q; D includes S, which contradicts the scaling rule. The pair (P, R) is the only one that survives both rules.

A frequency table has classes 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40 with frequencies 4, 8, 15, 12. Using the standard interpolation formulas, what is the median, to two decimal places?

Answer: 25.00

$$N = 39$$, so $$N/2 = 19.5$$. Cumulative frequencies are 4, 12, 27, 39; the first to reach 19.5 is the third class, so the median class is 20–30 with $$L_m = 20$$, $$F = 12$$, $$f_m = 15$$, $$h = 10$$. Median $$= 20 + \frac{19.5-12}{15}\times 10 = 20 + 5 = 25.00$$.

Trap: forgetting to subtract $$F$$, giving $$20 + 19.5 = 39.5$$, which is outside the table; or taking $$N/2 = 19.5$$ but choosing the second class because its cumulative frequency 12 is "close". The rule is the first class whose cumulative frequency reaches $$N/2$$, and 27 is the first that does. Assumption: equal class widths, with values spread evenly inside each class.

MSQOriginal practice question

For the list 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 6, which of the following are true?

  1. The mean is greater than the median
  2. The mode is greater than the mean
  3. The median is one of the values in the list
  4. The list has exactly one mode

Answer: A, B, D

Mean $$= 18/6 = 3.0$$; median $$= (2+3)/2 = 2.5$$; the value 6 occurs twice and nothing else occurs twice, so the mode is 6 and it is unique. So mean 3.0 > median 2.5 (A), mode 6 > mean 3.0 (B), one mode (D), and the median 2.5 is not in the list, so C is false.

Why this item exists: A and B together are the counterexample to "right-skewed means mode < median < mean". The mean exceeds the median, yet the mode is far above both. C fails on the even-$$n$$ averaging convention.

NATOriginal practice question

The list 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 has mean 5 and standard deviation $$\sigma$$. Give the value of $$\sigma^2$$.

Answer: 4

Deviations from 5: −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4. Squares: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16, summing to 32. Divide by $$n = 8$$: $$\sigma^2 = 4$$, so $$\sigma = 2$$.

Trap: dividing by 7 gives 4.571, the sample variance. The question says "the list", so $$n = 8$$. Shortcut: $$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2 - \bar x^2$$ gives the same 4 and avoids subtracting eight times — the two routes are checked to agree on the page.

MCQOriginal practice question

Which statement about the exponential distribution with rate $$\lambda$$ is true?

  1. Mean, median and mode are all equal to $$1/\lambda$$
  2. The median is $$\ln 2/\lambda$$, which is less than the mean, and the mode is 0
  3. The mode is $$1/\lambda$$ and the median is larger than the mean
  4. The mode does not exist because the density is decreasing everywhere

Answer: B

The mean is $$1/\lambda$$; solving $$1 - e^{-\lambda m} = \tfrac12$$ gives $$m = \ln 2/\lambda \approx 0.693/\lambda$$, so the median is about 69 per cent of the mean; the density $$\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$$ is largest at $$x = 0$$, so the mode is 0. With $$\lambda = 0.5$$: mean 2, median 1.386, mode 0, and the page evaluates the CDF at 1.386 and gets 0.5.

Why the others fail: A describes the normal distribution, not this one. C reverses the inequality, which no right-skewed distribution does. D confuses "the density is largest at the boundary" with "there is no maximum" — 0 is a perfectly good mode, and it is the reason the mode of a continuous distribution can sit outside the bulk of the mass.

The four definitions

Mean $$\bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$$ — mass.

Median — middle of the sorted list; average the two middles when $$n$$ is even.

Mode — maximally frequent value; may be none, one, or several.

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$$, $$\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$; divide by $$n-1$$ only for a sample.

Transformation rules

$$y = ax + b$$, with $$a>0$$:

$$\mu_y = a\mu_x + b$$

median and mode transform the same way

$$\sigma_y = |a|\,\sigma_x$$ — the $$b$$ never appears

Order statistics keep their index positions; with $$a<0$$ the order reverses.

Grouped data, three formulas

Mean: $$\sum f_i m_i / \sum f_i$$, midpoints $$m_i$$.

Median: $$L_m + \frac{N/2 - F}{f_m}h$$, median class = first with cumulative frequency $$\ge N/2$$.

Mode: $$L_o + \frac{f_1-f_0}{2f_1-f_0-f_2}h$$, modal class = largest frequency. Equal widths required.

The five named distributions

Uniform$$(a,b)$$: mean and median $$(a+b)/2$$; mode not unique.

Normal$$(\mu,\sigma^2)$$: mean = median = mode = $$\mu$$.

Exponential$$(\lambda)$$: mean $$1/\lambda$$, median $$\ln 2/\lambda$$, mode 0.

Poisson$$(\lambda)$$: mean = variance = $$\lambda$$; mode $$\lfloor \lambda \rfloor$$ (two modes if $$\lambda$$ is an integer).

Binomial$$(n,p)$$: mean $$np$$, variance $$np(1-p)$$, mode $$\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$$.

Two exact facts to quote

$$\sum (x_i - c)^2$$ is minimised at $$c = \bar x$$; the minimum value is $$n\sigma^2$$.

$$\sum |x_i - c|$$ is minimised at any median; for even $$n$$ every point between the two middle values ties.

Traps, in one line each

Median of even $$n$$ may not be a data value. Mode may not exist. Uniform has no unique mode. $$\sigma \ne$$ mean absolute deviation. Adding a constant never changes $$\sigma$$. $$n$$ against $$n-1$$ changes the answer. The empirical relation is approximate. Skew ordering is not a theorem.
