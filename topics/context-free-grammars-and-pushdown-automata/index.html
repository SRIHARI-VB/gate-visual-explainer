Topic and objective

TOPIC: Context-free grammars and pushdown automata — the second clause of GATE 2027 Section 6, Theory of Computation, together with the pumping lemma for context-free languages.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a grammar, read off the language and say whether the grammar is ambiguous. Given a language described in words, decide whether one stack is enough, and prove the answer either by exhibiting a grammar or machine or by refuting it with the pumping lemma. Convert a grammar to Chomsky normal form and say what the binary tree buys. Name the operations the class is closed under, the two it is not, and the one exception that resolves most questions.

I read this topic as one question asked at four scales: how many independent comparisons does the language demand? A grammar or a machine answers it for one stack, the normal form prices the tree, the class boundary answers it for whole families of languages, and the pumping lemma refutes it when no construction exists. Every definition, count and previous-year item below is that sentence at a different scale.

Prerequisites

  • Strings, languages and the empty string. A language is a set of strings, and almost every trap in this lesson is about whether ε is in it. Built in Sets and Set Operations.
  • Machines with finitely many states. A pushdown machine is a finite machine plus one stack, so the whole finite layer is assumed. Built in Regular Expressions and Finite Automata.
  • The pumping lemma for regular languages. The context-free version is the same argument one level up, and the comparison is the fastest way to remember which pieces move together. Also in the regular-expressions lesson.
  • Sets of states and the product construction. Closure under intersection with a regular language is a product machine. Built in Functions and Their Properties and the regular-expressions lesson.
  • Recurrence and counting. The number of parse trees for n operators is a Catalan number, which is a recurrence. Built in Recurrence Relations and Counting and Combinatorics.

Nothing about Turing machines or undecidability is assumed; that is the next lesson in this subject. Nothing about parsing is assumed either — FIRST and FOLLOW belong to Compiler Design, and this lesson stops at the grammar.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. The stack is last-in-first-out, so it can compare a block with the reverse of an earlier one, or with a count. It cannot compare a block with an earlier block in the same order.
  2. One independent comparison is context-free. Two that cross are not. Two that nest usually are.
  3. Ambiguity is a property of a grammar, measured by counting trees for one string. Inherent ambiguity is a property of a language and needs a theorem.
  4. The class is closed under union and not under intersection. Intersecting with a regular language is the exception, and it is the exception exams lean on.
  5. The pumping lemma refutes and never certifies. A proof that a language is context-free is a grammar or a machine, and nothing else.

Intuition: the counter you can only read from the top

Imagine a clerk checking that a queue of visitors arrives in pairs. He has a stack of cards and one rule: he may look only at the top card. For each visitor of the first kind he puts a card on; for each of the second kind he takes one off. If the stack is empty exactly when the queue ends, the two kinds came in equal numbers. That is a pushdown machine, and it is the whole of this topic.

Now ask him to check something harder: that the second half of the queue repeats the first half in the same order. He can stack the first half, but the cards come back off in reverse, so he reads the second half backwards and gets nonsense. Ask instead that the second half repeats the first half backwards, and the same stack works perfectly. One sentence explains both the power and the limit: whatever goes on comes off reversed.

Educational interpretation The analogy is a teaching device. It fixes the one fact every question in this topic turns on: a stack can match a mirror and a count, and it cannot match a copy.

Formal vocabulary, every symbol glossed

Grammar G = (V, Σ, P, S). V is the finite set of nonterminals — placeholders such as S. Σ is the finite set of terminals — the symbols of the final string. P is a finite set of rules, each written A → α with A one nonterminal and α a string of symbols. S is the start symbol and must be a nonterminal.

Derivation. A sequence of strings, each obtained from the previous by replacing one nonterminal with the right side of one of its rules. A string with no nonterminals left is a sentence, and its terminals are the string it derives.

L(G) is the set of sentences derivable from S. A language is context-free exactly when some grammar generates it.

PDA M = (Q, Σ, Γ, δ, q0, Z0, F). States, input alphabet, stack alphabet, transition function, start state, bottom-of-stack symbol, accepting states. δ(q, a, X) is a set of pairs (q′, γ): in state q, reading a (or reading nothing, when a = ε), with X on top, go to q′ and replace X by the string γ.

The one sentence the whole topic turns on

A stack is a last-in-first-out store. Push the block w and pop it later, and what comes out is w reversed. So a machine with one stack can check that a later block is the reverse of an earlier one, and it can check that two blocks have the same length, because a length has no order.

stack reads back the reverse of what it stored

Every classification in this lesson follows from that line. The mirror language works. The copy language does not. Equal counts work. Three equal counts do not, because the second comparison needs a second counter.

Three checks that catch most slips

Check one. Count comparisons, not symbols. A language over three letters may need one comparison or two, and the alphabet size says nothing.

Check two. Ask about ε separately. It is the exception in Chomsky normal form, it is the difference between "all palindromes" and "all odd-length palindromes", and it decides whether a grammar's language is closed under reversal in the obvious way.

Check three. Every claim needs a witness. To say a language is context-free, name the grammar. To say it is not, name the string and refute every split. To say a grammar is ambiguous, draw both trees.

Derivations, trees, and what ambiguity actually measures

A derivation is a sequence of replacements. A tree keeps the structure and drops the order. Almost every mistake in this topic comes from confusing the two.

Leftmost derivations, and why the order does not matter

A leftmost derivation always expands the leftmost nonterminal. Any derivation can be reordered into a leftmost one without changing the string it produces, so restricting to leftmost order loses no strings — which is why the model on this page enumerates the language that way.

What the order cannot change is the tree. Two derivations that differ only in order give the same tree; two derivations that give different trees are genuinely different structures. Ambiguity is about trees.

Ambiguity, with the smallest witness

With E → E + E | E * E | i, the string i+i*i has exactly two trees: one that multiplies first and one that adds first. The page counts them by dynamic programming over substrings and gets 2, then checks the same count by brute-force derivation.

Replace the rules by E → E + T | T, T → T * F | F, F → i and every string has exactly one tree, while the language is unchanged — verified here on every string up to length five.

Grammar ambiguity is not language ambiguity

A grammar is ambiguous when some string has two trees. A language is inherently ambiguous when no grammar for it is unambiguous. The second is a much stronger statement and cannot be shown by exhibiting one bad grammar.

Inherently ambiguous context-free languages exist; the standard example is the union of {aibjck : i = j} with {aibjck : j = k}. This page quotes that result and does not attempt its proof.

Chomsky normal form in four moves

1. Drop useless symbols. A symbol that can never produce a terminal string, or that no derivation reaches from S, contributes nothing. Removing it never changes the language.

2. Drop ε-rules. Compute the nullable nonterminals, then for every rule add all variants that omit some nullable symbols. A rule A → B C with both nullable yields A → B C, A → B, A → C.

3. Drop unit rules. For each A, take the transitive closure of A ⇒ B and copy B's non-unit rules onto A.

4. Binarise. Replace each terminal inside a long right side by a fresh nonterminal that produces it, then split every right side longer than two with fresh nonterminals. The result has only A → B C and A → a, and the empty string survives only if the start symbol produces it and appears in no right side.

The machine, the run, and the two ways to accept

One stack, one head that only moves forward, one top that is all the machine can see. Everything else is bookkeeping.

A move, in full

δ(q, a, X) = (q′, γ) means: in state q, reading input symbol a (or nothing at all when a = ε), with X on top of the stack, go to q′ and replace X by γ, whose first symbol becomes the new top.

So a push of A is γ = A X; a pop is γ = ε; a replace is γ = A B X. All three are the same operation written three ways, which is why the page draws one arrow for all of them.

Acceptance, two conventions

By final state: the input is exhausted and the machine stands in an accepting state, whatever is left on the stack. By empty stack: the input is exhausted and the stack is empty.

For nondeterministic machines the two conventions describe the same class of languages, and either can be converted into the other. For deterministic machines the correspondence needs care about how the input ends, which is why textbooks state the equivalence for the nondeterministic case.

What one stack can do, in one table

anbn. Push on a, pop on b. One counter. Context-free, and deterministic.

wwR. Push w, then pop against the second half. Needs a guess about where w ends, so it is context-free and not deterministic.

ww. The stack returns w reversed, so the copy cannot be checked. Not context-free.

anbncn. Two comparisons, one counter. Not context-free. Free the third block and it becomes context-free again.

Determinism is not free here

For finite machines, deterministic and nondeterministic versions accept the same languages. For pushdown machines they do not: the deterministic machines accept a proper subclass, because they cannot guess where the middle of a string is.

GATE CSE 2011 asked which pair of models differs in expressive power, and this is the pair. The other three pairs in that question are equal in power, which is exactly why the option set looks plausible.

From grammar to machine, and back

Grammar to machine. One state plus a loop. For a rule A → X1X2…Xk, pop A and push Xk … X1 so that X1 sits on top, then match terminals against the input. The stack holds the unexpanded part of the sentential form, which is why the construction is correct by construction.

Machine to grammar. Take nonterminals of the form [q A p] meaning "from state q with A on top, reach state p having popped A", and write rules that mirror the machine's moves. The construction is mechanical and produces a grammar whose derivations are exactly the accepting runs. Neither direction is the fast way to answer a question by hand; for that, describe the stack discipline in words and check it covers every symbol.

Worked examples, page 1 of 2

Every figure below is recomputed by this page's own model, and the Animation tab lists the checks. Nothing is copied from a key without being re-derived.

From S → aSb | ε to the language, and back

Step 1 — derive a few strings. S ⇒ ε, S ⇒ aSb ⇒ ab, S ⇒ aSb ⇒ aaSbb ⇒ aabb. The pattern is anbn.

Step 2 — argue the upper bound. Every use of S → aSb adds exactly one a and one b, and the only other rule adds nothing, so every generated string has equal counts and all a's precede all b's.

Step 3 — argue the lower bound. For any n, apply S → aSb n times and then S → ε. So every anbn is generated. Mathematical derivation The model confirms the two sets agree on all 127 strings of length at most six.

Two trees for i+i*i, and the count for four operators

Step 1 — find the first tree. E ⇒ E + E ⇒ i + E ⇒ i + E * E ⇒ i + i * i. The multiplication sits below the addition.

Step 2 — find the second. E ⇒ E * E ⇒ E + E * E ⇒ i + E * E ⇒ i + i * i. Same string, different structure: the addition sits below.

Step 3 — generalise. For n operators the count is the Catalan number Cn: 1, 1, 2, 5, 14. The page counts 2 for two operators and 5 for three, by dynamic programming, and agrees with brute-force derivation.

Closely confused: w wR against w anbnwR

w wR. Three rules: S → aSa | bSb | ε. The stack is pushed while reading the first half and popped against the second, which is the mirror — so the LIFO discipline fits exactly.

w anbn wR. Four rules: the same three, with the ε replaced by A and A → aAb | ε. Now the middle carries its own comparison, and the outer mirror still works because the middle is fully reduced before the popping starts.

Why they are worth separating. Both are context-free, both are checked here against their definitions on every string up to length six, and they differ by one rule. GATE CSE 2019 put both in the same option set, with a third language that is not context-free.

Worked examples, page 2 of 2

Three counts and one refutation. The first three are the same move — name the comparison, then name the block that pays for it.

anbncm, and why the third block is free

Step 1 — name the comparison. Only a and b are tied together. The count of c is unconstrained, and an unconstrained count needs no memory at all.

Step 2 — write the grammar. S → A C, A → aAb | ε, C → cC | ε. The comparison lives entirely inside A.

Step 3 — check it. The model compares the grammar's language with the definition on all 121 strings up to length four and finds no disagreement. A machine does the same job: push on a, pop on b, then ignore the c's.

ambncn, and their intersection

Step 1 — the mirror grammar. S → A B, A → aA | ε, B → bBc | ε. Now the comparison is between b and c, and a is free.

Step 2 — intersect. A string in both languages must have #a = #b and #b = #c, so it is exactly anbncn. The page computes both sides up to length six and confirms the equality.

Step 3 — conclude. Both languages are context-free, their intersection is not, so the class is not closed under intersection. Mathematical derivation This is GATE CSE 2017 Set 1's question, and the answer is that only the union is context-free.

The refutation, done exhaustively rather than rhetorically

The claim. Suppose anbncn were context-free, with pumping constant p. Take s = apbpcp, of length 3p ≥ p.

The splits. Every legal split writes s = uvwxy with |vwx| ≤ p and |vx| ≥ 1. Because vwx is short, it can touch at most two of the three blocks. Pumping v and x therefore changes the counts in at most two blocks while leaving the third fixed, and anbncn requires all three equal.

The check. Rather than trust that argument, this page enumerates every legal split of a2b2c2 and of a3b3c3, and for each one looks for a repetition count in {0, 2, 3} that leaves the language. It reports 37 and 121 splits with zero survivors. The enumeration is exhaustive for those p and illustrates an argument that is uniform in p; the proof for every p is the paragraph above, not the count.

Where each rule stops holding

Every rule in this topic carries a condition. These are the conditions, and what happens on the other side of each.

The empty string is the exception everywhere

Chomsky normal form forbids A → ε, so a language containing ε gets one exception: S → ε, and S must not appear on any right side. Drop that clause and the normal-form theorem is false.

The same string separates "all palindromes" from "all odd-length palindromes", and it is why S → aSa | bSb | a | b is not the grammar of all palindromes: ε is a palindrome and that grammar cannot produce it.

A string that begins and ends alike is not a palindrome

For the grammar S → aSa | bSb | a | b, the tempting wrong option is "strings that begin and end with the same symbol". The string aaba begins and ends with a and is not generated, because the grammar forces every inner symbol to mirror.

This is GATE CSE 2009's option C, and it is the option that reads best. Generate three or four strings before choosing.

A union of context-free languages is context-free; an intersection need not be

The union of anbncm and ambncn has a five-rule grammar, checked here up to length five. Their intersection is anbncn and is not.

So a language that looks like "either of two conditions" is usually safe, and one that looks like "both conditions at once" usually is not. The quantifier, not the shape, decides.

Complement is not the mirror image of membership

The copy language ww is not context-free and its complement is. So the class is not closed under complement, and a language and its complement can sit on opposite sides of the boundary.

Deterministic context-free languages are closed under complement. That asymmetry is real, and it is why the deterministic subclass gets its own name in this chapter.

Two comparisons are fatal, but nesting is not

anbncn fails because the two comparisons cross: a with b, then b with c, sharing a block. The mirror language nests instead — the inner comparison is finished before the outer one begins — so one stack serves both.

The reliable test is the order in which the comparisons finish, not how many there are. Finished-last-out is what a stack is for.

The pumping lemma survives on languages that are not context-free

The lemma is necessary, not sufficient. Languages that satisfy it and are still outside the class exist, so satisfying it proves nothing.

The practical consequence: never answer "prove this language is context-free" with the pumping lemma. Give a grammar, a machine, or a closure argument from a language already known to be context-free.

The pumping constant belongs to the grammar, not to the language

Two grammars for the same language may give different constants p, and both are valid. So a refutation must work for an arbitrary p — which is why the honest procedure is to pick a string whose blocks all depend on p, then argue uniformly, rather than to fix p = 3 and enumerate.

The page enumerates for small p because a machine can do that and a reader can check it. That enumeration is a check on the argument, never a substitute for it.

Eight beliefs that cost marks

Each is stated as a learner would state it, then broken with the smallest counterexample available.

1. "Two derivations mean the grammar is ambiguous"

Why it is wrong. A derivation is a sequence of replacements, and two sequences can differ only in the order they expand nonterminals while producing the same tree. Ambiguity needs two distinct trees.

The check. Count trees, not derivation orders. This page's inside algorithm counts trees and returns 1 for the levelled expression grammar on every string tested, though that grammar has many derivation orders per string.

2. "Fixing precedence changes the language"

Why it is wrong. Splitting E into E, T and F restricts which trees exist, not which strings. The page compares the two grammars' languages on every string up to length five and finds them identical.

Where it is tested. Any question that asks whether a refactoring preserves the language. Precedence, left factoring and normal forms all preserve it; only the trees change.

3. "A stack can check that a string is w w"

Why it is wrong. Pushing w and popping gives w reversed, which matches wR and not w. The copy language needs the first half in the same order, which a stack cannot deliver.

Where it is tested. GATE CSE 2022 asks which statements about ww are false; the trap is to assume that because wwR is easy, ww must be too.

4. "DFA equals NFA, so DPDA equals NPDA"

Why it is wrong. The finite case is special. A deterministic pushdown machine cannot guess where the middle of a string is, so wwR has a nondeterministic machine and no deterministic one.

Where it is tested. GATE CSE 2011, whose answer is exactly this pair. The other three pairs in the question are equal in power.

5. "The class is closed under intersection, like union"

Why it is wrong. anbncm and ambncn are both context-free and their intersection is anbncn, which is not.

What to remember instead. Intersection with a regular language stays inside the class, and that is the version almost every question actually needs.

6. "If a language is not context-free, its complement is not either"

Why it is wrong. The complement of ww is context-free: a machine guesses one position where the two halves disagree and checks it. Non-membership of the class is not inherited by the complement.

Where it is tested. GATE CSE 2022, statement D, which is false for exactly this reason.

7. "The pumping lemma can prove a language is context-free"

Why it is wrong. It is necessary, not sufficient. Satisfying it is consistent with being outside the class, so it refutes and never certifies.

The safe route. A grammar, a machine, or closure from a known member. Anything else is not a proof of yes.

8. "The pumping lemma pumps one block, like the regular one"

Why it is wrong. The context-free lemma pumps two pieces, v and x, together, and constrains the middle vwx rather than the prefix. The proof walks a tree, not a chain of states, and the tree branches.

What it costs. Using the regular conditions on a context-free question produces refutations that are invalid, and the answer looks right for the wrong reason.

Two confusions that are not quite misconceptions

"The empty string is in every context-free language." It is in some, and its presence is decided by whether the start symbol is nullable. It is also the single exception in Chomsky normal form, so it must be handled rather than assumed.

"A language with one visible comparison must be context-free." Counting comparisons is a heuristic for reading a question quickly. The proof obligation is unchanged: a construction for yes, an exhaustive refutation for no.

The test to apply before answering

Ask what the stack must hand back, and in what order. If the answer is "reversed", one stack is enough. If it is "in the same order", no stack is. If it is "the same count", one stack is enough, and if it is two counts at once, it is not — unless the two comparisons nest, in which case the inner one is finished before the outer begins and one stack still serves. Every classification, count and refutation in this lesson is that single question, and it is answerable before any grammar is written.

What this clause actually asks

The items quoted across this lesson run from 2009 to 2022. Five shapes account for almost all of them: pick the non-context-free language out of four, judge a union and an intersection of the same pair, rule on a cluster of statements, read a grammar back to its language, and name the pair of models with different power. The patterns below describe those questions; they are not a forecast.

Pattern 1 — which of these is not context-free

Four languages, three of which have short grammars. The fourth needs two comparisons at once, or a copy.

Seen in GATE CSE 2019. Procedure: for each option, try to name the one comparison it makes. If you cannot, look for the copy or the crossing pair. The page builds grammars for three options and refutes the fourth by enumeration.

Pattern 2 — union against intersection of one pair

Two languages with a free block each, and a question about what happens when you combine them.

Seen in GATE CSE 2017 Set 1. Procedure: write out the intersection explicitly before judging it. Here it collapses to anbncn, which is the standard non-member. The union is safe by closure.

Pattern 3 — the statement cluster

Three languages and four claims about determinism, intersection and complements, with a request for the false ones.

Seen in GATE CSE 2022. Procedure: judge each claim on its own and expect more than one false. A statement that is half true is false; a statement about a complement is not settled by the language itself.

Pattern 4 — read the grammar back to a language

Give a three-rule grammar and four descriptions of what it generates.

Seen in GATE CSE 2009. Procedure: generate the shortest four or five strings, then test the tempting over-general option against one of them. "All palindromes" dies on ε; "begins and ends alike" dies on aaba.

Pattern 5 — the pair with different power

Four pairs of machine models; exactly one pair differs.

Seen in GATE CSE 2011. Procedure: remember the single asymmetry — deterministic pushdown machines are strictly weaker — and that every other familiar pair, finite and Turing, is equal in power.

The two wrong approaches that feel right

Drawing a machine until it looks convincing. A picture of a stack discipline does not prove that every symbol is covered. The check is whether each block's symbols are consumed by a rule of the machine.

Reaching for the pumping lemma on a yes. It cannot answer yes, and a page of algebra that ends in "therefore context-free" scores zero even when the conclusion is true.

GATE CSE 2019 Q1 (MCQ, 2 marks)

Which one of the following languages over Σ = {a, b} is not context-free?

(A) {wwR | w ∈ {a,b}*}   (B) {w anbn wR | w ∈ {a,b}*, n ≥ 0}   (C) {w an wR bn | w ∈ {a,b}*, n ≥ 0}   (D) {anbi | i ∈ {n, 3n, 5n}, n ≥ 0}

(C). Every legal split of (ab)2pa2p(ba)2pb2p leaves the language, so the pumping lemma refutes it: the machine would have to hold the mirror w wR and the count anbn at the same time.

(A) is three rules, S → aSa | bSb | ε. (B) is the same three with ε replaced by A → aAb | ε. (D) is a union of three languages, each with its own fixed ratio, and a union of context-free languages is context-free. The model checks (A), (B) and (D) against their definitions on every string up to length six, and refutes (C) exhaustively at p = 2 and p = 3.

GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q2 (MCQ, 2 marks)

Let L1 = {anbncm | m, n ≥ 0} and L2 = {ambncn | m, n ≥ 0}. Which of the following are context-free languages? I. L1 ∪ L2   II. L1 ∩ L2

(A) I only   (B) II only   (C) I and II   (D) Neither I nor II

(A). The union is context-free by closure, and the page exhibits a five-rule grammar that matches the union on every string up to length five. The intersection forces #a = #b and #b = #c, which is anbncn, and that is not context-free.

(C) is the tempting option, because both halves are context-free and the union looks like the easy case — it is. (D) over-corrects. The page computes both sides of the intersection up to length six and confirms they are the same set.

GATE CSE 2022 Q3 (MSQ, 2 marks)

Consider L1 = {ww | w ∈ {a,b}*}, L2 = {anbncm | m, n ≥ 0}, L3 = {ambncn | m, n ≥ 0}. Which of the following statements is/are false?

(A) L1 is not context-free but L2 and L3 are deterministic context-free.   (B) Neither L1 nor L2 is context-free.   (C) L2, L3 and L2 ∩ L3 all are context-free.   (D) Neither L1 nor its complement is context-free.

(B), (C) and (D). (A) is true: L1 is the copy language, which is not context-free, and both free-block languages are deterministic context-free — push on a and pop on b, or skip the a's and match b with c. (B) is false because L2 is context-free. (C) is false because the intersection is anbncn. (D) is false because the complement of ww is context-free: guess one mismatching position.

(A) reads like a mixed bag and is still true as a whole, which is the usual reason a cluster question is missed. Note that (D) is not settled by L1's own status: in this class a language and its complement can sit on opposite sides.

GATE CSE 2009 Q4 (MCQ, 1 mark)

The language generated by the grammar S → aSa | bSb | a | b over the alphabet {a, b} is the set of:

(A) all palindromes   (B) all odd length palindromes   (C) strings that begin and end with the same symbol   (D) all even length palindromes

(B). Both base rules give length 1 and each wrapping rule adds 2, so every generated string has odd length; and the wrapping keeps the mirror, so every generated string is a palindrome. The page compares the grammar's language with the odd-length palindromes on every string up to length six and finds no disagreement.

(A) fails on ε, which is a palindrome of even length and is not generated. (C) fails on aaba, which begins and ends with a and is not generated. (D) fails immediately, since a is generated.

GATE CSE 2011 Q5 (MCQ, 1 mark)

Which of the following pairs have DIFFERENT expressive power?

(A) DFA and NFA   (B) DPDA and NPDA   (C) deterministic single-tape TM and nondeterministic single-tape TM   (D) single-tape TM and multi-tape TM

(B). Deterministic pushdown machines accept the deterministic context-free languages, a proper subclass of the context-free languages accepted by nondeterministic ones — wwR is the witness, since a deterministic machine cannot find the middle. The other three pairs accept the same languages in each case.

(A) is the pair that tempts people to answer "different" by analogy with (B): subset construction gives an equivalent DFA for every NFA. (C) and (D) are equal in power as well, by simulation arguments that belong to the Turing-machine lesson.

Original practice Q6 (NAT)

A grammar has nonterminals {S, A, B}, terminals {a, b, c}, start symbol S, and rules S → AB, A → aA | ε, B → bBc | ε. How many strings of length at most 4 does it generate?

9. The language is {ambncn}. Up to length 4 that is ε, a, aa, aaa, aaaa, bc, abc, aabc and bbcc — nine strings.

The usual slip is to forget ε, which every nullable grammar produces, and then to stop at the strings built from the first rule alone. The model enumerates the language directly by leftmost derivation and gets the same nine.

Original practice Q7 (MSQ)

Let L = {anbn | n ≥ 0}. Which of the following are true?

(A) L is context-free.   (B) L is deterministic context-free.   (C) L is regular.   (D) The complement of L is context-free.

(A), (B) and (D). The grammar S → aSb | ε is three rules; the machine pushes on a and pops on b, and never needs to guess, so it is deterministic; and deterministic context-free languages are closed under complement, so the complement is context-free too.

(C) is false: a finite machine cannot count without bound, which is the previous lesson's pumping lemma. (D) is the statement most often marked false by mistake, because it is false for the copy language's neighbourhood rather than for this one.

Original practice Q8 (NAT)

Under the grammar E → E + E | E * E | i, how many distinct parse trees does the string i+i*i+i have?

5. With three operators the count is the Catalan number C3 = 5, and the page's inside algorithm returns exactly 5, agreeing with a brute-force enumeration of derivations.

The slip is to answer 3 or 4 by drawing a few trees and stopping. The count is 1, 1, 2, 5, 14 for one, two, three and four operators, and it is not the number of derivation orders, which is larger.

Original practice Q9 (MCQ)

Which statement about a grammar in Chomsky normal form is correct?

(A) Every derivation of a string of length n takes exactly 2n − 1 steps.   (B) Every derivation of a string of length n takes exactly 2n − 2 steps.   (C) A grammar in the form may contain the rule A → ε for any nonterminal A.   (D) A right side may be a nonterminal followed by a terminal.

(B). The tree has n leaves and n − 1 internal nodes, so the derivation applies n − 1 binary rules and n rules of the form A → a: 2n − 1 nodes in all, which is 2n − 2 steps from the start symbol.

(A) counts nodes rather than steps. (C) is wrong except for the start symbol, and only when it appears in no right side. (D) is excluded: a mixed right side must be split with a fresh nonterminal first.

Original practice Q10 (MCQ)

Which of the following is not closed under complement?

(A) the regular languages   (B) the deterministic context-free languages   (C) the context-free languages   (D) the recursive languages

(C). Context-free languages are not closed under complement. The copy language ww is outside the class and its complement is inside, which is enough to break closure.

(B) is the trap: deterministic context-free languages are closed under complement, and that is the one asymmetry in this corner of the subject. (A) and (D) are closed under complement by flipping accepting states and swapping accept with reject respectively.

The one-screen revision sheet

Definitions, the decision procedure, the two lemmas side by side, and the traps. Nothing here is new; it is the lesson compressed to what you would want in the last ten minutes before a paper.

Definitions in one line each

CFG (V, Σ, P, S) with every rule A → α, A a single nonterminal.

L(G) the sentences derivable from S.

Ambiguous some string has two distinct trees.

Inherently ambiguous no grammar for the language is unambiguous.

CNF only A → BC and A → a, with S → ε the sole exception.

PDA (Q, Σ, Γ, δ, q0, Z0, F); a move reads, pops and pushes.

The decision procedure

  1. Rewrite the language as blocks and conditions. one condition means one stack
  2. Try to construct a grammar or a machine. a construction settles a yes
  3. If it fails, pump: one block per dependent count. every legal split must die
  4. Check determinism, ε and the quantifiers. the four standard slips

The two lemmas, side by side

Regular. s = xyz, |xy| ≤ p, |y| ≥ 1, pump y alone. Proof walks a chain of states, so one block repeats.

Context-free. s = uvwxy, |vwx| ≤ p, |vx| ≥ 1, pump v and x together. Proof walks a binary tree, so a nonterminal repeats and carries two sibling subtrees.

Both are necessary and not sufficient, so both only refute.

Closed, and not

Closed: union, concatenation, star, reversal, homomorphism, inverse homomorphism, intersection with a regular language.

Not closed: intersection, complement.

Deterministic subclass: closed under complement, not closed under intersection.

The traps, in one list

Derivations are not trees. Precedence changes trees, not the language. A stack matches a mirror, not a copy. Determinism costs power here. Intersection and complement are not closed. ε is the normal-form exception. The lemma cannot prove a yes.

Every one of these is true in the regular case and false here, which is why they are the standard distractors.

The five sentences worth memorising

  1. A stack hands back the reverse of what it stored, so it can match a mirror and a count, and cannot match a copy.
  2. One independent comparison is context-free; two that cross are not; two that nest usually are.
  3. Ambiguity is counted in trees, not in derivation orders, and it belongs to the grammar until a theorem says otherwise.
  4. The class is closed under union and not under intersection, and intersecting with a regular language is the exception.
  5. The pumping lemma refutes and never certifies; a grammar or a machine is the only proof of yes.
