Topic and objective

TOPIC: The uniform distribution, in both shapes: the discrete uniform on a list of equally likely values, and the continuous uniform on an interval [a, b]. The density as the reciprocal of the width, the CDF as a ramp, the interval probability as a length ratio, the affine map that turns the standard uniform into any other, the moments that give the endpoints back, rounding a uniform into a discrete one, order statistics and the equal-spacing argument, the inverse transform that manufactures every other distribution, and the place where the word "uniform" quietly names the wrong object.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide from the wording whether the object is discrete or continuous, and answer a point question accordingly — zero on one side, 1/n on the other. Convert any interval question into a length ratio without integrating. Move between [0,1] and [a,b] in both directions, and recover a and b from two moments. Use the maximum and minimum factorisations and the k/(n+1) rule instead of integrating. Read a CDF backwards to get the exponential or a power density. And refuse to accept "chosen at random" until the object that is uniform has been named.

I am reading this microtopic as one decision and three habits: the decision is discrete against continuous, and the habits are length ratio, affine map, and order statistics. Every scene and every question on the page is one of those applied to new words.

The sentence to keep

A uniform distribution says that equal size means equal probability — equal counts for the discrete family, equal lengths or areas for the continuous one. Every question on this topic is a length, a count or a moment, and every trap on this topic comes from measuring the wrong object.

Level 1 — Intuition, and Level 2 — the formal definitions

Educational interpretation A bus comes every ten minutes and you arrive without looking at a timetable. Your wait is uniform on 0 to 10: no minute is more likely than another, because nothing in the situation favours one. That is the whole idea — the interval has no preferred place — and the arithmetic that follows is only bookkeeping on a flat surface.

Discrete uniform. Let n be a positive integer and let X take the values 1, 2, …, n. If P(X = k) = 1/n for every one of those values, X is uniformly distributed on 1 to n. The n masses are equal and they sum to 1, which is the only requirement.

Continuous uniform. Let a < b be real numbers. If the density of X is f(x) = 1/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b and 0 outside, X is uniform on the interval [a, b], written X ~ U(a, b). Probability is the area under the density, so the area over [a, b] is the height times the width, which is 1.

Read the density aloud as "one divided by the width". The width alone sets the height; where the interval sits does not change the height at all, which is why the mean moves with a but the variance never does.

Level 3 — Visual understanding: a flat surface, and a ramp above it

The density is a rectangle of height 1/(b − a) sitting on the interval. A probability is a slice of that rectangle, so its area is (slice width) × (same height) — and dividing by the total area cancels the height entirely. That cancellation is why every continuous uniform probability is a length ratio.

The CDF is the running area, which grows linearly, so it is a straight ramp from 0 to 1. Where the ramp is steep the density is high; where the ramp is flat the density is zero. On a uniform the ramp has one slope everywhere inside the interval and none outside.

Level 4 — Worked examples, one line each

A die. X ~ uniform on 1..6. Mean (n+1)/2 = 3.5, variance (n²−1)/12 = 35/12 = 2.916667. The page sums all six masses and all six squared deviations and gets the same two numbers.

X ~ U(2,8). Density 1/6. P(3 ≤ X ≤ 5.5) = 2.5/6 = 0.416667. Mean 5, variance 36/12 = 3.

Backwards. A uniform has mean 10 and variance 4/3. Then a + b = 20 and b − a = √16 = 4, so b = 12 and a = 8.

Three points on [0,1]. E[min] = 1/4, E[max] = 3/4, expected range 1/2. One cut instead: the longer piece expects 3/4, and the piece containing 0.4 expects 0.74.

Level 5 — Boundaries: what each family requires

The discrete family needs a finite list. "Uniform on 1 to n" needs n decided in advance. Uniform on the subsets of a set, on a permutation, on a bit string — each is a discrete uniform, but on a different list, and the probabilities are one over the size of that list.

The continuous family needs a bounded interval of positive width. If b = a the width is zero and no density exists. There is no continuous uniform on the whole real line, and "uniform on (0, ∞)" is not a distribution at all.

Independence is not automatic. Two coordinates can each be uniform and the pair not uniform on the region — that is exactly the disk counterexample of scene 9, and it is the reason the joint distribution has to be stated.

Level 6 — Edge cases and a counterexample

Endpoints. For a continuous uniform it makes no difference whether the interval is written open or closed: P(X = a) = 0, so [a,b] and (a,b) carry the same distribution. For the discrete uniform every endpoint matters.

n = 1. A discrete uniform on one value is a point mass with variance 0. The formulas (n+1)/2 and (n²−1)/12 still return 1 and 0.

Degenerate width. b → a drives the variance to 0 and the density to infinity; the limit is a point mass, not a uniform.

Counterexample to "uniform inputs give a uniform output". A uniform radius and a uniform angle do not give a uniform point on the disk: the inner half-radius circle holds a quarter of the area but half the points.

Level 7 — GATE application

The exam almost never asks for the density. It asks you to find a uniform inside a sentence — a point chosen on an interval, a value drawn at random from a range, a permutation sampled uniformly — and then to produce one of four things: a length ratio, a count over equally likely objects, a moment, or the endpoints recovered from two moments.

The 2019 item hides a uniform inside a quadratic: the polynomial has real roots when a discriminant is non-negative, and the discriminant condition is an inequality in Y, so the answer is the length of the Y-interval that satisfies it. The 2020 item is a discrete uniform over bit strings, where the answer comes from one bit rather than from counting all 2n strings.

Example 1 — the definition, used directly

X ~ U(2, 8). Find the density, P(4 ≤ X ≤ 7), and the mean and variance.

Density: 1/(8 − 2) = 1/6 = 0.166667. Probability: (7 − 4)/6 = 3/6 = 0.5, which is also the rise of the ramp from F(4) = 2/6 to F(7) = 5/6. Mean: (2+8)/2 = 5. Variance: 36/12 = 3.

The trap that is not here. Nothing needed integrating. Every one of the four answers is a length, a midpoint or a width, because the density is flat and its height cancels.

Example 2 — the interval is a length ratio, twice over

A packet's arrival time is uniform on 0 to 60 seconds. Find P(it arrives in the first 15 seconds) and P(it arrives between 20 and 50 seconds).

Answers: 15/60 = 0.25 and 30/60 = 0.5. Both are widths divided by the total width, and the two add with the remaining 15 seconds to 1.

Why the arithmetic is safe: the density is constant, so doubling a stretch doubles its probability. Any question of the form "what is the chance it falls in this stretch" is answered by measuring the stretch.

Example 3 — the reverse question: two moments give the endpoints (GATE CSE 2019, in disguise)

Y is uniform on (1, 6). Find the probability that 3x² + 6xY + 3Y + 6 has only real roots.

Step 1 — the condition. A quadratic Ax² + Bx + C has real roots exactly when B² − 4AC ≥ 0. Here A = 3, B = 6Y, C = 3Y + 6, so the condition is 36Y² − 12(3Y + 6) ≥ 0, which simplifies to Y² − Y − 2 ≥ 0, that is (Y − 2)(Y + 1) ≥ 0.

Step 2 — the interval. That inequality holds for Y ≥ 2 or Y ≤ −1. On the support (1, 6) only Y ≥ 2 survives, so the favourable stretch is [2, 6], of width 4.

Step 3 — the ratio. 4/5 = 0.8. The model integrates the indicator of the discriminant condition over (1, 6) and gets 0.800000, which is the same number reached by measuring the interval. Why this is a uniform question and not a calculus question: the hard part is the inequality, and once it is solved the probability is a length.

Example 4 — a discrete uniform over bit strings (GATE CSE 2020)

For n > 2, let a ∈ {0,1}n be a non-zero vector. x is chosen uniformly at random from {0,1}n. Find the probability that the sum of aixi over i is odd.

Answer: 1/2. Let j be the largest index with aj = 1, which exists because a is non-zero. The parity of the sum is decided by the bits other than xj together with xj itself, and flipping xj flips the parity while leaving every other bit alone. So the 2n strings pair up into 2n−1 odd and 2n−1 even.

Why it is a uniform question: the model is the discrete uniform on the 2n strings, and the answer is a count of half of them. The page's model reports 0.5 without enumerating, and the pairing argument is the proof that the count is exact for every n.

Example 5 — the stick, both versions

A point is chosen uniformly on (0,1) and the interval is cut there. Two questions follow, and they have different answers.

The longer piece. Its length is max(X, 1−X), whose mean is the integral of that function, 3/4 = 0.75. Equivalently, the shorter piece expects 1/4 by symmetry, so the longer expects 3/4.

The piece containing 0.4. If the cut lands above 0.4 the piece is [0, X] of length X; if below, it is [X, 1] of length 1−X. Averaging the two cases gives 1/2 + p − p², which at p = 0.4 is 0.74.

Why both are computed here: a GATE CSE 2025 Set 2 item divides the unit interval at a uniform point, and the public reproductions of it differ — one asks for the piece containing 0.4, which gives 0.74, while a widely circulated key of 0.75 is the expected length of the longer piece. Both quantities are computed on this page, so either version can be answered and the difference is visible.

Example 6 — the k-th order statistic, without integrating

Four independent points are dropped uniformly on [0,1] and sorted. Where does the second largest sit on average?

Answer: 3/5 = 0.6. The k-th smallest of n uniforms has mean k/(n+1). The second largest of four is the third smallest, so k = 3 and n = 4, giving 3/5.

The check that costs nothing: the four expected positions are 1/5, 2/5, 3/5 and 4/5, which are equally spaced and symmetric about 1/2. That symmetry is forced — the distribution of the draws is symmetric about the midpoint, so the expected positions must be too. If a computed set of order-statistic means is not symmetric about 1/2, the arithmetic is wrong.

Uniform inputs, non-uniform output

A radius uniform on [0,R] and an angle uniform on [0,2π) give a point that is not uniform on the disk. The inner half-radius circle holds 1/4 of the area but 1/2 of the points.

Uniformity is a property of a distribution on a set, not of the ingredients. The correct radial density is 2r/R², proportional to the circumference at that radius, and with it the proportion inside R/2 becomes exactly 1/4 — which the page computes.

The piece containing a point is not the longer piece

At p = 0.4 the expected length of the piece containing p is 0.74, while the expected length of the longer piece is 0.75. The two differ by one hundredth — small enough to be invisible in a quick estimate and large enough to lose a mark.

The general formula 1/2 + p − p² is largest at p = 1/2, where it equals 3/4 and coincides with the longer piece. That coincidence is why the two get confused.

Open or closed, it does not matter — on one side only

For a continuous uniform, P(X = a) = 0, so [a,b], (a,b), [a,b) and (a,b] all give the same probabilities. Writing the interval open changes nothing.

For the discrete uniform it changes everything: uniform on 1..6 has P(X = 1) = 1/6, and dropping the endpoint 1 leaves a different distribution with mean 4 and variance 2. The open/closed question is a discrete question.

Degenerate width, and the limit that is not a uniform

If b = a the interval has zero width and there is no density: 1/(b−a) is undefined. As b → a the density grows without bound while the area stays 1, and the limit is a point mass at a, not a uniform.

The discrete analogue is n = 1: one value with probability 1, mean 1, variance 0. Both formulas (n+1)/2 and (n²−1)/12 still return the right numbers there, so no special case is needed.

Independence has to be stated

"Two points chosen uniformly on [0,1]" means independent unless the question says otherwise. If they were dependent — say the second is always above the first — the order statistics would be the two given points and every maximum/minimum formula would change.

The factorisation P(max ≤ x) = xn is exactly where independence enters: it multiplies n copies of the same probability. Without independence the product is not available and the formula fails.

A uniform bit string is not a uniform integer

Choosing uniformly from the 2n strings of length n gives each string probability 2−n. But the integer the string denotes is not uniform on 0..2n−1 in any useful sense: the leading bit alone decides which half of the range the value falls in.

So "pick a random n-bit number" is ambiguous between two different distributions, and only one of them is uniform on the integers. Name the object before computing.

"On a continuous uniform, P(X = 4) is small but not zero"

Wrong. It is exactly 0. A point is an interval of length zero, and probability is proportional to length. The page integrates the density over [4,4] and gets 0 to nine decimal places.

What is true: the density at 4 is 1/6, which is not zero. The density is a rate, not a probability — confusing the two is the commonest error in the whole topic.

"The variance of U(a,b) is (b−a)/12"

Wrong. It is (b−a)²/12. The square is the point: the variance of the standard uniform is 1/12, and scaling by the width multiplies the variance by the width squared.

The check: the standard deviation is (b−a)/√12, which is the width over about 3.46. For U(2,8) that is 1.732, and squaring it returns 3.

"Uniform means the values are evenly spread in a sample"

Wrong. Uniform is a statement about the distribution, not about any particular sample. Ten draws from U(0,1) will not be evenly spaced; on average the largest is 10/11, not 1.

What is true: the order statistics spread evenly in expectation, at k/(n+1). That is an average over many samples, and it is the content of scene 6.

"Rounding a uniform always gives a fair discrete distribution"

Only when the input is uniform. floor(6U) + 1 is fair; floor(6U²) + 1 puts 0.408250 of the mass on face 1. The rounding rule partitions the interval, and the probabilities are the lengths of the pieces — after the input has been transformed.

The lesson: a transformation can destroy uniformity even though every step is deterministic arithmetic. Check the input, not the rule.

"The mean of a uniform is its most likely value"

Careful. On a continuous uniform every value in the interval is equally likely, so there is no most likely value at all. On a discrete uniform with n odd the mean (n+1)/2 is a value; with n even it is not — a die's mean of 3.5 is not a face.

What is true: the mean is the balance point and the midpoint of the support. That is a different statement from "the most likely", and the two coincide only in the symmetric discrete case with odd n.

"Two moments always determine a uniform"

Careful. Two moments determine the two endpoints given that the distribution is uniform. Mean 5 and variance 3 are also consistent with a normal, a triangular or a beta distribution, and those have different answers to every probability question.

The discipline: the moments recover the parameters of a model you have already justified. They never justify the model. A question that hands you two moments and asks for endpoints is telling you the family; one that hands you two moments and asks for a probability is not.

The procedure, in the order you should run it

1. Discrete or continuous? A die, a card, a permutation, a bit string, a subset — discrete. A point on a line, a moment in time, a length — continuous. This decides whether P(X = c) is zero. 2. What are the two endpoints or the two integers? Given, or recovered from two moments. 3. Turn the question into a length or a count. Interval probability = length / total length; a discrete question is a count of equally likely objects. 4. Does the question mention a maximum, a minimum or a gap? Then use k/(n+1) and the range formula rather than integrating.

If the options contain a and b, the expected answer is an expression in them. If the options are numbers, compute the ratio exactly and round only at the end. If the question gives two moments and asks for endpoints, expect the square root of twelve times the variance.

The four answer shapes in past papers

A length ratio — (y − x)/(b − a), the shape of the 2019 item once its inequality is solved.

A count over equally likely objects — half the 2n strings, one sixth of the faces, one of the n! permutations. This is the 2020 shape.

A moment — (a+b)/2, (b−a)²/12, k/(n+1), or two of them solved backwards.

An expected length — 3/4 for the longer piece, 1/2 + p − p² for the piece containing p, (n−1)/(n+1) for the range.

Four traps, each with the sentence that avoids it

Trap 1 — reading a density as a probability. The height 1/6 is not the probability of anything; the probability is the area. Trap 2 — dropping the square. Variance is (b−a)²/12 and the width is √(12 × variance). Trap 3 — measuring the wrong piece. "The piece containing p" is 1/2 + p − p²; "the longer piece" is 3/4. Trap 4 — assuming "at random" means uniform on whatever you pictured. Name the object: a point, an edge, a permutation, a bit string.

Each trap is a different way of losing the same point: the model was chosen from the shape of the words rather than from the object the words describe.

NATGATE CSE 2019

Suppose Y is distributed uniformly in the open interval (1, 6). The probability that the polynomial 3x2 + 6xY + 3Y + 6 has only real roots is ______ (rounded off to one decimal place).

Answer: 0.8

Real roots need the discriminant non-negative: (6Y)² − 4·3·(3Y + 6) ≥ 0, that is 36Y² − 36Y − 72 ≥ 0, that is Y² − Y − 2 ≥ 0, that is (Y − 2)(Y + 1) ≥ 0. On (1,6) that is Y ≥ 2, a stretch of width 4 out of 5. So the probability is 4/5 = 0.8.

The page integrates the indicator of the discriminant condition over (1,6) and reports 0.800000, which is the same number reached by measuring the interval.

The trap: dividing 4 by 6 instead of 5, which uses the upper endpoint as the denominator and forgets that the support starts at 1. The denominator is always the width of the support, b − a.

NATGATE CSE 2020

For n > 2, let a ∈ {0,1}n be a non-zero vector. Suppose that x is chosen uniformly at random from {0,1}n. Then the probability that the sum of aixi over i = 1 to n is an odd number is ______.

Answer: 1/2 = 0.5

Take the largest index j with aj = 1, which exists because a is non-zero. Flipping xj changes the parity of the sum and changes nothing else, so the 2n strings pair up: each string with an even sum is matched to one with an odd sum. Exactly half are odd, so the probability is 1/2 for every n.

Why the answer does not depend on a or on n: the argument uses one coordinate and the uniformity of that coordinate. The other bits only shift the parity, they do not bias it.

The trap: trying to count the strings directly. The count is 2n−1 but proving it needs the pairing; enumerating small cases suggests the answer and does not establish it.

MCQOriginal practice question

X is uniform on [0, 1]. What is E[X2]?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/4
  4. 1/12

Answer: B — 1/3

E[X²] is the integral of x² over [0,1], which is 1/3. The page computes it numerically and gets 0.333333. Then Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² = 1/3 − 1/4 = 1/12, which is the standard uniform variance, so the two answers are consistent.

Why A fails: 1/2 is E[X], the first moment, not the second. Why C fails: 1/4 is the square of E[X]. Why D fails: 1/12 is the variance, which is E[X²] minus 1/4.

MCQOriginal practice question

The unit interval is cut at a point chosen uniformly. What is the expected length of the piece that contains the point 0.4?

  1. 0.70
  2. 0.74
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.80

Answer: B — 0.74

If the cut lands above 0.4 the piece containing 0.4 runs from 0 to the cut, of length X; if below, it runs from the cut to 1, of length 1 − X. So the expected length is the integral of X over [0.4, 1] plus the integral of 1 − X over [0, 0.4], which is 0.42 + 0.32 = 0.74. In general it is 1/2 + p − p².

Why C fails: 0.75 is the expected length of the longer piece, a different question. Why A fails: 0.70 is 1 − 0.3, the expected length of the piece containing 0.3, not 0.4. Why D fails: 0.80 is the expected length of the piece containing 0.5 minus the correction, and comes from misapplying the quadratic.

MSQOriginal practice question

X is uniform on [a, b] with b > a. Which of the following are true?

  1. P(X = a) = 0
  2. E[X] = (a + b)/2
  3. Var(X) = (b − a)/12
  4. P(a ≤ X ≤ (a+b)/2) = 1/2

Answer: A and B and D

A is true: a point has zero width, so it carries no probability. B is true: the mean is the midpoint by symmetry. D is true: the lower half of the interval has half the width, so it holds half the probability.

Why C fails: the variance is (b − a)²/12. For U(2,8) that is 3; the expression (b−a)/12 would give 0.5, which is also the square root of the variance divided by the width — a coincidence that makes the wrong formula look plausible.

NATOriginal practice question

A continuous uniform distribution has mean 3 and variance 0.75. What is its upper endpoint b?

Answer: 4.5

Mean 3 gives a + b = 6. Variance 0.75 gives (b − a)² = 12 × 0.75 = 9, so b − a = 3. Adding: 2b = 9, so b = 4.5 and a = 1.5. The page puts both moments back into the recovered interval and gets 3 and 0.75.

The trap: using (b−a)/12 = 0.75, which gives a width of 9 and then a = −1.5, b = 7.5. That interval has variance 81/12 = 6.75, not 0.75 — substituting back catches it immediately.

MCQOriginal practice question

Four independent points are chosen uniformly on [0, 1] and sorted. What is the expected value of the second largest?

  1. 0.4
  2. 0.6
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.8

Answer: B — 0.6

With four points the second largest is the third smallest, so k = 3 and n = 4, and the mean is k/(n+1) = 3/5 = 0.6.

Why A fails: 0.4 is the second smallest, that is k = 2. Why C fails: 0.75 is the largest, k = n. Why D fails: 0.8 is the largest for n = 4, that is 4/5, which is the maximum and not the second largest.

The check: the four expected positions are 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8. They are equally spaced and symmetric about 0.5, which they must be because the draws are symmetric about 0.5. Any computed set that is not symmetric is wrong.

NATOriginal practice question

Three independent points are chosen uniformly on [0, 1]. What is the expected length of the interval between the smallest and the largest? Give the answer to two decimal places.

Answer: 0.50

The expected maximum is n/(n+1) = 3/4 and the expected minimum is 1/(n+1) = 1/4, so the expected range is 3/4 − 1/4 = 1/2 = 0.50. The general formula is (n−1)/(n+1), which is 2/4 here.

The equal-spacing picture gives the same answer without an integral: three points make four gaps of expected length 1/4 each, and the range is the middle two, so 1/2.

The trap: assuming the range grows to 1 as n grows. It does — but slowly: (n−1)/(n+1) is 0.5 at n = 3, 0.818 at n = 10 and 0.980 at n = 100.

MCQOriginal practice question

U is uniform on [0,1). Which rule gives a fair die on the faces 1 to 6?

  1. floor(6U)
  2. floor(6U) + 1
  3. round(6U)
  4. floor(6U2) + 1

Answer: B — floor(6U) + 1

The six intervals [0,1/6), [1/6,2/6), …, [5/6,1) each have length 1/6, so each face gets probability 1/6. Over 60000 grid draws the six buckets hold exactly 10000 each, and the page reports a largest deviation of 0.

Why A fails: it produces 0 to 5, not 1 to 6. Why C fails: rounding makes the two end faces half as likely, because only half an interval maps to each. Why D fails: squaring first makes the input non-uniform, and face 1 then takes 24495 of 60000 draws — a probability of 0.408250.

NATOriginal practice question

X is uniform on [0,1]. The point Y = X2 is formed. What is the probability that Y is at most 0.25?

Answer: 0.5

Y ≤ 0.25 exactly when X ≤ 0.5, since X is non-negative and squaring is increasing there. So the probability is the length 0.5. The page measures it on 60000 grid points and compares it with the closed form P(Y ≤ y) = √y, which at y = 0.25 gives 0.5.

The trap: answering 0.25, which is y itself. That would be right for P(Y ≤ 0.5) = 0.25 only if the CDF of Y were the identity, and it is not: the CDF of Y is √y, so the density is 1/(2√y), which falls rather than staying flat.

The one-screen revision sheet

Everything here is either a definition or a one-line consequence of one. If you can rebuild the right-hand column from the left, this topic is finished.

The two definitions

Discrete: P(X = k) = 1/n for k = 1..n. Masses are equal and sum to 1.

Continuous: f(x) = 1/(b−a) on [a,b], 0 outside. Area is probability.

P(X = c) = 0 continuous · P(X = c) = 1/n discrete.

Density is a rate, never a probability: the height 1/6 is not the chance of anything.

The CDF and the length ratio

F(x) = (x − a)/(b − a) on [a,b]; 0 below, 1 above.

P(x ≤ X ≤ y) = (y − x)/(b − a) — a length over the total length.

U(2,8): P(3 ≤ X ≤ 5.5) = 2.5/6 = 0.416667.

Endpoints do not matter on the continuous side; they decide everything on the discrete side.

The two sets of moments

Discrete: mean (n+1)/2 · variance (n²−1)/12. Die: 3.5 and 2.916667.

Continuous: mean (a+b)/2 · variance (b−a)²/12 · sd (b−a)/√12.

U(2,8): mean 5, variance 3, sd 1.732051.

The variance never depends on where the interval sits, only on its width.

The affine map, both directions

X = a + (b − a)U takes U(0,1) to U(a,b).

(X − a)/(b − a) takes it back. Answer on [0,1], then scale.

Reverse from two moments: a + b = 2 × mean, b − a = √(12 × variance).

Mean 10, variance 4/3 → a = 8, b = 12. Mean 3, variance 0.75 → a = 1.5, b = 4.5.

Order statistics and the gaps

P(max ≤ x) = x^n · P(min > x) = (1−x)^n

E[X_(k)] = k/(n+1) · E[range] = (n−1)/(n+1)

n = 3: min 0.25, max 0.75, range 0.5, gaps 1/4 each. n = 4: 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8.

The "all of them" wording is what factorises, and independence is what lets the factors multiply.

Rounding, the stick, the inverse

floor(nU) + 1 is uniform on 1..n — only if U is uniform.

Longer piece 3/4 · piece containing p: 1/2 + p − p² (0.74 at p = 0.4) · both pieces ≥ 1/3 has probability 1/3.

x = F⁻¹(u): exponential −ln(1−u)/λ; X = √U has CDF x² and density 2x; X = U² has CDF √x and a falling density.

The CDF of any continuous variable is uniform on [0,1] — that is why the inverse works.

The four-step procedure

  1. Discrete or continuous? Decides mass against density, and whether P(X = c) is 0.
  2. Which two endpoints or two integers? Given, or from 2 × mean and √(12 × variance).
  3. Length or count. Interval probability is width over total width; a discrete question is a count.
  4. Maximum, minimum or gap? Use k/(n+1) and the range formula; do not integrate.
