Topic and objective

TOPIC: Amortised analysis — the aggregate, accounting (banker’s) and potential (physicist’s) methods, applied to a doubling table, a binary counter and a stack with multipop.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a structure with one operation that occasionally costs a lot, compute the amortised cost per operation by all three methods, check the answer against an exhaustive count, and say precisely what the claim does and does not assert — then recognise when the claim fails because the operation set changed.

I read the request as the technique itself, not a re-run of the best/average/worst lesson: one operation set, three proofs of the same bound, every number counted. Formal amortised lower bounds (Tarjan’s potential-function theory) are named once and not developed.

Prerequisites

  • Asymptotic notation: O, Θ and what a constant factor hides — asymptotic complexity.
  • Best, average and worst case as three separate questions — best/average/worst and amortised.
  • Geometric series: 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2k = 2k+1 − 1.
  • Logarithms base 2, and the fact that doubling a capacity k times reaches 2k.
  • Counting arguments of the kind used for exact comparison counts — exact counts.

Five ideas this page establishes

  1. Amortised cost is a bound on a sequence: n operations cost at most n times the amortised cost, for every sequence.
  2. Doubling makes the copies cost 2n − 1 in total, so the amortised cost of an insertion is at most 2.
  3. Accounting saves credit on cheap operations; the credit must never go negative.
  4. Potential turns that saving into a function: amortised = actual + change in potential, and the changes telescope.
  5. The claim is about an operation set. Add decrement, or halve a table at half full, and O(1) becomes Θ(log n) or Θ(n).

Intuition first: the bill, not the receipt

Educational interpretation A restaurant bills the table, not the diner. One diner orders the expensive dish, and his own receipt is enormous — but split across eight people at the table, the meal is ordinary. Amortised analysis is the split: the total is divided by the number of operations, and the answer is quoted per operation, even though no single operation actually cost that.

The analogy stops at one point, and the point matters: the split is not an average over diners chosen at random. It is a guarantee about the worst possible order the table could place, which is why it is stronger than an average and weaker than a worst case.

Formal definition

Suppose a sequence of n operations is performed on a data structure. Let ci be the actual cost of the i-th operation, and let T(n) = c1 + c2 + … + cn be the total.

amortised cost per operation = T(n) / n The number is a bound on the average over the sequence, not the cost of any one step. When we say “amortised O(1)” we mean T(n) = O(n): the total grows at most linearly in the number of operations.

Every symbol: ci is one operation’s cost in abstract units — one write, one copy, one bit flip; n is the number of operations, not the number of items; T(n) is their sum. No probability appears anywhere, which is the difference from average-case analysis.

Why one operation is not enough

Mathematical derivation In the doubling table the 1,025th insertion copies 1,024 items and costs 1,025 units, while the 1,026th costs 1. A per-operation bound must hold for the worst of them, so it must be Θ(n) — useless. The sequence bound is Θ(1) per operation. Both statements are true; they answer different questions.

The cost model of the doubling table

This is the standard TABLE-INSERT: a block of slots, a count of items in it, and a rule that when the block is full a new block of twice the capacity is allocated and every item copied across.

  • Writing one new item costs 1 unit.
  • Copying one existing item costs 1 unit, charged to the insertion that triggered the copy.
  • Capacity starts at 0 and doubles: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … The table is therefore always at least half full.
  • An insertion is expensive exactly when it arrives at a full table, which happens at item counts 2, 3, 5, 9, 17 … — one more than a power of two.

The three methods, in one line each

Aggregate: add the whole bill, divide by n.

Accounting: overcharge each cheap operation and bank the change; the bank must never go negative.

Potential: write the bank as a function of the data structure, and use actual + change in potential.

The two formal statements

accounting:   crediti = crediti−1 + chargei − ci  ≥  0   for every i The credit after the i-th operation. If it never falls below zero, then the sum of the charges, which is n times the amortised cost, is at least the total actual cost.
potential:   amortisedi = ci + Φ(Di) − Φ(Di−1),   Φ(D0) = 0,   Φ(Di) ≥ 0 Di is the data structure after i operations and Φ is any non-negative function of it that is zero at the start. Summing over i cancels every middle term, leaving total cost + Φ(Dn) − 0 — and since Φ is never negative, that is at least the total cost.

The non-negativity of Φ is not decoration. It is the only reason the telescoped sum bounds the total from above; a potential that can go negative gives a number that may be smaller than the truth.

Assumptions to keep in view

  • All three methods bound the same sequence of operations; none of them says anything about a step.
  • The cost model is declared, not discovered: one unit per write and one per copy. Change the model and the constants change, not the order.
  • Amortised analysis presumes the operation set is fixed. Adding an operation can destroy the bound, which is what the ninth scene shows.

Example 1 — the costs of the first ten insertions

Grow the table from empty, charging one unit per write and one per copy.

  • Capacity 0, items 0. Insert: capacity becomes 1, no copies. cost 1
  • Full at 1 of 1. Insert: capacity 2, one copy. cost 2
  • Full at 2 of 2. Insert: capacity 4, two copies. cost 3
  • Room at 3 of 4. cost 1
  • Full at 4 of 4. Insert: capacity 8, four copies. cost 5
  • Room at 5, 6, 7 of 8. cost 1 each
  • Full at 8 of 8. Insert: capacity 16, eight copies. cost 9

So the first nine costs are 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9 and the capacities 1, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8, 8, 8, 16. The tenth insertion finds room at 9 of 16 and costs 1.

Example 2 — the aggregate count for 1,024 insertions

The copy costs are exactly the item counts at each expansion: 1, 2, 4, …, 512.

1 + 2 + 4 + … + 512 = 1,023   copies A geometric series with ratio 2 sums to one less than its next term: 1,024 − 1. The last expansion happens at the 513th insertion, when 512 items are copied.
T(1,024) = 1,024 writes + 1,023 copies = 2,047 = 2n − 1 Every insertion pays one unit for its own item; the copies are the extra. Dividing by n gives 1.999 units per insertion, and the bound 2 is never reached.

The same division at other lengths: n = 8 gives 1.875, n = 16 gives 1.9375, n = 64 gives 1.9844, n = 4,096 gives 1.9998. Each is below 2, and the sequence rises towards 2 without passing it.

Example 3 — the potential on the ninth insertion

Take Φ = 2 × items − capacity. Before the ninth insertion the table holds 8 items in 8 slots, so Φ = 16 − 8 = 8.

  • The insertion copies 8 items and writes 1: actual cost 9.
  • The table doubles to 16 slots and holds 9 items: Φ = 18 − 16 = 2.
  • The change in potential is 2 − 8 = −6.
  • Amortised cost = 9 + (−6) = 3. the credit spent, written as a subtraction

The tenth insertion costs 1 and raises Φ from 2 to 4, so its amortised cost is 3 again. In fact every insertion after the first has amortised cost exactly 3, which the model checks over all 4,096 of them; the first has amortised cost 2.

Example 4 — the binary counter

Sixteen increments from zero flip this many bits each:

1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 4, 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 5

The total is 31. Two ways to see it: the final bit flips on all 16 increments, and the carries flip 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 15 more. Or use the closed form 2n − popcount(n) = 32 − 1 = 31.

Mathematical derivation With the number of ones as the potential, an increment flipping t + 1 bits changes it by 1 − t, so the amortised cost is (t + 1) + (1 − t) = 2 — the same for every increment, however many bits it flips.

Example 5 — the stack with multipop

Seven pushes then one multipop of seven: the costs are 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 7, and the total is 14 over 8 operations.

The single operation costs 7 and the average costs 1.75. The bound is 2n = 16, and the enumeration of all 78,125 sequences of seven operations found a maximum total of 10 against a bound of 14.

Mathematical derivation The items removed can never exceed the items pushed, and each push is one of the n operations, so the total is at most 2n. The bound is loose here and the order is still right, which is all an exam asks.

Example 6 — the same potential, a different operation set

Keep the number of ones as the potential and add decrement. Alternate between 65,535 (sixteen ones) and 65,536 (one one):

  • 65,535 + 1: sixteen ones become zeros and a new bit is set. 17 flips; potential falls from 16 to 1
  • 65,536 − 1: the single one is cleared and sixteen zeros become ones. 17 flips; potential rises from 1 to 16
  • Repeat. Every operation flips 17 bits, so the average over any number of operations is 17. the counter never leaves the boundary

The potential is bounded between 1 and 16, so it cannot absorb 17 flips: the amortised bound it gives is 1 for the increment and 2k = 32 for the decrement. Increment and decrement together are Θ(log n) amortised, not O(1) — a fact the counter’s reputation as the “O(1) amortised” example hides.

Edge case 1 — the worst single operation never goes away

The 1,025th insertion into a table of 1,024 items copies 1,024 items and costs 1,025 units. The amortised cost is 2. The ratio is 512.5.

This is not a contradiction: 2 is a bound on the average over the sequence, and 1,025 is the cost of one term. A question that asks for the worst-case cost of one insertion wants Θ(n); a question that asks for the amortised cost wants Θ(1).

Edge case 2 — the first insertion

With capacity 0 there is nothing to copy, so the first insertion costs 1, and charging it 3 would leave 2 units of credit that no copy ever needs. The model charges 2 for the first and 3 for the rest, which is why the first amortised cost is 2 rather than 3. Textbooks differ on whether to start the capacity at 1; the bound is the same.

Edge case 3 — a multipop on an empty stack

A multipop of a thousand items on an empty stack removes nothing and costs nothing. It is one operation with cost 0, so it lowers the average. The bound 2n does not notice, and neither does the exam — but a “cost at least 1 per operation” assumption is false.

Edge case 4 — halving at half full destroys the bound

Insert into a full table of 8: it doubles to 16 and copies 8, cost 9. Delete: the table is now half full, so it halves to 8 and copies 8, cost 9. Insert again, and the pair repeats.

Every one of the eight alternating operations costs 9 units — the model counts them — so the amortised cost is Θ(n), not O(1). The standard fix is to halve at one quarter full instead: the model then gives 9 once and 1 for each of the next seven operations, because the table stays between one quarter and full.

Edge case 5 — the growth factor is not 2 by magic

Doubling is convenient, not necessary. Growing by a factor of 1.5 also gives O(1) amortised; growing by a constant number of slots, or by the square root of the size, does not, because then the copies are not paid for often enough. The relevant condition is that the capacity grows by a constant factor.

Edge case 6 — a potential must start at zero

The telescoped bound is total cost + Φ(Dn) − Φ(D0). If Φ(D0) is not zero, that term has to be carried; if it is large and negative, the bound is meaningless. With 2 × items − capacity on an empty table, both terms are zero and the bound is exactly the sum of the amortised costs.

Edge case 7 — a bound can be correct and still not tight

The potential 2 × items − capacity proves at most 3 units per insertion. The aggregate count proves at most 2. Both are true; 2 is better. The model confirms both: no amortised cost exceeds 3, and T(1,024)/1,024 = 1.999.

The sharper 2 does not come from this potential at all: it comes from adding up the geometric series of copies directly, which is why the aggregate method is the right tool here and the potential argument is the right tool when you need a proof written line by line. The lesson to carry: a potential function gives a valid bound, and the exam marks the bound you can defend, not the sharpest one that exists.

Misconception 1 — “amortised O(1) means every operation is fast”

Why it tempts: the phrase quotes a per-operation cost, so it reads like a per-operation promise.

Minimal counterexample: the 1,025th insertion costs 1,025 units while the amortised cost is 2 — 512.5 times larger.

Correct rule: amortised O(1) means n operations cost O(n) in total. One of them may cost Θ(n).

Misconception 2 — “amortised is another word for average case”

Why it tempts: both divide a total by a count, and both are described as “on average”.

Minimal counterexample: no distribution appears in any of the three methods. The doubling table’s bound holds for the sequence an adversary chooses, including one that triggers every expansion.

Correct rule: average case needs a probability distribution over inputs; amortised needs only a sequence of operations. Amortised is a worst-case statement about sequences.

Misconception 3 — “any potential function works”

Why it tempts: the definition allows any non-negative function that is zero at the start.

Minimal counterexample: take Φ = 0 always. Then every amortised cost equals the actual cost, and the 1,025th insertion has amortised cost 1,025. The identity holds and the bound is useless.

Correct rule: the potential must rise during cheap operations and fall during expensive ones. The definition permits anything; the argument needs a function that actually banks work.

Misconception 4 — “the counter is O(1) amortised, so increment and decrement are too”

Why it tempts: the increment-only counter is the standard example of an O(1) amortised operation.

Minimal counterexample: alternate between 65,535 and 65,536. Every operation flips 17 bits, so the average is 17 — Θ(log n).

Correct rule: an amortised bound belongs to an operation set. Adding an operation that undoes the cheap work can destroy it.

Misconception 5 — “the credit may go negative as long as it ends positive”

Why it tempts: the total is what the theorem bounds, so the end state looks like the only thing that matters.

Minimal counterexample: charge 1 for the first nine insertions of the doubling table: the credit goes to −9 at the ninth and would only recover later. Over any prefix of nine operations the bound fails, so the claim “n operations cost at most n × 1” is false at n = 9.

Correct rule: the credit must be non-negative after every operation, because the theorem is applied to every prefix.

Misconception 6 — “the potential method and the accounting method give different answers”

Why it tempts: they look like different theories, one about credit and one about functions.

Minimal counterexample: on the doubling table both give 3 per insertion, and the credit column equals the potential column at every step — 1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 6, 8, 2, …

Correct rule: the credit is the potential. The accounting method attaches it to operations and the potential method attaches it to states; the numbers agree, and choosing the presentation is a matter of what is easier to write.

The distinction that costs the most marks

Amortised and worst case are not rivals, and one does not correct the other. Both are true of the same structure. The exam sentence is: the worst-case cost of a single operation is Θ(n), and the amortised cost per operation over any sequence is O(1). Answer with the one the question names, and if it names neither, say both.

Shape 1 — the total over n operations

“A table starts empty and doubles when full. What is the total number of copy operations over n insertions?”

Reasoning demanded: sum the geometric series of copies, not the individual costs. The answer is at most n − 1 copies, so the total cost is at most 2n − 1.

Common wrong approach: multiply n by the average of the first few costs, which answers nothing and breaks as soon as n grows.

Shape 2 — the amortised cost per operation

“What is the amortised cost of one insertion?”

Reasoning demanded: divide the total by n and name the order: O(1), with a constant of 2 for doubling or 3 for the potential argument.

Common wrong approach: answering Θ(n) from the worst single insertion, which is the right answer to a different question.

Shape 3 — which potential function works

“Which of these is a valid potential function for the operation?”

Reasoning demanded: check three things — non-negative on every state, zero at the start, and a bounded amortised cost on every operation. Then check the arithmetic, not the intuition.

Common wrong approach: picking the function that “looks like” the cost. The cost itself is a valid potential that proves nothing.

Shape 4 — a sequence with a mixture

“n operations, each a push, a pop or a multipop of k. What is the worst-case total?”

Reasoning demanded: bound the removals by the insertions and note that each insertion is itself one of the n operations. The answer is at most 2n, and the model confirms no sequence of seven operations exceeds 10.

Common wrong approach: multiplying n by k, which assumes every operation is a full multipop — impossible, since a multipop needs items to remove.

Shape 5 — the change of operation set

“A counter supports increment and decrement. What is the amortised cost?”

Reasoning demanded: look for a boundary the sequence can bounce across. Alternating 65,535 and 65,536 flips 17 bits every time, so the answer is Θ(log n), not O(1).

Common wrong approach: quoting the increment-only result. The question changed the operation set; the answer must change with it.

Traps the wording sets

  • “Worst case” in the stem of a question about a sequence usually means the worst sequence, not the worst step. Read which noun the adjective modifies.
  • “Expected” signals average case and demands a distribution. If the question gives none, the answer is not an expectation.
  • “At most” and “exactly” differ: T(n) = 2n − 1 is exact for the doubling table; 3n is a bound.
  • A table that grows by one slot has amortised cost Θ(n) per insertion. The growth rule is part of the data structure, not a detail.

How to write the answer

For an amortised question, three sentences earn the marks: name the method, name the quantity, name the bound.

“Aggregate: the copies are 1 + 2 + … + n/2 < n, so n insertions cost under 2n. Amortised O(1).”

“Potential: Φ = 2·items − capacity ≥ 0, so amortised = actual + ΔΦ ≤ 3. Amortised O(1).”

MCQ Original Q1 · basic

A dynamic table starts empty and doubles its capacity when full, copying every item. Which statement is correct?

(A) Each insertion costs O(1) in the worst case.   (B) Each insertion costs Θ(n) in the worst case, and O(1) amortised.   (C) Each insertion costs Θ(n) amortised.   (D) Each insertion costs O(1) amortised only if the inputs are random.

Answer: (B). The 1,025th insertion costs 1,025 units, and 1,024 insertions cost 2,047 in total, which is 1.999 each.

(A) contradicts the 1,025th insertion. (C) confuses the total with the total per operation — the total is Θ(n), the per-operation share is constant. (D) invents a condition: no distribution is used anywhere in the argument.

NAT Original Q2 · basic

An empty table doubles when full. Over 1,024 insertions, the total number of item copies is ______.

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 1023. The copies are 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 512, a geometric series summing to 1,024 − 1.

1,024 is the answer if you count the writes as copies. 2,047 is the total cost including the writes, not the copies.

NAT Original Q3 · basic

With Φ = 2 × items − capacity, the amortised cost of the ninth insertion into an initially empty doubling table is ______ units.

Answer as an integer.

Answer: 3. The potential is 8 before (8 items in 8 slots) and 2 after (9 items in 16 slots); the actual cost is 9; 9 + (2 − 8) = 3.

9 is the actual cost, which is what the question does not ask for. 2 is the potential after the insertion, not the amortised cost. 1 is the cost of a cheap insertion.

MSQ Original Q4 · application

Which of the following are true of the amortised cost of an operation? (Select all that apply.)

(A) It can exceed the worst-case cost of a single operation.   (B) It can be smaller than the worst-case cost of a single operation.   (C) It requires a probability distribution over inputs.   (D) It bounds the total cost of any sequence of n operations by n times its value.

Answer: (B) and (D). The amortised cost lies between the best and the worst case, so it is never above the worst case, and the total over any sequence is at most n times it.

(A) is the commonest inversion: since every operation costs at most the worst case, any average of them does too. (C) describes average-case analysis; amortised analysis uses no distribution.

NAT Original Q5 · application

A binary counter starts at zero. Sixteen increment operations are performed. The total number of bit flips is ______.

Answer: 31. The flips are 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 4, 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 5, which sum to 31; the closed form is 2n − popcount(n) = 32 − 1.

32 is the answer if every increment is assumed to flip the low bit and the carries are double-counted. 16 counts only the low-bit flips. 15 counts only the carries.

MCQ Original Q6 · analysis

A table of 8 items is full. It doubles when full and halves when it becomes half full. Insert one item, then delete one item, and repeat. The cost of each operation is:

(A) 1 unit, because the table size is unchanged.   (B) 9 units, because each operation copies 8 items.   (C) 1 unit amortised, by the doubling argument.   (D) 5 units, the average of a copy and a write.

Answer: (B). Insert doubles to 16 and copies 8 (cost 9); delete halves back to 8 and copies 8 (cost 9). The model counts all eight alternating operations at 9 units each.

(C) is the trap: the doubling argument needs the cheap operations to bank work, and here the structure returns to the state it just left, so nothing is banked. (A) ignores the copies. (D) is arithmetic on the wrong quantities.

MCQ Original Q7 · analysis

For the doubling table, which function is a valid potential — non-negative on every reachable state and zero at the start — that gives an amortised cost of at most a constant?

(A) the number of items   (B) 2 × items − capacity   (C) the cost of the last operation   (D) capacity − items

Answer: (B). It is 0 at the empty table, never negative because the table is at least half full, and gives 2 for the first insertion and 3 for every later one.

(A) is zero only at the start but its changes are ±1, so an expensive insertion still has amortised cost of order n. (C) is not a function of the state, and it is not zero at the start. (D) is negative whenever the table is more than half full, which is most of the time, so the telescoped bound fails.

NAT Original Q8 · challenge

A counter starts at 65,535 and alternates increment, decrement, increment, decrement. The number of bit flips in each operation is ______.

Answer: 17. 65,535 is sixteen ones; adding one flips all sixteen and sets a new bit. 65,536 is a single one; subtracting one clears it and sets sixteen. The model confirms every one of 100 operations flips 17 bits.

1 is the answer for an increment in the middle of a run of zeros, which is what makes the increment-only counter look cheap. 16 counts the ones and forgets the new bit. 2 is the increment-only amortised cost, which this operation set does not have.

The revision sheet, in one screen

Everything on this page is one of these lines. If you can state the claim and reproduce one computation, the topic is covered.

The claim

For every sequence of n operations, total cost ≤ n × (amortised cost). No distribution, no randomness, any sequence.

Three methods

  • Aggregate: sum the costs, divide by n.
  • Accounting: charge more than the cost, bank the difference, credit never negative.
  • Potential: amortised = actual + ΔΦ, with Φ ≥ 0 and Φ(start) = 0.

Doubling table

Costs 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9, 1 … Capacities 1, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8, 8, 8, 16, 16 … T(n) = 2n − 1. Amortised 2. Φ = 2·items − capacity gives 2 then 3.

Binary counter

Total flips = 2n − popcount(n). For n = 16 that is 31. Φ = number of ones gives amortised exactly 2 per increment. Add decrement and it is Θ(log n).

Stack with multipop

Total ≤ 2n, since removals ≤ pushes ≤ n. The bound is loose: the largest total over all 78,125 sequences of 7 operations is 10.

Numbers to remember

1,024 insertions: 2,047 units, 1,023 copies, 1.999 each. Worst single insertion: 1,025 units, 512.5 times the amortised 2. Sixteen increments: 31 flips.

Four traps

  • Amortised is not the cost of every operation.
  • Amortised is not average case: no distribution.
  • A potential must be non-negative at every state, not just at the end.
  • The bound belongs to an operation set; adding decrement or halving at half full destroys it.

The one-line test

Is the expensive step paid for by work already done? Doubling banks it in the cheap insertions; the counter banks it in the zeros it set; a stack banks it in the pushes. If nothing is banked, the amortised cost is the worst case.
