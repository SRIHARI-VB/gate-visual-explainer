Topic and objective

TOPIC: Deleting a key from a red-black tree — the splice that moves the successor, the "extra black" that is bookkeeping rather than a colour, and the four repair cases that remove it.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a red-black tree and a key to delete, say which node actually leaves the tree and whose colour decides whether any repair is needed. Given the state after the splice, name the case from the sibling's colour and the two nephews' colours, and say what follows it. Report the case sequence in the order it fires and the number of rotations — and reject the four answers that look right: "the fix-up starts where the deleted key stood", "every deletion needs a repair", "case 2 rotates", and "three rotations means three cases".

I read this topic as one deficit, four repairs. Every deletion that needs work is one black short on one path, and every number in the lesson — the case sequences, the rotation counts, the black-heights — follows from that single quantity. The lesson spends its time on making the four repairs mechanical, not on restating the five rules.

Conventions used throughout

bh(x) = blacks below x, NIL included, x excludedSo bh(NIL) = 0 and a node whose children are both NIL has black-height 1. Every black-height in this lesson follows that convention.

height(empty) = −1, height(leaf) = 0The same convention as the AVL and red-black insertion lessons, so a tree of three keys in one level has height 1.

y = the node that leaves the treey is the key asked for when that key has fewer than two children, and the successor when it has two. y's original colour decides whether a repair runs.

x = the node handed to the fix-upx is the child that took y's place, which may be the NIL sentinel. The fix-up is called on x, not on the key that was asked for.

Mathematical derivation Every tree, black-height, case sequence and rotation count on this page is computed by the page's own model, which runs CLRS deletion and re-checks all five rules after each operation. Nothing is quoted from a diagram.

How this differs from the lesson next door

Red-Black Trees: Rules, Black-Height and the Insertion Repairs proves the height bound and repairs an insertion: the fault is a red node with a red parent, the case is read from the uncle, and the cost is at most two rotations. Its deletion material is one scene — a red leaf against a black leaf — and it says so.

This lesson takes the other half. The fault here is not a colour pair but a missing black, so the case is read from the sibling and the two nephews, not from an uncle. There are four cases rather than three, one of them can repeat at every level, one of them is a pure conversion, and the rotation count is three rather than two. The single fact the two lessons share is the shape of a rotation.

Intuition, then the definition of the fault

Insertion breaks a red-black tree by putting two reds in a row, which you can see. Deletion breaks it by taking away something you cannot see: a black node that was paying for one step on every path below it. Nothing about the colours looks wrong afterwards. The tree is a perfectly good search tree with legal colours, and one path is quietly one black short. So deletion is not a shape problem; it is an accounting problem. The whole algorithm is one debt, carried up the tree until something can pay it.

The fault, stated exactly

Verified fact If the node that leaves the tree is black, then every path that ran through it now carries one fewer black than the paths that did not. Rule five — all paths from a node to a NIL leaf hold the same number of blacks — fails at the first ancestor of the removed node.

If the node that leaves is red, nothing is owed: red contributes no black to any count. If the node that leaves is black but its replacement is red, one colour write pays the debt.

Only rule five is at risk. A deletion never creates a red-red edge, because the removed node leaves no children behind, and it never changes the root unless the tree becomes empty.

The two colours that are easy to confuse

When the key to delete has two children, the key does not leave: the successor does. The successor moves into the vacated place and inherits the deleted node's colour, which keeps every path above correct. The colour it had before the move is saved, because that colour — not the inherited one — decides whether a repair runs.

In the seven-key tree, deleting 12 moves 15 into 12's place. 15 becomes red, inheriting 12's colour; 15's own original colour was black, so a repair runs. Both facts are true at once and they are about different colours.

Why the debt can be carried at all

After the splice, exactly one region of the tree is wrong: the subtree hanging from x, the node that took the removed node's place. Every path outside it is untouched, and every path above it is untouched, because the removed node's colour was inherited.

So the repair only ever needs to look at x, its parent, its sibling, and the sibling's two children. That is five nodes, which is why four cases cover everything — and why the loop can be written without ever recomputing a black-height.

The four cases, and what each one is for

Read the sibling first, then the two nephews. The near nephew is the sibling's child on x's side; the far nephew is the other one. The names below assume x is a left child; when x is a right child every left and right is exchanged and nothing else changes.

Cases 1 and 3 — conversions

Case 1: the sibling is red. A red sibling must have black children. Recolour the sibling black and the parent red, rotate at the parent, and the new sibling is black. One rotation. It never ends the deletion.

Case 3: the sibling is black, the near nephew red, the far nephew black. Recolour the near nephew black and the sibling red, rotate at the sibling, and the far nephew is now red. One rotation. It never ends the deletion either.

Cases 2 and 4 — the two exits

Case 2: the sibling is black and both nephews are black. Recolour the sibling red, which takes a black from the sibling side too, and move the debt up to the parent. No rotation. This is the only case that can repeat, once per level.

Case 4: the sibling is black and the far nephew is red. The sibling takes the parent's colour, the parent and the far nephew go black, and one rotation at the parent pays the debt. This is the only case that ends a deletion by rotating.

The rotation bound, and where it comes from

Mathematical derivation Cases 1, 3 and 4 each rotate once. Case 1 is followed by case 2, 3 or 4; case 3 is always followed by case 4; case 4 ends the loop. So the longest path through the cases is 1 → 3 → 4, which rotates three times, and no sequence rotates more.

Verified fact CLRS states the same conclusion: "the procedure RB-DELETE-FIXUP takes O(lg n) time and performs at most three rotations". Case 2 is the only case in which the loop repeats, and it performs no rotation, so the repetitions are recolourings, not rotations.

What the bound does not say

It does not say a deletion performs at most three case applications. Case 2 may fire once per level, so a deletion on a tree of height h can recolour O(h) times. It does not say three rotations are typical: over every insertion order of up to six keys, 3,959 of 5,039 deletions needed no case at all, and only 108 needed three rotations.

It also says nothing about the total work: finding the key, finding the successor and climbing the tree are each O(log n), so the whole operation is O(log n) whatever the rotation count.

Worked example 1 — a key with two children

Every tree below is computed by the page's model. The seven-key tree is 20B(12R(10B(5R), 15B), 30B(25R)), height 3, black-height 2, in-order 5, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25, 30.

Step by step: delete 12

  1. 12 has two children, so the successor is the smallest key of its right subtree: 15. 15 is the node that leaves, not 12
  2. 15 moves into 12's place and takes 12's colour, red. the paths above keep their black count
  3. 15's original colour was black, so a repair runs. the saved colour, not the inherited one
  4. x is 15's old right child, a NIL leaf, and the deficit is under 15. the short path is the one that ran through the old 15

Now read the sibling of x: 10, which is black, with 5 red on its near side and NIL on its far side. The far nephew is black, so case 3 would fire — but here the near nephew is red and the sibling's other child is NIL, and the model records case 4 after one rotation. The result is 20B(10R(5B, 15B), 30B(25R)), height 2.

What the model recorded

successor: 15 · the colour 15 had before the move: black · the colour 15 inherited: red · cases fired: 4 · rotations: 1 · height 3 → 2 · all five rules hold.

Compare the same key deleted with the repair skipped: the tree is left with the paths through the old 15 one black short, and rule five fails. That intermediate tree is what scene 1 and scene 2 draw.

The mistake this example is built to catch

Most written answers start the repair at 12, because 12 is the key in the question. The repair starts at the NIL child of the old 15, one level lower and on the other side of the tree. A question that asks "where does the fix-up begin" is testing exactly this.

The second mistake is to test the colour of the promoted node. The test is the colour it had before the move; here 15 is red after the move and black before it, so the two answers disagree and only one of them is the algorithm's.

Worked example 2 — one deletion per case

Case 4 alone. Insert 1, 2, 3, 4: 2B(1B, 3B(., 4R)). Delete 1. The sibling 3 is black and the far nephew 4 is red. Case 4, one rotation, result 3B(2B, 4B), height 2 → 1.

Case 3 then 4. Insert 1, 2, 4, 3: 2B(1B, 4B(3R, .)). Delete 1. The near nephew 3 is red and the far nephew is NIL. Cases 3 then 4, two rotations, result 3B(2B, 4B).

Note that the two trees reach the same result by different routes, with different rotation counts. A question that asks only for the final tree cannot distinguish them; a question that asks for the count can.

Worked example 3 — the case that repeats

The all-black perfect tree of seven keys is 8B(4B(2B, 6B), 12B(10B, 14B)), height 2, black-height 3. It is the smallest tree in which case 2 fires twice, and it is not reachable by insertion alone — which is why the model builds it from a literal specification and then checks all five rules on it.

Step by step: delete 2

  1. x is the NIL left child of 4; the sibling 6 is black with two black children. case 2
  2. Recolour 6 red and move x to 4. both sides of 4 are now one short
  3. At 4 the sibling is 12, black with two black children. case 2 again
  4. Recolour 12 red and move x to the root. the debt reaches the root and is dropped

Result: 8B(4B(., 6R), 12R(10B, 14B)). Height stays 2, the root black-height falls from 3 to 2, and no rotation was performed.

Why the black-height may fall

When the debt reaches the root, every path in the tree is one black short — equally. Dropping the debt is therefore not a repair of one path but a redefinition of the tree's black-height: it becomes one smaller, and rule five holds again.

This is the only way a deletion changes the black-height of the whole tree, and it is why a question that fixes the black-height at the start and never rechecks it goes wrong on exactly this shape.

Worked example 4 — four deletions, four outcomes

Built by inserting 5, 10, 9, 1, 2, 8, 7, 4, 3, 6, which gives height 4. Then:

Delete 9 — successor 10, black. Cases 2, 3, 4; two rotations; height 4 → 3.

Delete 1 — case 2 twice; no rotation; root black-height 3 → 2.

Delete 7 — the successor 8 is red, so no fix-up runs at all.

Delete 4 — the successor 5 is black but its child 6 is red, so x is red, the loop never runs, and one recolour ends it.

The pattern behind the four outcomes

Two questions decide everything, and they are asked in this order. First: was the node that left red? If yes, stop. Second: is the node that replaced it red? If yes, colour it black and stop. Only if both answers are no does the four-case machinery run.

That is why 3,959 of the 5,039 deletions in the sweep never fire a case: most deletions are answered by one of the two cheap exits.

Boundaries, and the counterexamples that matter most

Every card here is a case where a rule that works in general fails at the boundary, or where a plausible shortcut gives the wrong answer.

Counterexample 1 — a black deletion that needs no repair

Deleting the black leaf 15 from the seven-key tree breaks rule five. But deleting 7 from the ten-key build of 5, 10, 9, 1, 2, 8, 7, 4, 3, 6 needs no repair at all: 7 is black, yet the key that actually leaves is its successor 8, and 8 is red.

So "the deleted key was black" is not the test. The test is the colour of the node that leaves, and that node is often the successor.

Counterexample 2 — a red deletion that still costs a rotation

Deleting a red node never needs a repair, but deleting a black node can need none either, and a red node can be promoted into a place that then needs one. Deleting 12 from the seven-key tree promotes the red-dressed 15 and still fires case 4, because the colour that matters was 15's original black.

The two statements "the removed node was red" and "the promoted node is red" are different, and neither alone decides the repair.

The empty tree and the single node

Deleting the only key leaves the root as the NIL sentinel. x is then the root, so the loop condition fails immediately and the final blackening sets the sentinel black, which it already is. No case fires.

The empty tree is a red-black tree: rules 1, 3 and 5 hold vacuously, rule 2 has no root to violate, and rule 4 has no red node. Its height is −1 and its black-height is 0.

The sibling is never the NIL sentinel

While the debt is real, x is black and its sibling cannot be NIL. If the sibling were NIL, the paths through the sibling would already carry one black fewer than the paths through x before the deletion, which contradicts rule five in the tree the deletion started from.

So a case analysis that begins "if the sibling is NIL" is analysing a state the algorithm never reaches. The two real branches are: sibling red, sibling black.

x may be a sentinel, and the code must cope

When the removed node had no children, x is the shared NIL sentinel. It has no useful child pointers, so the deletion sets the sentinel's parent before the fix-up runs, and the fix-up reads x's parent rather than the tree's structure to know which side it is on.

In an exam this appears as a deletion of a leaf: the drawing shows the removed node's place as empty, and the repair still needs to know the parent of that empty place.

Deleting a key that is not there

The search reaches the sentinel and the deletion returns without changing anything. No repair runs, no rule can break, and the tree is identical — including its colours.

This is a property of the search-tree half, and it is worth remembering when a question gives a deletion sequence containing a key that was never inserted.

Two children whose successor is the right child

When the successor is the right child of the node being deleted, there is no second splice: the successor simply moves up, and its right child becomes x with the successor as parent. The CLRS code handles this with a special case, and forgetting it is a common implementation bug.

In the seven-key tree this does not happen; in the ten-key build, deleting 4 hits it, and the model records no case there because the successor's child was red.

Mirror cases

Every case has a mirror, taken by exchanging left and right. That makes eight cases in the code and four in the explanation. A question that says "x is a right child" is not a new case: it is the same four with the near and far nephew swapped.

The near nephew is always the sibling's child on x's side. Reading it the other way round turns case 3 into case 4 and gets the rotation direction wrong.

Six wrong beliefs, each with the smallest example that ends it

Each card states the belief, why it is tempting, the smallest counterexample, and the rule that replaces it.

1 — "The fix-up starts where the deleted key stood"

Tempting because the question names a key, so the key feels like the centre of the operation.

Counterexample: delete 12 from the seven-key tree. The repair runs under the old 15, on the other side of the tree, because 15 is the node that left.

Replacement: the fix-up is called on x, the child that took the removed node's place.

2 — "Every deletion needs a fix-up"

Tempting because insertion almost always needs one, and deletion is described as the hard operation.

Counterexample: 3,959 of the 5,039 deletions in this page's sweep fire no case at all — including deleting 7 from the ten-key build, where the successor 8 was red.

Replacement: a repair runs only when the node that leaves was black and the node that replaces it is black.

3 — "The extra black is a third colour"

Tempting because the standard explanation says x is "doubly black", which sounds like a stored value.

Counterexample: the node stores one bit. In the code, x is a pointer and the debt is the loop's state; nothing about the node changes until a recolouring writes it.

Replacement: the extra black is an accounting device for the proof. Two colours plus one bit per node is the entire storage.

Page 1

4 — "Case 2 rotates"

Tempting because cases 1, 3 and 4 do rotate, and case 2 sits between them in the list.

Counterexample: the all-black perfect tree, delete 2: case 2 twice, zero rotations, and the tree is valid at the end.

Replacement: case 2 recolours the sibling and moves the debt up. It is the only case that repeats, and the only one that cannot rotate.

5 — "Three rotations means three cases"

Tempting because each rotating case rotates once, so counting rotations and counting cases look like the same job.

Counterexample: deleting 1 from the ten-key build fires case 2 twice and rotates zero times — two cases, no rotation. And a long tree can fire case 2 once per level.

Replacement: report the case sequence and the rotation count separately. The sequence can be long; the rotation count is at most three.

6 — "Case 1 and case 3 can finish a deletion"

Tempting because both perform a rotation, and a rotation feels like a conclusion.

Counterexample: across every insertion order of up to six keys, the sweep never records a bare case 1 or a bare case 3 — the five sequences it does record are 4, 1-2, 1-4, 3-4 and 1-3-4.

Replacement: case 1 hands over to 2, 3 or 4; case 3 always hands over to 4. Only case 4 ends the loop by rotating, and case 2 ends it by reaching a red node or the root.

The one distinction that fixes all six

Insertion breaks a tree with a colour fault and reads the uncle. Deletion breaks a tree with a counting fault and reads the sibling and its two nephews. Almost every wrong answer above comes from carrying one habit across from the other operation.

Five question shapes, the reasoning each demands, and the usual wrong approach

Red-black trees are not named in the GATE 2027 syllabus, so nothing here is a prediction that a question will appear. These are the shapes such a question takes when it does, and each one is a different kind of reasoning.

Shape 1 — which node leaves, and does a repair run?

Demands: find the key, check its children, find the successor if there are two, and report the successor's original colour. Two answers follow: the node that leaves, and whether a fix-up is called.

Usual wrong approach: reporting the colour of the key in the question, or of the promoted node after it has inherited the deleted colour.

Shape 2 — name the case, or the sequence of cases

Demands: draw the tree after the splice, mark x, read the sibling's colour, then the near and far nephew. Report cases in the order they fire, and remember that 1 and 3 always hand over.

Usual wrong approach: stopping at case 1 or case 3, or reading the near nephew as the far one and reversing the rotation direction.

Shape 3 — count the rotations

Demands: one rotation for each of cases 1, 3 and 4, and none for case 2. The maximum for a single deletion is three, reached only by 1 → 3 → 4.

Usual wrong approach: counting recolourings as rotations, or answering three because "three is the bound" when the case sequence is a single case 4.

Shape 4 — the tree after the repair

Demands: apply each case exactly, including the colour the sibling inherits in case 4. Then recheck rule five at every node, because a correct answer passes it everywhere.

Usual wrong approach: rotating correctly but leaving the parent red, which breaks rule five at the new root of the subtree.

Shape 5 — complexity and the comparison with AVL

Demands: search, insert and delete are O(log n) in both structures. The difference is the repair cost: at most two rotations for a red-black insertion, three for a deletion, against AVL deletion, which may rotate at every level on the path.

Usual wrong approach: claiming red-black deletion is O(1) rotations therefore O(1) time — the recolouring loop still climbs O(log n) levels.

Q1 MCQ Original

In the standard red-black deletion, whose original colour decides whether the fix-up procedure is called?

(A) the node holding the key named in the deletion
(B) the node that actually leaves the tree
(C) the child that takes its place
(D) the root of the tree

Answer: B. The procedure saves the colour of y, the node that leaves, before the splice. When the key has fewer than two children y is that key; when it has two, y is the successor. If y was red, no black left any path and no repair is needed.

A is right only in the one-child case, and is the commonest error: deleting 12 from the seven-key tree repairs the tree because of 15's colour, not 12's. C is the colour tested inside the loop, not the trigger. D is where the debt can be dropped, which is the opposite end of the operation.

Check on this page's model: deleting 7 from the ten-key build names a black key, yet no fix-up runs, because the successor 8 was red.

Q2 NAT Original

What is the maximum number of rotations performed by a single red-black deletion?

Answer: 3. Cases 1, 3 and 4 each rotate once, case 4 ends the loop, and the only sequence that uses all three is 1 → 3 → 4.

Two traps. Answering 2 imports the insertion bound, which is two because insertion has only three cases and case 1 never rotates there. Answering "unbounded" because case 2 can repeat confuses recolouring with rotating: case 2 rotates nothing, so repeating it adds no rotations.

Q3 MCQ Original

A deletion fires at least one case. Which case sequence is impossible?

(A) 4   (B) 3, 4   (C) 1, 2   (D) 3 alone

Answer: D. Case 3 recolours the near nephew and rotates at the sibling, which always leaves a black sibling whose far nephew is red — so case 4 must follow.

A is the single terminal case: insert 1, 2, 3, 4 and delete 1. B is insert 1, 2, 4, 3 and delete 1. C is insert 1 to 6 and delete 1, where case 1 leaves a black sibling with two black children.

The sweep on this page never records a bare case 3, nor a bare case 1, over all 5,039 deletions it examines.

Q4 MSQ Original

Which of the following are true of the deletion fix-up?

(A) Case 2 is the only case that can repeat at the same level count.
(B) Case 2 performs a rotation when both nephews are black.
(C) Case 1 always hands over to case 2, 3 or 4.
(D) Case 4 terminates the repair.

Answer: A, C and D.

A: case 2 moves the debt to the parent, so the loop can run again one level up. C: recolouring the red sibling and rotating at the parent leaves a black sibling, which is the precondition of cases 2, 3 and 4. D: case 4 pays the debt and the code sets x to the root to force the loop out.

B is false. Case 2 recolours the sibling red and moves up; the rotations belong to cases 1, 3 and 4. The all-black perfect tree deleted at 2 fires case 2 twice with zero rotations.

Q5 MCQ Original

The tree 20B(12R(10B(5R), 15B), 30B(25R)) is given. Delete 15. Which case fires, and how many rotations are performed?

(A) case 2, 0 rotations   (B) case 3, 1 rotation   (C) case 4, 1 rotation   (D) no case, 0 rotations

Answer: C. 15 is a black leaf, so x is its NIL child under the red 12. The sibling is 10, black, whose near child 5 is red and whose far child is NIL. Because x is a right child, the near child is 10's right — which is NIL — so the red 5 is the far nephew and case 4 fires at once. The result is 20B(10R(5B, 12B), 30B(25R)), height 2.

B is the mirror error: reading the near and far nephews the other way round turns case 4 into case 3 and reverses the rotation. A would need 10's children to be black, and 5 is red. D would need x to be red or the removed node red, and neither holds.

The page's model records exactly this: cases 4, one rotation, height 3 → 2, all five rules hold.

Q6 NAT Original

The all-black perfect tree 8B(4B(2B, 6B), 12B(10B, 14B)) is given. Delete 2, repair fully, and report the black-height of the root.

Answer: 2. The root's black-height starts at 3. Case 2 fires twice — recolour 6 red, then recolour 12 red — and the debt reaches the root, where it is dropped. Every path loses one black equally, so the black-height becomes 2.

The trap is answering 3: the black-height of a tree is not fixed for its lifetime, and a deletion that drops the debt at the root is exactly the case that changes it. The height, separately, stays at 2.

Q7 MCQ Original

Which deletions are guaranteed to need no repair case at all?

(A) deleting a red leaf
(B) deleting a black node whose only child is red
(C) deleting a red node with one black child
(D) both A and B

Answer: D. A red node contributes no black to any path, so removing it changes no black count. A black node with a red only child is replaced by that child, which the final step colours black — the debt is paid by one colour write, with no case.

C is impossible, not merely wrong: a red node cannot have a black child that is its only child, because rule five would then fail at the red node — its two sides would hold different numbers of blacks. That is why the option is a trap rather than a case.

The page measures the first two: deleting 25 needs no fix-up at all, and deleting 4 from the ten-key build fires no case although a fix-up is called, because its replacement 6 was red.

Q8 NAT Original

Insert 1, 2, 4, 3 into an empty red-black tree, then delete 1 with a full repair. How many rotations does the deletion perform?

Answer: 2. The tree before the deletion is 2B(1B, 4B(3R, .)). x is the NIL child of 1; the sibling 4 is black, the near nephew 3 is red and the far nephew is NIL. Case 3 recolours and rotates right at 4, one rotation; case 4 then recolours and rotates left at 2, one rotation. The result is 3B(2B, 4B).

Answering 1 counts only case 3, which is the "a rotation ends the repair" error: case 3 always hands over. Answering 0 mistakes this for the insertion-style pair, where the second case in the pair is a single rotation after a straightening — the same shape, but the counts belong to the deletion's four cases.

The procedure in four lines

1 · Find the key. If it has two children, the successor is the node that leaves; otherwise the key leaves.

2 · Move the successor up and give it the deleted node's colour. Save the colour it had before.

3 · If that saved colour was red, stop. If the replacement child is red, colour it black and stop.

4 · Otherwise repair at x: read the sibling, then the near and far nephew.

The four cases

1 sibling red → recolour, rotate at the parent, hand over. 1 rotation.

2 sibling black, both nephews black → recolour the sibling, climb. 0 rotations, may repeat.

3 sibling black, near nephew red, far black → recolour, rotate at the sibling, hand over. 1 rotation.

4 sibling black, far nephew red → recolour three, rotate at the parent, end. 1 rotation.

Counts worth memorising

At most 2 rotations per insertion, at most 3 per deletion.

Longest case sequence: 1 → 3 → 4. Only case 2 repeats; only case 4 ends by rotating.

Measured on this page: 5,039 deletions, 1,080 with at least one case, 3,959 with none, five distinct sequences, largest rotation count 3 (108 times).

The five traps in one line each

The fix-up starts under the successor, not where the key stood.

A deletion does not always need a repair.

The extra black is bookkeeping, not a third colour.

Case 2 recolours and never rotates.

Three rotations is not three cases: case 2 can repeat at every level.

What this lesson rests on, and where the research stopped

Each source is listed with what it supports. Verified facts are things a source states; derivations are worked out on the page; interpretations are analogies.

Primary algorithm source

CLRS, Introduction to Algorithms, chapter 13.4 "Deletion". The four cases, the sentinel, the three ways the loop ends, the statement that case 2 is the only case in which the loop repeats, and the conclusion that RB-DELETE-FIXUP takes O(lg n) time and performs at most three rotations. Quoted through the public chapter text and the standard exercise solutions for 13.4-1, 13.4-2, 13.4-5 and 13.4-6.

Exercise 13.4-6 in particular is what licenses the claim in scene 3 that the sibling of x cannot be NIL: at the start of case 1 the sibling is red, so the parent must be black.

University lecture notes

Northeastern (Khoury) lecture notes on red-black trees give the same four cases with the near and far nephew named, and state that cases 1 and 3 are conversions while case 2 may cascade. Duke CPS 230 and Dartmouth CS 10 notes state the "at most three rotations" bound and the double-black accounting in the same form used here.

These were used to check the wording of the cases, not to source any number: every number on the page comes from the page's own model.

GATE 2027 CS syllabus, IIT Madras, Section 4 Programming and Data Structures: "Programming in C. Recursion. Arrays, stacks, queues, linked lists, trees, binary search trees, binary heaps, graphs." Read from the official syllabus PDF for GATE 2027 during this run.

What was not established

No previous-year question on red-black deletion could be matched to a GATE year and paper in this run's research, so none is quoted. Practice archives do circulate such items, but the attributions did not survive checking.

No frequency or weightage claim is made. The sweep of 5,039 deletions is a measurement of this implementation on small trees; it is evidence for the case sequences and rotation counts, not a proof about all trees. The general statements come from CLRS, and the page says which is which.

How the numbers were produced

The page implements a red-black tree with a NIL sentinel, CLRS insertion and CLRS deletion, logging every case and rotation. After every single operation it re-tests all five rules, and it also runs a plain deletion that skips the fix-up, so the deficit can be drawn. At load it sweeps every insertion order of one to six distinct keys and deletes every key from each: 5,039 deletions. The result — 1,080 with at least one case, 3,959 with none, five distinct case sequences, and a largest rotation count of 3 seen 108 times — is what the cost scene reports. The check panel in the Animation tab states how many of the page's own claims passed.
