Topic and objective

TOPIC: Mensuration and geometry for GATE General Aptitude — length, area and volume; the scale law for similar figures; and the map that turns a flat sheet of paper into a cylinder or a cube.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a figure or a sheet, decide which quantity is being asked for (perimeter, area, surface area, volume), fix the unit, decide which length plays which role, compute the answer without calculus, and test it against the k, k squared, k cubed scale law.

I read the request as the mensuration half of the GA syllabus: plane areas, solid surfaces, volumes and the flat-sheet-to-solid map. Coordinate geometry appears only where a length between two points is needed; trigonometry and calculus are not used anywhere on this page.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus. Section 2 (Quantitative Aptitude) of the official GATE 2027 General Aptitude syllabus, published by IIT Madras, prints: “Mensuration and geometry, Elementary statistics and probability.” (read 2026-11-04 from the GA syllabus PDF.)

Verified fact General Aptitude carries 15 of the 100 marks in the GATE paper, so the GA section is a fixed part of every attempt rather than an optional subject.

Unverified claim No claim is made here about how many GA questions in a given year were mensuration. Two previous-year items are reproduced on the Examples tab because their paper, question number and answer could be confirmed; no frequency is inferred from them.

Educational interpretation The topic is examinable because every step is one multiplication and one division, the answer is a single number, and the distractors are built from the three or four standard slips.

Prerequisites

  • Multiplication and division of decimals, and the meaning of a unit such as 1 cm.
  • π as the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter, taken as 22/7 or 3.14159 when a question says so.
  • Fractions on a number line: reading 3/4 − 1/3 as a width.
  • That a square’s area is side times side, and a rectangle’s is length times breadth.
  • No calculus and no trigonometry. Where a volume carries a 1/3 or a 4/3 factor, the page shows it by stacking thin slices, which is counting, not integration.

Nothing on this page needs a calculator: every figure is computed in the browser when the page loads, and the check count is printed under the canvas.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • Name the quantity — fence, paint and water are three different measurements, and only one of them is asked.
  • Area counts squares, volume counts cubes — that is where the squared and cubed units come from.
  • k, k squared, k cubed — scaling every length by k scales areas by k² and volumes by k³.
  • One phrase fixes the roll — which edge becomes the circle decides the answer, and here it is worth a factor of six.
  • The optimum needs its condition — the square wins among rectangles, the sphere among closed shapes.

Approximate learning time

About 50–70 minutes for the nine levels, with the animation watched twice: once for the sheet-to-solid map, once for the scale law and the optima. (Approximate, and an estimate rather than a measurement.)

Intuition first: one wall, three questions

A gardener, a painter and a plumber can all be sent to the same walled tank. Educational interpretation

The gardener measures the wall to buy fencing: a length, in metres. The painter measures the wall to buy paint: a surface, in square metres. The plumber measures the tank to fill it: a space, in cubic metres. Three trades, one object, three quantities. Almost every mensuration question is decided by working out which trade is asking.

The second decision is which length plays which role. A sheet of paper 6 cm by 1 cm rolled into a tube has a circle of circumference 6 and a height of 1 — or a circle of circumference 1 and a height of 6, depending on which edges you bring together. The paper is identical; the solid is not.

Definition 1 — the four measurements

Perimeter: the length of the boundary of a plane figure, in centimetres. A fence, a frame, a running track’s inner edge.

Area: the number of unit squares that cover a plane figure, in square centimetres. Paint, paper, land.

Surface area: the number of unit squares that cover the boundary of a solid. Lateral (or curved) surface counts only the side wall; total surface adds the flat faces.

Volume: the number of unit cubes that fill a solid, in cubic centimetres. Water, air, concrete.

Definition 2 — why the units square and cube

A rectangle a by b is tiled by a × b unit squares, so its area is a·b square units. A box a × b × c is filled by a·b·c unit cubes, so its volume is a·b·c cubic units.

The sheet of this lesson is 6 cm by 1 cm: area 6 × 1 = 6 cm², perimeter 2(6 + 1) = 14 cm. Both numbers describe the same rectangle and neither can replace the other.

Mathematical derivation The multiplication is a shortcut for the count: a columns of b squares each.

Trap 1 — enough area is not enough shape

The sheet has area 6, and a 2 × 2 square has area 4, which is less. Yet no 2 × 2 square can be cut from a strip that is 1 cm wide. Area compares amounts; the shape still has to fit. Mathematical derivation 2 > 1, so the width fails before the area is consulted.

The scale law: the one fact that survives every shape

Scale every length in a figure by the same k. A rectangle a × b becomes ka × kb, whose area is k²(ab). A box becomes ka × kb × kc, whose volume is k³(abc). Mathematical derivation

The same factor appears for a circle (π(kr)² = k²πr²), a sphere (4/3π(kR)³ = k³ · 4/3πR³) and a cone, because each of them can be tiled or sliced into copies of itself. Lengths go k, areas k², volumes k³.

Plane formulas, and the condition on each

  • Rectangle: A = ab, P = 2(a+b).
  • Triangle: A = ½ · base · height — the height is perpendicular to the base chosen.
  • Triangle from three sides: A = √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)), s = (a+b+c)/2 (Heron). Needs a valid triangle: each side less than the sum of the other two.
  • Trapezium: A = ½(a+b)h — the two parallel sides only.
  • Circle: A = πr², circumference C = 2πr.
  • Sector of angle θ: A = πr² · θ/360, arc = 2πr · θ/360.
  • Annulus (ring): A = π(R² − r²).

Solid formulas, and the condition on each

  • Cuboid a×b×c: V = abc, S = 2(ab+bc+ca).
  • Cube side a: V = a³, S = 6a² — six faces, all equal.
  • Cylinder radius r, height h: V = πr²h, curved = 2πrh, total = 2πrh + 2πr² only if closed.
  • Cone radius r, height h, slant l = √(r²+h²): V = ⅓πr²h, curved = πrl, total = πrl + πr².
  • Sphere radius R: V = 4/3πR³, S = 4πR².
  • Hemisphere: V = 2/3πR³, curved = 2πR², total = 3πR².
  • Pyramid, square base a, height h: V = ⅓a²h.

The sheet-to-solid map, in four steps

Step 1. Read the phrase that fixes the roll: by bringing the short edges together means the short edges become the vertical seam, so the long edge wraps into the circle. Educational interpretation

Step 2. Circumference = that wrapped edge, so 2πr = 6 and r = 3/π ≈ 0.9549 cm.

Step 3. Height = the other edge, unchanged: h = 1 cm.

Step 4. Check that paper was neither stretched nor added: the curved surface 2πrh = 2π(3/π)(1) = 6 equals the sheet’s area. If it does not, the mapping is wrong. Mathematical derivation

The cube from a sheet

A closed cube is six equal squares, so its whole surface is 6a². If the paper is the entire surface, 6a² = 6 gives a = 1 and V = 1.

A 6 × 1 strip cuts into exactly six 1 × 1 squares, so nothing is wasted and nothing larger fits: a bigger cube would need a face wider than the strip.

The assumption hiding in the wording

“Assuming the ends of the cylinder are closed” is a statement about the ends, not a gift of extra paper. The curved side uses all 6 cm². Closing the two ends would need another 2πr² = 18/π ≈ 5.730 cm², which the sheet does not have.

Mathematical derivation With the ends paid for, the total paper would be 6 + 18/π ≈ 11.73 cm², twice what we own.

The takeaway of the build-up

Every mensuration question is two decisions and one check: which quantity, which length is which, then does the paper (or the fence) add up. The arithmetic after that is one multiplication.

Example 1 — a verified previous-year question

Verified GATE PYQ GATE 2021, CS, Set 1, General Aptitude, two-mark MCQ (question id 823251526).

“We have 2 rectangular sheets of paper, M and N, of dimensions 6 cm × 1 cm each. Sheet M is rolled to form an open cylinder by bringing the short edges of the sheet together. Sheet N is cut into equal square patches and assembled to form the largest possible closed cube. Assuming the ends of the cylinder are closed, the ratio of the volume of the cylinder to that of the cube is ______.” Options include π/2, 3/π, 9/π and 3π. Recorded answer: 9/π.

  1. Short edges together, so the 6 cm edge is the circle: 2πr = 6 ⇒ r = 3/π. Justification: the seam is where the two short edges meet, so each short edge is vertical.
  2. Height is the untouched short edge: h = 1.
  3. Vcyl = πr²h = π(9/π²)(1) = 9/π ≈ 2.865 cm³.
  4. Six unit squares from sheet N close a cube of side 1: 6a² = 6 ⇒ a = 1, Vcube = 1.
  5. Ratio = (9/π)/1 = 9/π ≈ 2.865.

Why the other options fail. 3/π is what the other roll gives (see Example 4). 3π comes from dividing by π instead of multiplying, and π/2 has no route to it at all. The page recomputes all five numbers rather than quoting a key.

Example 2 — the second verified previous-year question

Verified GATE PYQ GATE 2022, CS, General Aptitude, Q2, one-mark MCQ. A function y(x) on [0,1] equals 2 for 0 ≤ x < 1/3, 3 for 1/3 ≤ x < 3/4, and 1 for 3/4 ≤ x ≤ 1. The area under the curve is asked. Official key: C, 13/6.

Widths: 1/3, 3/4 − 1/3 = 5/12, 1 − 3/4 = 1/4; they add to 1. Products: 2/3, 5/4, 1/4.

Sum in twenty-fourths: 8 + 30 + 6 = 44, and 44/24 = 13/6 ≈ 2.1667. A flat top makes each piece a rectangle, so no calculus is needed.

Example 3 — a path around a garden

A square garden of side 10 m gets a path 1 m wide all round. Path area = 12² − 10² = 144 − 100 = 44 m². Adding four strips of 10 × 1 gives only 40 and forgets the four corner squares.

Now the same 1 m path round a circle of radius 5 m: π(6² − 5²) = 11π ≈ 34.558 m². Same width, same idea, different number: the path area depends on the shape, so “width times perimeter” is only an approximation.

Example 4 — the other way to roll

Bring the long edges together instead. The 1 cm edge is now the circle: 2πr = 1 ⇒ r = 1/(2π) ≈ 0.159, and h = 6.

V = πr²h = π(1/4π²)(6) = 3/(2π) ≈ 0.477 cm³. The ratio to the first roll is exactly 6 — the ratio of the two edges — because the volume scales with the square of the wrapped edge and inversely with the height. Same paper, six times less water.

Example 5 — a cone, r = 3, h = 4

Slant height l = √(3²+4²) = 5. Volume = ⅓π(9)(4) = 12π ≈ 37.699. Curved surface = πrl = 15π ≈ 47.124. Total = 15π + 9π = 24π ≈ 75.398.

Mathematical derivation The 1/3 is not a convention: the page stacks 4000 thin discs of radius 3(1 − x/4) and their total agrees with 12π to two decimal places. Using the vertical height 4 in place of the slant 5 gives 12π for the curved surface instead of 15π.

Example 6 — a sphere at r = 3

V = 4/3π(27) = 36π ≈ 113.097 and S = 4π(9) = 36π ≈ 113.097: at this one radius the two numbers coincide, and the units differ (cm³ against cm²).

Solving 4/3πR³ = 4πR² gives R = 0 or R = 3. At R = 4 the volume is 256π/3 ≈ 268.08 and the surface 64π ≈ 201.06, so the coincidence is not a rule.

Example 7 — fixed material, best shape

Fence 12 cm: the integer rectangles are 1×5 (5), 2×4 (8), 3×3 (9). The square wins, and among all real rectangles it is still the maximum. Mathematical derivation a(6−a) is largest at a = 3.

Paper 6 cm² closed all round: the cube holds 1, and a sphere of the same surface holds 4/3π(0.691)³ ≈ 1.382. The sphere is the optimum, but only among shapes that close completely — which the cylinder of Example 1 does not.

Open against closed

An open cylinder’s surface is 2πrh only. A closed one adds 2πr². For the 6 × 1 sheet that is 6 against 6 + 18/π ≈ 11.73 — nearly double. Read the word closed before choosing the formula.

A pipe, a well and a tunnel are open. A can, a drum and a tank are closed.

Slant height against vertical height

A cone has two heights: the vertical h (used in the volume) and the slant l (used in the curved surface), with l² = r² + h². At r=3, h=4: l = 5, so the curved surface is 15π and not 12π.

A pyramid has the same split: slant height for the triangular faces, vertical height for the volume.

Counterexample — a longer fence does not mean a bigger field

A 4 × 4 square has perimeter 16 and area 16. A 1 × 7 rectangle has the same perimeter 16 and area 7. Perimeter and area are independent measurements of a figure, and neither determines the other.

The violated belief: “a shape with a bigger boundary holds more”. The correct rule: for a fixed perimeter the square holds the most, but between two arbitrary shapes nothing follows.

Counterexample — more paper does not mean more volume

The 6 cm² sheet makes a cylinder of volume 2.865, because its paper is one open side wall. A sphere with the same 6 cm² of paper, closed, holds 1.382. The cube holds 1.

What the paper is doing matters more than how much there is. An open surface encloses nothing by itself; it only becomes a container when something else closes it.

Units before arithmetic

A sheet 6 cm by 1 cm has area 6 cm², which is 0.0006 m². Mixing cm and m in one product gives a number that is wrong by a factor of 100 in area and 10 000 in volume.

Convert every length to one unit first; convert the answer afterwards, remembering that the conversion factor is applied twice for area and three times for volume.

What “largest possible” is capping

In the 2021 question the strip is 1 cm wide, so the cube’s side cannot exceed 1 cm however much area the strip has. Area sets the total; the narrowest dimension sets the bound.

A 12 × 0.5 strip has the same area 6 but cannot make a unit cube at all, because a face needs 1 cm in both directions.

Degenerate and boundary figures

A triangle with sides 1, 2, 3 is flat: 1 + 2 = 3 exactly, so Heron’s expression collapses to 0 and the “triangle” is a segment. A sector of 360° is the whole disc; of 0°, nothing. A cylinder of height 0 is a disc with no volume; of radius 0, a segment with no volume. Each formula above holds at these limits and returns 0, which is the check that the formula has not lost a term.

“Double the side and the area doubles”

Why it tempts: every length doubled, so it feels as if every quantity should double. Why it is wrong: two lengths multiply, so each of them contributes the factor.

Minimal counterexample: a 1×1 square has area 1; a 2×2 square is four unit squares, area 4. Correct rule: lengths k, areas k², volumes k³.

Question that exposes it: Practice Q7. Visual: scene 3 tiles the 2×2 square with four copies.

“Circumference 6, so radius 6”

Why it tempts: both are lengths, and the formula is remembered as “pi r”. Why it is wrong: C = 2πr, so dividing by π alone leaves the radius twice too large.

Minimal counterexample: using r = 6 gives a disc of 36π ≈ 113.1 instead of 9/π ≈ 2.865: about 39 times too large. Correct rule: r = C/(2π).

Question that exposes it: Practice Q4, option B. Visual: scene 4 shows the division by 2π.

“Curved surface and total surface are the same”

Why it tempts: most textbook pictures of a cylinder are drawn closed, so the ends look free. Why it is wrong: the ends are extra area: 2πr².

Minimal counterexample: the 6 × 1 sheet. Curved 6, total if closed 6 + 18/π ≈ 11.73, and the sheet only has 6. Correct rule: decide open or closed first, then choose the formula.

Question that exposes it: Practice Q10. Visual: scene 6 labels the cylinder’s paper as the side only.

“The height of a cone is its slant height”

Why it tempts: the slanted edge is the one drawn prominently, and for a shallow cone the two are close. Why it is wrong: the slant is longer by √(r²+h²), and the curved surface uses the slant.

Minimal counterexample: r=3, h=4: curved surface 15π, not 12π. Correct rule: volume uses h, curved surface uses l.

Question that exposes it: Practice Q5. Visual: the Build-up table prints l = √(r²+h²) beside the formula that needs it.

“The roll direction does not matter”

Why it tempts: the paper is the same, so the area check passes both ways. Why it is wrong: the volume depends on which edge is the circle, and the dependence is quadratic in that edge.

Minimal counterexample: 9/π ≈ 2.865 against 3/(2π) ≈ 0.477, a factor of exactly 6. Correct rule: find the phrase in the question that fixes the seam, then map the edges.

Question that exposes it: Practice Q9. Visual: scene 7 draws both rolls side by side.

“A bigger surface means a bigger volume”

Why it tempts: for a family of similar solids the two do move together. Why it is wrong: between different shapes, no. The open cylinder of 6 cm² holds 2.865; the closed cube of the same 6 cm² holds 1.

Minimal counterexample: the two sheets of the 2021 question. Correct rule: for a fixed surface, the sphere holds the most among closed shapes; an open surface holds nothing until something closes it.

Question that exposes it: Practice Q7, option D. Visual: scene 10 compares the cube and the sphere at the same surface.

The question categories that actually appear

C — calculation (almost every item): one figure, one or two steps, one number. G — misconception trap: the distractors are the standard slips — radius from circumference, area scaled linearly, open treated as closed. H — multi-step: a sheet becomes a solid, then the solid is compared with another. D — construction: the largest cube from a strip, the least number of cuts. B — property verification appears in statement-based MSQs (Practice Q7). Educational interpretation

Categories A (direct definition recall) and E (single counterexample) are rare as standalone GA items; they appear only inside the other four. This page covers C, G, H, D and B for that reason.

Decision procedure for a sheet-to-solid question

  1. Underline the phrase that fixes the seam (“short edges together”).
  2. Write which edge is the circumference and which is the height.
  3. r = circumference / (2π), keep π symbolic.
  4. Choose open or closed; add 2πr² only if closed and only if the paper pays for it.
  5. Check the paper: 2πrh (plus ends) must equal the sheet’s area.
  6. Answer in the form the options use — often a multiple of π, not a decimal.

Keeping π symbolic is what makes 9/π and 3/(2π) distinguishable in an option list.

Decision procedure for a plane-figure question

  1. Name the quantity: boundary, covering or capacity.
  2. Fix one unit for every length.
  3. If a triangle, fix the base and the height perpendicular to it; if the height is not given, get it from the area, from Pythagoras, or from similarity.
  4. If a region is a difference (a path, a frame, a washer), subtract areas, do not add widths.
  5. If a curve is flat-topped, read it as rectangles on the given break points.
  6. Test the answer with the scale law: double a length, the area must quadruple.

Step 4 is where the 44-against-40 mistake lives: four strips miss the four corners.

The five slips that build the distractors

(1) r = C/π instead of C/2π — gives 36π where 9/π was wanted. (2) Linear scaling of an area — gives 12 where 24 was wanted for the doubled 3-4-5. (3) Open treated as closed — adds 2πr² that the paper does not have. (4) The wrong edge as the circle — a factor of 6 here. (5) The wrong height in a triangle or cone — a factor of h/l or of 12/5 against 3. Every one of them appears in the option list of a real question, which is why the options are worth reading before the answer is written down.

Twelve questions, all original

MCQ Original Q1 · basic

A rectangle is 12 cm by 5 cm. Which pair gives its area and its perimeter?

(A) 60 cm² and 34 cm   (B) 60 cm² and 17 cm   (C) 34 cm² and 60 cm   (D) 60 cm² and 34 cm²

Concept tested: area against perimeter, and the unit of each.

Answer: (A). Area = 12 × 5 = 60 cm²; perimeter = 2(12+5) = 34 cm.

(B) is the half-perimeter — the most common slip. (C) swaps the two quantities. (D) gives the perimeter a squared unit, which no length can have.

MCQ Original Q2 · basic

A circular field has circumference 44 cm. Take π = 22/7. Its area is

(A) 7 cm²   (B) 154 cm²   (C) 616 cm²   (D) 44 cm²

Answer: (B). 2πr = 44 ⇒ r = 44 × 7/(2 × 22) = 7 cm, then πr² = (22/7)(49) = 154 cm².

(C) is πr² with r = 14 — the diameter. (A) stops at the radius. (D) repeats the circumference.

NAT Original Q3 · basic

A triangle has sides 13 cm, 14 cm and 15 cm. Its area, in cm², is ______.

Answer to the nearest integer.

Answer: 84. s = 21, A = √(21·8·7·6) = √7056 = 84.

Check by base and height: on base 14 the height is 2(84)/14 = 12, and 13² − 5² = 12² — consistent. A common wrong answer is 91, from using 14 × 13 / 2.

MCQ Original Q4 · application

A 6 cm × 1 cm sheet is rolled into an open cylinder by bringing the short edges together. Its volume is

(A) 9/π cm³   (B) 36π cm³   (C) 3/π cm³   (D) 3/(2π) cm³

Answer: (A). 2πr = 6 ⇒ r = 3/π, h = 1, V = π(3/π)²(1) = 9/π ≈ 2.865.

(B) is the r = 6 slip. (D) is the other roll. (C) is 9/π with the height taken as 1/3.

NAT Original Q5 · application

A cone has base radius 3 cm and vertical height 4 cm. Its total surface area, divided by π, equals ______ cm².

Answer: 24. Slant l = √(9+16) = 5; curved πrl = 15π; base πr² = 9π; total 24π.

Answering 21 is the slant-height slip (using h = 4 in πrl). Answering 12 is the volume divided by π.

MCQ Original Q6 · application

A square garden of side 10 m has a path 1 m wide all round it. The path area is

(A) 40 m²   (B) 44 m²   (C) 21 m²   (D) 121 m²

Answer: (B). 12² − 10² = 144 − 100 = 44.

(A) is four 10×1 strips and misses the four corner squares. (C) is one side’s strip plus a corner. (D) is the whole outer square.

MSQ Original Q7 · conceptual

Which of the following statements are TRUE?

(A) Doubling every edge of a cube multiplies its volume by 8.
(B) Doubling the radius of a circle doubles its area.
(C) For two similar solids, the ratio of volumes is the cube of the ratio of corresponding lengths.
(D) A plane figure with a larger perimeter has a larger area.

Answer: (A) and (C).

(B) is false: the area multiplies by 4. (D) is false, and it is the counterexample of this page: a 4×4 square has perimeter 16 and area 16, while a 1×7 rectangle has the same perimeter 16 and area 7.

This is the item that separates “similar figures” from “arbitrary figures”: (A) and (C) hold inside a family of similar solids, (D) would need a claim across families, and no such claim is true.

MCQ Original Q8 · conceptual

For a sphere of radius R, the numerical value of the volume equals the numerical value of the surface area when

(A) at every radius   (B) only at R = 3   (C) at R = 3 and at R = 0   (D) at no radius

Answer: (C). 4/3πR³ = 4πR² gives R²(R−3) = 0, so R = 0 or R = 3.

(B) is the tempting answer and it is the reason the option is offered: the degenerate case R = 0 is easy to forget. Even at R = 3 the two numbers are equal only because the units differ — 36π cm³ against 36π cm².

MCQ Original Q9 · tricky

The same 6 cm × 1 cm sheet can be rolled two ways. Which roll gives the larger volume, and by what factor?

(A) long edge as the circle, by a factor of 6   (B) short edge as the circle, by a factor of 6   (C) both equal   (D) long edge as the circle, by a factor of 36

Answer: (A). 9/π ≈ 2.865 against 3/(2π) ≈ 0.477; the ratio is exactly 6, the ratio of the two edges.

(D) is the plausible-looking trap: the volume depends on the square of the wrapped edge but also inversely on the height, and 36/6 = 6. Both rolls pass the paper-area check, which is why the area check alone cannot decide.

MCQ Original Q10 · tricky

For the cylinder of Q4, the extra paper needed to close both ends is

(A) 9/π cm²   (B) 18/π cm²   (C) 6 cm²   (D) 36/π cm²

Answer: (B). Two ends, each πr² = 9/π, so 2πr² = 18/π ≈ 5.730 cm².

(A) is one end. (D) is four ends. (C) is the curved surface, which the sheet already provides. The point of the item is that the 2021 question’s “ends closed” cannot be paid for out of the same sheet: 6 + 5.73 exceeds 6.

MCQ Adapted Q11 · tricky

Adapted from the GATE 2022 GA step-curve item: let y = 1 on [0, ½), y = 4 on [½, ¾) and y = 2 on [¾, 1]. The area under the curve is

(A) 1.5   (B) 2   (C) 2.5   (D) 3

What changed: the heights and the first break point; the structure of the question is the original’s.

Answer: (B). Widths ½, ¼, ¼; products 0.5, 1, 0.5; total 2.

(D) comes from using height 4 across the whole interval — the “tallest height everywhere” slip. (C) comes from forgetting that the last width is 1 − 3/4.

NAT Original Q12 · tricky

Among all rectangles with integer sides and perimeter 20 cm, the largest possible area, in cm², is ______.

Answer: 25. a + b = 10, so the pairs are 1×9 (9), 2×8 (16), 3×7 (21), 4×6 (24), 5×5 (25).

The maximum is at the square, and the same holds among real rectangles because a(10−a) peaks at a = 5. Answering 24 is the slip of stopping one case early.

Formulas, with the condition attached

P = 2(a+b) · A = ab · Atri = ½bh · Atrap = ½(a+b)h

C = 2πr · A = πr² · sector πr²θ/360 · ring π(R²−r²)

Vcube = a³, S = 6a² · Vcyl = πr²h, curved 2πrh · Vcone = ⅓πr²h, curved πrl, l = √(r²+h²)

Vsph = 4/3πR³, S = 4πR² · hemisphere 2/3πR³, total 3πR²

Decision procedures

Sheet to cylinder: seam phrase → which edge is the circle → r = C/2π → open or closed → check 2πrh equals the paper.

Sheet to cube: 6a² equals the paper → a → check the strip is wide enough for a.

Path or frame: outer area minus inner area, never width times perimeter.

Flat-topped curve: rectangles on the given break points, widths from subtracting ends.

Traps, each with its defusing number

Linear scaling of an area: 1×1 → 2×2 is 4, not 2.

Radius from circumference: C=6 gives r = 3/π ≈ 0.955, not 6.

Open treated as closed: 6 against 11.73 cm² on the same sheet.

Slant against height: 15π against 12π at r=3, h=4.

Roll direction: 2.865 against 0.477, factor 6.

Bigger boundary, bigger area: 4×4 (16, 16) against 1×7 (16, 7).
