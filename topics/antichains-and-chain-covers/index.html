Topic and objective

TOPIC: Antichains, chains and the width of a partial order — the largest set of mutually incomparable elements, the fewest chains that cover the poset, and the two theorems that make those numbers equal to the quantities on the other side: Dilworth’s theorem and Mirsky’s theorem. One section also covers the widest antichain of a power set (Sperner) and the matching that computes a minimum chain cover.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a finite poset, compute its width and its height, produce a partition into as many antichains as the height and a cover by as many chains as the width, and say which theorem licenses each. Recognise the two previous-year shapes: a question that gives the longest chain and asks for antichains (Mirsky) and a question that gives an antichain and asks for chains (Dilworth). Refuse the three shortcuts that circulate — width equals height, the widest level is the width, a greedy chain cover is minimum.

GATE RELEVANCE: Class B — a prerequisite technique for clauses the 2027 syllabus names. Section 1 names partial orders and lattices and, separately, graphs: connectivity, matching, colouring; the chain cover here is a maximum matching in a bipartite graph, and the antichain is an independent set of a comparability graph, so the lesson sits exactly on the junction of those two clauses. Two previous-year items were verified this run and are worked on the GATE patterns tab.

PREREQUISITES: a partial order, comparability and the Hasse diagram (Partial orders, Hasse diagrams and lattices); bipartite graphs and maximum matching (Matching and colouring); the binomial coefficient (Counting and combinatorics).

Prerequisites, precisely

Must already be comfortable with:

  • A partial order as a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive, and its Hasse diagram.
  • Comparable and incomparable: one of the two comes before the other, or neither does.
  • A bipartite graph and a matching: a set of edges with no shared endpoint.
  • The binomial coefficient as a count of choices, and factorial notation.

Helpful, not required: having met the power set of a set ordered by inclusion, which is the poset behind most subset questions.

Approximate learning time: 45–60 minutes with the animation, or 10 minutes for the Revision tab alone. The figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. An antichain is a set of mutually incomparable elements; a chain is a set of mutually comparable ones. They share at most one element, and that single fact proves every lower bound here.
  2. Width is the largest antichain, height is the longest chain. Either can exceed the other, so neither bounds the other.
  3. Mirsky: the minimum number of antichains covering a poset equals its height. The levels are the construction.
  4. Dilworth: the minimum number of chains covering a poset equals its width. A maximum matching in the split graph is the construction.
  5. The widest level is not the width, and a greedy chain cover is not minimum. Both are refuted here by computed counterexamples, not by assertion.

Legend: green marks a value the page computed and checked; amber marks a set the page is highlighting; red marks a refuted claim, and the refutations are the teaching.

Intuition: a project plan and its machines

A project plan is a partial order: some tasks must precede others, and most pairs are free. Two questions follow immediately, and they are different questions.

How many machines do I need so that nothing ever waits? Only mutually independent tasks can run at the same time, and a set of mutually independent tasks is exactly an antichain. So the answer is the largest antichain — the width.

How few crews can I form so that each crew does one task at a time and every task is done? A crew working through a sequence of tasks is exactly a chain. So the answer is the fewest chains covering the plan.

Dilworth’s theorem says those two numbers are equal. That is not obvious from the plan, and it is the reason the topic is examinable.

Educational interpretation The analogy is exact about structure — antichain as parallel work, chain as sequential work — and loose about cost, since real tasks have durations. Nothing here models durations.

Formal definitions

Let $$P$$ be a finite set with a partial order $$\le$$. Write $$x < y$$ when $$x \le y$$ and $$x \ne y$$.

Chain: a subset in which every two elements are comparable. Antichain: a subset in which no two distinct elements are comparable. Both are non-empty by convention here; the empty set is an antichain of size zero and is counted only where a count of all antichains is asked for.

Height is the size of a largest chain. Width is the size of a largest antichain.

Chain cover: a family of chains whose union is the whole set. Antichain partition: a family of pairwise disjoint antichains whose union is the whole set.

$$\text{width}(P) \;=\; \min\{\,|\mathcal{C}| : \mathcal{C} \text{ a chain cover}\,\} \qquad \text{height}(P) \;=\; \min\{\,|\mathcal{A}| : \mathcal{A} \text{ an antichain partition}\,\}$$

The left equality is Dilworth’s theorem (1950); the right one is Mirsky’s (1971). Both hold for every finite poset, and neither is an inequality that sometimes fails.

Mathematical derivation The easy direction of both: a chain meets an antichain in at most one element, since two shared elements would have to be comparable and incomparable at once. So a chain of size $$h$$ needs $$h$$ antichains, and an antichain of size $$w$$ needs $$w$$ chains. Both theorems say that bound is attained.

Level 1–3: the easy bound, then Mirsky

Give each element $$x$$ the number $$1 + $$ the length of the longest chain that ends at $$x$$. Call it the level of $$x$$. The page computes it by a pass over the arrows, and the largest level is the height.

Two elements of the same level are never comparable: if $$x < y$$ then any chain ending at $$x$$ extends to one ending at $$y$$, so $$y$$ has the larger level. So each level is an antichain, the levels partition the set, and there are exactly as many of them as the height.

That is Mirsky’s theorem in full, and its proof is one paragraph. The lower bound came from the pigeonhole step above, so the two directions meet and the theorem is proved.

Mathematical derivation Nothing is quoted here: the argument is complete as written, and the page checks it numerically on every poset it draws — each level tested to be an antichain, the number of levels compared with the computed height.

Dilworth, and what makes it hard

The antichain gives the lower bound in one line. The upper bound — that $$w$$ chains always suffice — is the hard direction, and no picture of the poset makes it visible.

The construction. Take two copies of the element set, a left copy and a right copy, and draw an edge from the left copy of $$x$$ to the right copy of $$y$$ whenever $$x < y$$. The result is a bipartite graph. Take a maximum matching, follow the matched links, and you get a chain cover of size $$n - \nu$$, where $$\nu$$ is the matching size. König’s theorem on the same graph produces an antichain of exactly that size.

$$\text{width}(P) \;=\; n \;-\; \nu(P)$$

Here $$n$$ is the number of elements and $$\nu$$ the maximum matching in the split graph. The page checks this equality on 220 seeded random posets, and checks that every chain it produces really is a chain.

Verified fact The reduction to bipartite matching is the standard one; the maximum-matching algorithm itself is the matching lesson’s topic, not this one’s. Nothing here depends on which augmenting-path algorithm you use.

The power set: Sperner, and the middle level

Order the subsets of an $$n$$-element set by inclusion. A chain here is a family in which every two sets are comparable; an antichain is a family in which no set contains another.

Sets of the same size are never comparable, so each size level is an antichain of size $$\binom{n}{k}$$. Sperner’s theorem says the widest of those levels is the widest possible antichain:

$$\text{width}\big(2^{[n]}\big) \;=\; \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$$

The page checks it by brute force for $$n = 1, 2, 3, 4$$: widths 1, 2, 3, 6 against binomials 1, 2, 3, 6. Four cases are a check, not a proof.

For the same poset the height is $$n+1$$, because a chain of distinct subsets has strictly increasing sizes and sizes run from $$0$$ to $$n$$. That is the arithmetic of the 2005 item on the GATE patterns tab.

Educational interpretation The power set is the one poset where the widest level really is the width. Generalising from it is the trap the eighth scene breaks.

Graded posets, and why the levels mislead

A poset is graded when a rank function rises by exactly one on every cover relation, all minimal elements sit at rank zero and all maximal elements at the same top rank. Every Boolean lattice is graded, which is why “the levels” feel like the natural decomposition.

A graded poset need not have the Sperner property: the widest level need not be a maximum antichain. The page builds a seven-element graded poset whose levels have sizes 1, 3 and 3 while its width is 4, and it finds the antichain by testing all 128 subsets.

What the levels do give, always and without a hypothesis, is Mirsky’s partition: an antichain partition whose size is the height.

Mathematical derivation The seven-element example is computed, not recalled: gradedness is tested rank by rank on every cover, the levels are read off the rank function, and the width comes from exhaustive search. The two numbers disagree, which is the whole point.

Counting antichains is a third question

“How many antichains does this poset have?” is not the width and not the height. In the power set of an $$n$$-element set that count is the Dedekind number, and the page computes it: 2, 3, 6, 20 and 168 for $$n = 0$$ to $$4$$.

For the power set of three elements there are twenty antichains in all, but only two of size three: the three singletons, and the three two-element subsets. Both are maximum; the other eighteen are smaller.

So a question that says “maximum”, “largest” or “width” wants one number, and a question that says “how many” wants another. The page keeps the two apart on every tab.

Example 1 — the opening poset, both numbers

Elements $$A, B, C, D, E$$ with $$A < B$$, $$A < C$$, $$B < D$$, $$C < D$$, and $$E$$ comparable with nothing.

  1. Chains: $$A, B, D$$ has size three, and so does $$A, C, D$$. Nothing longer exists, so the height is 3.
  2. Antichains: $$B, C, E$$ is pairwise incomparable, so the width is at least three.
  3. Can it be four? Adding $$A$$ or $$D$$ to that set breaks it, since $$A$$ is below $$B$$ and $$D$$ is above $$B$$. So the width is 3.
  4. The chain cover $$\{A, B, D\}, \{C\}, \{E\}$$ uses three chains, which meets the antichain. Answer proved without quoting a theorem.

Width and height both happen to be three here. The page says so in the animation rather than letting the coincidence pass as a rule.

Example 2 — the divisors of 60

Twelve divisors of 60 under divisibility. All four numbers below are computed by the page, and the width by testing all 4096 subsets.

The widest level here is the middle one, with four elements, and it does equal the width. That is a fact about this poset, not a method: the graded counterexample on the Edge cases tab has a widest level of three and a width of four.

Four chains, all twelve divisors, and four is the width: the cover is optimal and it is exhibited, so nothing is being inferred from a formula.

Example 3 — the power set of three elements

Eight subsets under inclusion. The page enumerates all 256 subsets of the eight elements to find the antichains.

  1. Size levels have sizes 1, 3, 3, 1. So the width is at least 3.
  2. Testing every antichain shows no antichain of size 4 exists, so the width is 3 — which is $$\binom{3}{1}$$, as Sperner predicts.
  3. The height is 4, from $$\emptyset < \{a\} < \{a,b\} < \{a,b,c\}$$ — that is $$n+1$$ for $$n = 3$$.
  4. There are 20 antichains in all, and exactly 2 of size 3: the three singletons and the three pairs.

For four elements the same computation gives width 6 and 168 antichains, so both sequences grow fast; the width grows like $$\binom{n}{n/2}$$ and the count like a Dedekind number.

Example 4 — the matching, step by step

Six elements: the opening poset plus an extra pair $$E < F$$. The split graph has twelve vertices and an edge for every strict comparison.

  1. Edges go $$A \to B, A \to C, A \to D, B \to D, C \to D, E \to F$$, taking the transitive ones too.
  2. A maximum matching pairs three of them, using each element at most once: the page’s matching has size 3, and no element appears as both a source and a target of two matched edges.
  3. Chains start at elements that are not the target of a matched edge. Following the links gives $$\{A, B, D\}, \{C\}, \{E, F\}$$.
  4. Count: $$6 - 3 = 3$$ chains, which equals the width, so the cover is optimal.

Step 2 is the one place a hand computation goes wrong: a matching may not reuse an element. The page enforces that by construction, and its cover is checked to contain each element exactly once.

The empty case matters in one exam sense only: a question about a non-empty poset never needs it, and a question about all subsets does, because the empty set is a subset and the empty antichain is an antichain.

Whether the empty antichain counts is a convention, and it changes the Dedekind count by one. The page counts it, which is why the sequence starts at 2 and not 1.

Counterexample 1 — a graded poset whose widest level is not the width

Seven elements with ranks 0, 1, 2 of sizes 1, 3, 3. Every maximal chain has length three, so the poset is graded. Its levels are 1, 3 and 3.

Its width is 4: the two middle-rank elements $$B$$ and $$C$$, together with $$F$$ and $$G$$ from the top rank, are pairwise incomparable. No level contains all four, so no level is a maximum antichain.

The widest-level rule would answer 3, and the correct answer is 4.

Mathematical derivation Gradedness was tested on every cover relation, the rank function was computed, and the width came from testing all 128 subsets. The disagreement is a computed fact, not an illustration.

Counterexample 2 — the greedy cover overpays

Seven elements, width 4. Repeatedly taking a maximal chain from the smallest available minimal element gives $$A, D$$; then $$B, E$$; then $$C$$; then $$F$$; then $$G$$ — five chains.

A minimum cover has 4 chains, and the page exhibits one. So the greedy answer is an upper bound that is not tight, and the gap of one is measured rather than asserted.

The reason is the usual one: taking the longest chain first consumes elements that a better cover needed. Here $$A$$ is used with $$D$$, which leaves $$C$$ to sit alone.

A greedy cover is a proof that the width is at most five. Only a cover that meets an antichain of four settles the width at four.

Counterexample 3 — maximal is not maximum

In the opening poset, $$\{A, E\}$$ is a maximal antichain: the two are incomparable, and no third element can be added, because $$A$$ is below $$B$$, $$C$$ and $$D$$.

It has size 2, while the width is 3. So a maximal antichain can be strictly smaller than a maximum one, and a question that says “maximal” is not asking for the width.

The same distinction applies to chains: a maximal chain (one that cannot be extended) need not be longest, and the height is the length of the longest one.

In a finite poset a maximum antichain is always maximal; the converse fails, and this two-element set is the smallest failure.

“Dilworth says width equals height”

Wrong. Dilworth pairs the largest antichain with the smallest chain cover. Mirsky pairs the longest chain with the smallest antichain cover. The two theorems are duals, and neither says width equals height.

Refutation in one line: a chain of four has width 1 and height 4.

The wording of the question is the signal: it names the object you are given, and the object you are asked for. Given a chain, answer with antichains (Mirsky). Given an antichain, answer with chains (Dilworth).

“The widest level is the width”

True for a power set, false in general. It holds for every Boolean lattice by Sperner’s theorem, and it fails on the seven-element graded poset on the Edge cases tab, where the widest level has three elements and the width is four.

Why it feels true: every example in a first course is a power set or a divisor lattice, and both are Sperner posets.

The levels are a valid construction for Mirsky and for nothing else. Using them for the width is the single most common error on this topic.

“Any chain cover that looks tight is minimum”

Not without an antichain to match it. A cover of $$k$$ chains proves width $$\le k$$. It proves width $$= k$$ only when you also exhibit an antichain of size $$k$$.

Refutation: the greedy cover on the seven-element poset gives five chains and the width is four.

This is the cheapest habit in the topic: quote the antichain and the cover together, and the answer is proved without appealing to Dilworth at all.

“A chain cover must be a partition, so the two theorems differ in kind”

They do not. For both objects, a cover can always be trimmed to a partition with no more parts: a subset of a chain is a chain, and a subset of an antichain is an antichain.

So “cover” and “partition” give the same minimum in both theorems, and a question may use either word.

The one place the words matter is the 1991 item, where the blank is partition: the levels are disjoint, so the construction is a partition and not merely a cover.

“An antichain is an independent set, so the width is the independence number of any graph”

Half true. An antichain is an independent set of the comparability graph, and a chain is a clique of it. So Dilworth is the statement that independence number equals clique cover number for comparability graphs.

That equality is not a theorem about graphs in general: a five-cycle has independence number 2 and clique cover number 3. Comparability graphs are perfect, which is exactly what makes the poset case work.

Keep the direction: poset statement true, general graph statement false, and the odd cycle of length five is the counterexample to remember.

“How many antichains equals the width”

Two different questions. The width is the size of the largest antichain; the count of antichains is how many antichains exist at all sizes.

For the power set of three elements: width 3, but 20 antichains in all and only 2 of size 3. For four elements: width 6 and 168 antichains.

The count of antichains in a power set is the Dedekind number, and it is not on the GATE syllabus as a formula; it appears here only so the two questions cannot be confused.

The two verified previous-year items

Verified fact Both are reproduced from public question archives that quote the paper wording. The original paper scans were not consulted this run, so the wording is as the archive gives it.

GATE CSE 1991, Question 1(xiv) (fill in the blank)

“If the longest chain in a partial order is of length n, then the partial order can be written as a ____ of n antichains.”

Answer: partition. This is Mirsky’s theorem stated as a fact. The levels are disjoint and there are as many as the height, so the construction is a partition; no smaller number works because a chain of $$n$$ elements needs $$n$$ antichains.

Worked on the divisors of 30 in the animation: height 4, partition $$\{1\}, \{2,3,5\}, \{6,10,15\}, \{30\}$$.

GATE IT 2005, Question 33 (MCQ; some archives file it under the CSE paper, and the numbering could not be confirmed)

“Let A be a set with n elements. Let C be a collection of distinct subsets of A such that for any two subsets S1 and S2 in C, either S1 ⊂ S2 or S2 ⊂ S1. What is the maximum cardinality of C?”

Answer: n + 1. The collection is a chain in the power set, so the question asks for the height. A chain of distinct subsets has strictly increasing sizes, and sizes run from 0 to n, so at most $$n+1$$; the chain of initial segments attains it.

The distractor $$2^{n-1}+1$$ is the answer to a different question: the largest family with no two disjoint sets.

The right-hand column is the whole of the classification. Reading the question into one of those eight rows is faster than computing anything.

The decision procedure

  1. Name the object. Which of the eight rows above does the question fall in?
  2. Draw or describe the poset. Cover relations only, and remember the implied ones.
  3. Exhibit a lower bound: an antichain for the width, a chain for the height.
  4. Exhibit an upper bound: chains for the width, antichains (levels) for the height.
  5. If they meet, stop. The answer is proved and no theorem has to be quoted.
  6. If the poset is large, use the split graph: width $$= n - $$ maximum matching.

Steps 3 and 4 are also the check: a width that is wrong by one shows up immediately as a cover one short or an antichain one long.

Traps in the wording

  • Maximal against maximum. “Maximal antichain” may be smaller than the width; only “largest” or “maximum” is the width.
  • Chain against antichain. The 1991 wording gives a chain and asks for antichains; the 2005 wording gives comparability and asks for a family of subsets.
  • Cover against partition. Same minimum in both theorems; the 1991 blank wants the word partition.
  • Subset against proper subset. Strict inclusion makes the family a chain of distinct sizes; allowing equality would let a set be comparable with itself and inflate the count.
  • Counting against maximising. “How many antichains” is a different answer from “how big is the largest”.

None of these is a matter of opinion: each is decided by the definition on the Build-up tab.

Q1 — the opening poset

Elements $$A, B, C, D, E$$ with $$A < B$$, $$A < C$$, $$B < D$$, $$C < D$$, and $$E$$ incomparable with all four others. The width of this poset is

(a) 2   (b) 3   (c) 4   (d) 5

Answer: (b) 3

$$B$$, $$C$$ and $$E$$ are pairwise incomparable, so the width is at least 3. Any set of four must add $$A$$ or $$D$$ to that triple, and $$A$$ is below $$B$$ while $$D$$ is above $$B$$, so no antichain of size four exists.

(a) is the size of a largest antichain if you forget $$E$$. (c) is the number of elements at the middle of the picture, which is not a poset invariant. (d) is $$n$$, the count when nothing is comparable.

The page confirms 3 by testing all 32 subsets, and the chain cover $$\{A,B,D\}, \{C\}, \{E\}$$ matches it.

NAT original

Q2 — the height of a divisor lattice

The twelve divisors of 60 are ordered by divisibility. The height of this poset is ______.

Answer: 5

A chain is a sequence where each divides the next, so its length is bounded by the number of prime factors of 60, counted with multiplicity, plus one. $$60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$$ has four prime factors, so the longest chain has $$4 + 1 = 5$$ elements: $$1 < 2 < 4 < 12 < 60$$.

Common wrong answers: 12, the number of elements; 4, the number of prime factors, which forgets the 1 at the bottom; and 3, the number of distinct primes.

The page computes 5 by a pass over the arrows and reports the levels as 1, 3, 4, 3, 1.

MCQ original

Q3 — the closely confused pair

For the divisors of 60 under divisibility, what are the minimum number of chains needed to cover the poset, and the minimum number of antichains?

(a) 4 and 4   (b) 4 and 5   (c) 5 and 4   (d) 5 and 5

Answer: (b) 4 and 5

Chains: Dilworth says the minimum equals the width, which is 4, attained by $$\{4, 6, 10, 15\}$$. A cover of four exists: $$1,2,4,12,60$$; $$3,6,30$$; $$5,10,20$$; $$15$$.

Antichains: Mirsky says the minimum equals the height, which is 5. The levels give it directly.

(a) assumes the two theorems name the same number. (c) swaps them, which is the mistake of pairing a chain with chains. (d) uses the height for the chains as well.

The pair to keep apart: chains are constrained by the largest antichain, antichains by the longest chain.

MSQ original

Q4 — which statements hold for every finite poset

Select every statement that is true for every finite non-empty poset.

(a) The width is at most the height.
(b) A chain and an antichain share at most one element.
(c) The minimum number of chains covering the poset equals the width.
(d) The minimum number of antichains covering the poset equals the height.
(e) The widest level of the poset is a maximum antichain.
(f) The width times the height is at least the number of elements.

Answer: (b), (c), (d), (f)

(b) is the pigeonhole fact behind every lower bound here. (c) is Dilworth. (d) is Mirsky. (f) holds because the height layers are antichains of size at most the width.

(a) fails on a chain of four: width 1, height 4. (e) fails on the seven-element graded poset on the Edge cases tab: widest level 3, width 4.

(f) is an inequality and not an equality: five elements with width 3 and height 3 give 9.

NAT original

Q5 — a power set, by Sperner

The subsets of a four-element set are ordered by inclusion. The size of the largest family of mutually incomparable subsets is ______.

Answer: 6

Sperner: the answer is $$\binom{4}{2} = 6$$, the two-element subsets. The level sizes are 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, so the middle level is the largest.

Common wrong answers: 4, the number of singletons or of three-element subsets; 8, which doubles the middle level for no reason; 16, the number of subsets, which is the size of the poset and not of an antichain.

The page confirms 6 by testing all 65,536 subsets of the sixteen elements.

The subsets of a three-element set are ordered by inclusion. The total number of antichains in this poset, counting the empty one, is ______.

Answer: 20

Count by size, over the eight subsets: the empty antichain (1); antichains of one set (8, one per subset); pairs of incomparable sets (9); maximum antichains of size 3 (2). Total $$1 + 8 + 9 + 2 = 20$$.

That is the Dedekind number for three elements; the sequence is 2, 3, 6, 20, 168 for zero to four elements, and the page computes all five by testing every subset.

A count like this is not worth deriving by cases in an exam; the point is that it is not the width (which is 3) and not the number of maximum antichains (which is 2).

Q7 — the counterexample

A poset has a bottom element $$A$$, three middle elements $$B, C, D$$ and three top elements $$E, F, G$$, with $$A$$ below all six, $$B$$ and $$C$$ below $$E$$, and $$D$$ below $$F$$ and $$G$$. Every maximal chain has length three. Its width is

(a) 2   (b) 3   (c) 4   (d) 7

Answer: (c) 4

$$B, C, F, G$$ are pairwise incomparable, so the width is at least 4. No antichain of size 5 exists, which the page confirms by testing all 128 subsets.

(b) is the widest level, and it is the trap: the poset is graded, so the levels look authoritative, but a maximum antichain can straddle two ranks.

The general lesson: graded does not imply Sperner. Only the Boolean lattice is guaranteed to have its width on a level, and even that is a theorem, not a definition.

Q8 — the greedy cover, and what it proves

A seven-element poset has width 4. A greedy procedure that repeatedly takes a maximal chain produces five chains. Which statements are true?

(a) The greedy result proves the width is at most 5.
(b) The greedy result proves the width is exactly 5.
(c) A minimum chain cover has 4 chains.
(d) If a cover of 4 chains and an antichain of 4 elements are both exhibited, the width is proved to be 4.
(e) The greedy cover is minimum because each chain it takes is maximal.

Answer: (a), (c), (d)

(a) is what a cover always gives: five chains covering the poset is a proof that no antichain has six elements. (c) follows from Dilworth and from the exhibited four-chain cover. (d) is the matching-bounds argument, which needs no theorem.

(b) confuses an upper bound with the value. (e) is the mistake: taking maximal chains is a choice about the cover, and a different cover does better.

Every one of these numbers is computed on the animation tab: the greedy cover costs 5, the matching finds 4, and the antichain $$\{C, D, F, G\}$$ has size 4.

The procedure in one place

  1. Classify the question into one of the eight rows on the GATE patterns tab.
  2. Exhibit a lower bound: an antichain for the width, a chain for the height.
  3. Exhibit an upper bound: chains for the width, the levels for the height.
  4. Stop when they meet, and say which theorem the meeting illustrates.
  5. For a large poset, take the split graph and a maximum matching: width $$= n - \nu$$.

The two exhibits double as a check on any computed answer.

Traps, in one line each

  • Width equals height: false, and a chain of four refutes it.
  • The widest level is the width: true only for Sperner posets, false for the seven-element graded example.
  • A maximal antichain is maximum: false, $$\{A, E\}$$ has size 2 against a width of 3.
  • A greedy chain cover is minimum: false, 5 against 4.
  • Independence equals clique cover in any graph: false; true for comparability graphs.
  • How many antichains equals the width: false, 20 against 3.

Each of these is refuted with a computed counterexample on the Edge cases or Misconceptions tab.

Mathematical references

  • Dilworth’s theorem — the statement that the largest antichain and the smallest chain decomposition of a finite poset have the same size, published by R. P. Dilworth in 1950, with the width as the common value. Supports the statement, the name and the date.
  • Mirsky’s theorem — the dual statement: the largest chain and the smallest antichain partition have the same size, with the height-layer proof. Supports the statement and the construction used in the animation.
  • Sperner’s theorem — the widest antichain in the power set of an n-element set is a middle level, of size $$\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$$. Supports the width formula checked here for n = 1 to 4.
  • Lecture notes on posets and chain partitions from university combinatorics courses (Georgia Tech Math 3012 and an MIT combinatorial-optimisation chapter among them) — used for the standard proof of both theorems and for the matching formulation of the Dilworth problem.

Mathematical derivation The layer proof of Mirsky, the split-graph reduction, the product bound and the height of the power set are all derived on the Build-up tab and then checked numerically: every width is computed by exhaustive search over all subsets and, where a second method applies, by a maximum matching, and the two are compared on 220 seeded random posets.
