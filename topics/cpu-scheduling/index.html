Topic and objective

TOPIC: CPU scheduling — the algorithms that decide which ready process runs next, the five quantities a schedule is scored on, and the trade between them. This is the "CPU and I/O scheduling" clause of Section 8 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus. The CPU half is here; the disk half (FCFS, SSTF, SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK) is a separate lesson and is still recorded as uncovered.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a process table, draw the Gantt chart for FCFS, non-preemptive SJF, SRTF, round robin with a stated quantum, or preemptive and non-preemptive priority; read completion times off it; compute average turnaround, waiting and response time; and say which algorithm optimises which metric and what it costs. Given a switch cost and a quantum, compute the fraction of CPU time lost.

I read this topic as one decision, five numbers. The scheduler makes a single choice — who runs now — and every question in the topic is either "what does your rule choose" or "which number does your rule improve". The algorithms differ only in what they are allowed to look at: arrival order, burst length, remaining time, priority, or recent behaviour.

Prerequisites

  • The process states, and the ready queue. Ready means runnable and waiting for the CPU; blocked means waiting for an event. Built in Processes, Threads and IPC.
  • The context switch as pure overhead. Save one context, load another. Same lesson, scene 3.
  • Arithmetic with averages. A mean is a sum over the number of processes in the set.
  • Reading a time axis. A Gantt chart is a bar per run, laid against a time axis; the left edge of a bar is a start time and the right edge is a completion.

Nothing from Computer Organization is assumed beyond "the CPU executes one instruction stream at a time". Where a question needs the hardware to preempt — a timer interrupt — that is stated rather than assumed.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. Turnaround is completion minus arrival; waiting is turnaround minus the burst; response is the first slice minus arrival. Waiting and turnaround differ by a constant, so one is a proxy for the other.
  2. FCFS is arrival order, never preempts, and never starves. Its failure is the convoy effect: waiting equals the sum of the bursts ahead of you.
  3. SJF minimises average waiting among non-preemptive schedules that respect arrivals — by an exchange argument, and only if the bursts are known. SRTF is the preemptive version and can starve long jobs.
  4. Round robin optimises response time, not waiting time. A quantum at least as long as the longest burst makes it exactly FCFS; a very small quantum is dominated by switch overhead.
  5. Priority scheduling is the one that starves; aging fixes it and degrades the policy towards round robin. Feedback queues buy response time at the cost of average waiting.

Intuition: one counter, many customers

A shop has one counter and a queue. The assistant serves whoever is at the front until that customer is done. That is first come first served, and it has an obvious flaw: if the first customer has a trolley of sixty items, the person behind with a single loaf waits for the trolley.

Now give the assistant a rule: look at the baskets, serve the small ones first. The average wait drops, and the trolley owner — who was served first a moment ago — now waits a long time. That is shortest job first, and it is the same trade, made explicit. Give the assistant a timer instead: serve each customer for two minutes, then send them to the back. Nobody waits long before being served at all, and everybody finishes later on average. That is round robin.

Educational interpretation The analogy fixes one thing: a scheduling policy is a rule for choosing, and the choice always trades one customer's wait against another's. It says nothing about preemption cost or about how a burst length is estimated, which are separate ideas below.

Formal definition: the CPU scheduler

A process in the ready queue is runnable and not running. The CPU can run one at a time. The CPU scheduler (short-term scheduler) is the function that selects one ready process whenever the CPU becomes free: at a completion, at a block, at a preemption, or at the arrival of a process that outranks the running one.

Non-preemptive (cooperative): a running process keeps the CPU until it blocks or terminates. Preemptive: the system may take the CPU away, either on a timer or when a higher-priority process arrives.

Dispatch is the act of handing the CPU to the chosen process; the dispatcher does the context switch. The time it takes is dispatch latency, and it is overhead, not work.

Formal definition: the five metrics

For a process with arrival time $$A$$, burst time $$B$$ and completion time $$C$$:

turnaround T = C − A

The whole time the process existed in the system, from arrival to finish.

waiting W = T − B = C − A − B

The time it spent ready. It excludes the burst and excludes time spent blocked.

response R = first service − A

How long before it ran at all. For a non-preemptive algorithm with no idle gaps, R equals W.

CPU utilisation is busy time over total time; throughput is completions per unit time. Both are system-wide, while T, W and R are per process and then averaged.

The identity that makes the topic tractable

Sum the turnaround formula over all processes: the sum of T equals the sum of C minus the sum of A. The arrivals are fixed by the problem and the bursts are fixed by the processes, so on a given process set the sum of waiting times and the sum of turnaround times differ by the constant sum of the bursts.

sum(T) = sum(W) + sum(B)

So "minimise average turnaround" and "minimise average waiting" are the same instruction. It does not follow that they are the same as "minimise average response": that one is a different question, and it is the question round robin answers.

The policies, each defined by what it is allowed to look at

Every algorithm below is the same loop — pick one ready process — with a different input. That is the whole taxonomy, and it is why the questions are answerable from a single table of rules.

FCFS

Looks at: arrival order. Preempts: no. Runs each process to completion, in the order it joined the ready queue. Waiting time of a process is the sum of the bursts of everyone ahead of it, which is the convoy effect. Starvation: impossible, because the queue advances.

On the set used throughout this lesson — A(0,10), B(2,6), C(4,3), D(6,7) — the order is A, B, C, D and the average waiting time is 8.25.

SJF and SRTF

SJF looks at: burst length among those that have arrived. Preempts: no. SRTF is the same rule on remaining time, and it preempts at arrivals. Both minimise average waiting (SRTF among preemptive schedules too), and both starve long jobs.

On the same set: SJF gives A, C, B, D with average waiting 7.5; SRTF gives six segments with average waiting 6.0 and average response 1.25.

Round robin

Looks at: arrival order and a fixed quantum q. Preempts: yes, on the timer. A process that exhausts its slice rejoins the tail of the ready queue. It optimises response time, not waiting time.

On the same set with q = 4: eight segments, average waiting 10.75, average response 3.75. With q ≥ 10 the chart is identical to FCFS.

Priority, with and without preemption

Looks at: a priority number. Whether a larger number is "higher" is stated by the question, not assumed. Preemptive priority takes the CPU at once when a higher-priority process arrives; non-preemptive priority waits for the running one to finish.

On P1(0,8,p2), P2(1,3,p1), P3(2,2,p3): preemptive gives P1, P3, P1, P2 with average waiting 3.67; non-preemptive gives P1, P3, P2 with 5.00.

Multilevel feedback queue

Looks at: recent behaviour. Several queues with increasing quanta; a new process enters the top; a process that uses its whole slice is demoted, one that blocks early stays. The bottom queue is usually FCFS so that every process eventually finishes.

On the same set with quanta 2, 4, then FCFS: average waiting 11.5, and a response time of 0 for every process.

What "optimal" means here, precisely

SJF is optimal for average waiting time, among non-preemptive schedules, on a fixed set of processes with known bursts, with arrivals respected. It is not optimal for response time, not optimal for the worst-case process, and its guarantee disappears the moment bursts must be predicted. A claim of optimality without those four conditions is a different, false claim.

One process set, five algorithms

Every worked example on this page uses the same four processes, so the numbers are comparable: A (arrival 0, burst 10), B (2, 6), C (4, 3), D (6, 7). Total burst is 26 units. Each row below was produced by the simulation on the Animation tab, not by hand.

Example 1 — FCFS

Order A, B, C, D. The chart is A 0–10, B 10–16, C 16–19, D 19–26.

Waiting: A 0, B 8, C 12, D 13. Sum 33, average 8.25. Turnaround: 10, 14, 15, 20; sum 59, average 14.75. Check the identity: 59 = 33 + 26.

C needs three units and waits twelve. That factor of four is the convoy effect, and it is caused entirely by A's ten units.

Example 2 — non-preemptive SJF

At t = 0 only A has arrived, so A runs 0–10. Then the shortest of B (6), C (3), D (7) is C: 10–13. Then B 13–19, then D 19–26.

Waiting: A 0, B 11, C 6, D 13. Sum 30, average 7.5. Turnaround average 14.0.

B is worse off than under FCFS (8 → 11) and C is much better off (12 → 6). The average fell, which is all SJF promises.

Example 3 — SRTF

A runs 0–2. At 2, B arrives with 6 against A's 8, so B runs 2–4. At 4, C arrives with 3 against B's 4, so C runs 4–7 and finishes. B resumes 7–11. D runs 11–18. A resumes 18–26.

Waiting: A 16, B 3, C 0, D 5. Sum 24, average 6.0. Response: 0, 0, 0, 5; average 1.25.

Six segments, and the last one is the process that started first. That sentence is the whole cost of preemption.

Example 4 — round robin, q = 4

A 0–4 (6 left), B 4–8 (2 left), C 8–11 (done), A 11–15 (2 left), D 15–19 (3 left), B 19–21 (done), A 21–23 (done), D 23–26 (done).

Waiting: A 13, B 13, C 4, D 12. Sum 43, average 10.75. Response: 0, 2, 4, 9; average 3.75.

Judged on waiting it is the worst of the four; judged on response it is better than FCFS by more than half. Both statements are true of the same schedule.

Example 5 — the degenerate quantum

Take the same round robin with q = 10. No process needs more than ten units in one slice except A, which needs exactly ten, so nothing is ever cut off. The chart becomes A 0–10, B 10–16, C 16–19, D 19–26.

That is the FCFS chart, and the average waiting time is 8.25. The simulation checks the equality for q = 10 and q = 20. So "round robin with a large quantum" and "FCFS" are the same schedule, which is a statement about a limit, not a coincidence.

Example 6 — priority, both ways

P1 (arrival 0, burst 8, priority 2), P2 (1, 3, 1), P3 (2, 2, 3). Larger number is higher priority, as stated here.

Preemptive: P1 0–2, then P3 preempts 2–4 and finishes, P1 resumes 4–10, P2 10–13. Waiting: P1 2, P2 9, P3 0. Average 3.67.

Non-preemptive: P1 0–8, P3 8–10, P2 10–13. Waiting: 0, 9, 6. Average 5.00. Same priorities, same processes, a different chart, because "preemptive" is a rule about events.

Example 7 — a full previous-year item, worked from the table

Verified by recomputation GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q37. Four processes A (0, 10), B (2, 6), C (4, 3), D (6, 7) — the same set used throughout this lesson. The question asks which option gives the average waiting times under preemptive SRTF and non-preemptive SJF. SRTF gives 6 and NP-SJF gives 7.5, so the option "SRTF = 6, NP-SJF = 7.5" is correct. The two simulations on this page produce exactly those numbers, independently of any answer key.

Example 8 — round robin by transition counting

GATE CSE 2022, Q42. Four processes P, Q, R, S arrive together in that order; the quantum is 4. The question states that there is exactly one switch S→Q, exactly one R→Q and exactly two Q→R, and no S→P, then asks which burst table is impossible.

Simulating all four options: A (4, 10, 6, 2), B (2, 9, 5, 1) and C (4, 12, 5, 4) each produce exactly one S→Q, one R→Q and two Q→R. Option D (3, 7, 7, 3) produces one S→Q and two Q→R but no R→Q at all, because R finishes before Q's last slice ends. So D is the impossible one.

This is the pattern worth remembering: a round-robin question with constraints on switches is answered by simulating and counting, never by guessing at a formula.

Example 9 — SRTF with a gap in arrivals

GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, Q26. P1 to P4 arrive at 0, 1, 2 and 8 with bursts 10, 13, 6 and 9. SRTF is used and context switches are ignored.

The chart is P1 0–2, P3 2–8 (it finishes), P1 8–16, P4 16–25, P2 25–38. Turnarounds are 16, 37, 6 and 17, which average 19.

Two things to notice: the long P2 finishes last despite arriving second, and the arithmetic is only safe if every resume is drawn as its own segment.

Example 10 — the minimum average waiting time

GATE CSE 2021 Set 1, Q25. Three processes arrive at time zero with bursts 16, 20 and 10, and the scheduler knows the bursts in advance. The minimum achievable average waiting time under a non-preemptive scheduler is asked.

With all three available at zero, SJF is available and optimal: 10, then 16, then 20. Waiting is 0, 10, 26, which averages 12.

The phrase "prior knowledge about the length of the CPU bursts" is what licenses SJF here. Without it, the question would be unanswerable.

Example 11 — utilisation from a quantum

A system switches context in 1 ms and uses a quantum of 4 ms. One cycle is 5 ms, of which 4 ms is user work, so utilisation is 4/5 = 80 per cent and 20 per cent of the CPU is spent switching.

Raise the quantum to 19 ms and the cycle becomes 20 ms: 19/20 = 95 per cent. The gain from 4 to 19 is large; the gain from 19 to 99 is small. That flattening is the reason real quanta are a few tens of milliseconds rather than a few thousand.

Example 12 — why a small quantum is not "more fair"

Take the same four processes with q = 1 and a 1 ms switch. There are 26 units of work and therefore about 26 slices, so about 26 switches: 26 ms of switching against 26 ms of work, and utilisation near 50 per cent.

The response time improves — every process is touched almost at once — while the completion time of everything roughly doubles. Fairness in slices is not the same as efficiency, and no metric in this lesson rewards slicing alone.

Where the rules stop working

Each card is a boundary: a condition a result depends on, or a case where the usual shortcut gives the wrong number.

Idle time at the start

If the first process arrives at t = 5, the axis still starts at 0 and the CPU is idle for five units. Every completion time is measured from 0, not from the first arrival.

The case where this matters: a question that says "assume the CPU is free at time 0" is telling you to count that idle gap as zero; a question that gives an arrival of 5 is telling you to draw it.

A tie in the remaining time

At an arrival, if the new process's burst equals the running process's remaining time, SRTF has no reason to preempt. The running process continues unless the question states a tie rule.

Counterexample to "always preempt on arrival": A has 5 left and B arrives with exactly 5. Preempting changes the chart and the average, but nothing in the definition requires it. This is why questions say "scheduling is carried out only at arrival or completion" and why they state the tie rule when it matters.

SJF optimality without known bursts

The exchange argument compares the true burst lengths. If the scheduler must predict them, the argument does not apply to the schedule it actually produces.

So "SJF minimises average waiting" is true of the algorithm with perfect information, and false of an implementation that guesses. The GATE wording "if the scheduler has prior knowledge about the length of the CPU bursts" exists for exactly this reason.

A quantum longer than every burst

Then no process is ever cut off, and round robin is FCFS — the simulation confirms identical charts for q = 10 and q = 20 on this set.

The boundary is the longest burst in the set, not a fixed number. A question that says "the time slice exceeds the maximum burst" is telling you the answer is FCFS, not asking you to simulate.

Arrivals during the first slice

In round robin, a process that arrives while another is running joins the tail of the queue immediately. When the running process is then preempted, it goes behind the arrival.

Getting this order wrong changes every later completion time. The convention used here — arrival first, then the preempted process — is the standard one; a question that uses the other convention says so.

Starvation against a long wait

A process waiting 100 units under FCFS is not starving: it is at a known position in the queue and will run. A process under priority scheduling whose wait has no upper bound is starving.

The distinguishing property is the existence of a finite bound, not the size of the number. GATE 2023 asked which algorithms can starve, and the answer was priority scheduling and SJF — the two that can postpone a job indefinitely — not FCFS and not round robin.

What the switch-cost formula assumes

Utilisation q/(q+s) assumes exactly one switch per cycle, a fixed switch cost s, and no other overhead. Real systems also have cache effects, and a process switch costs more than a thread switch because the address space changes.

It is a model, and it is the model the questions use. It is not a measurement of any particular machine.

Six wrong beliefs, each with the smallest case that refutes it

Every counterexample below is computed by the same simulation as the animation, so the numbers can be checked against the chart.

"Waiting time and turnaround time are the same"

Why it tempts: for the first process in a non-preemptive schedule, both are zero, and the two words are used loosely in conversation.

Minimal counterexample: A runs 0–10 with arrival 0. Turnaround is 10, waiting is 0. They differ by the burst, always.

Correct rule: T = C − A and W = T − B. If an answer is off by a constant, it is the other metric.

"FCFS can starve a process"

Why it tempts: FCFS can make a process wait a very long time, and "very long" sounds like starvation.

Minimal counterexample: every process in the queue is at a fixed position that only moves forward, so each is served after finitely many completions.

Correct rule: FCFS is starvation-free and non-preemptive. SJF, SRTF and priority scheduling are the starvation risks.

"Preemptive is always better"

Why it tempts: preemption improves the averages in most textbook examples, including both used here.

Minimal counterexample: under SRTF on this set, A waits 16 units where FCFS gave it 0. Under preemptive priority here the average improves, but only because the high-priority job was short.

Correct rule: preemption improves an average, at the cost of the processes that lose, plus a switch per preemption. It is not a per-process improvement.

"A smaller quantum always gives better performance"

Why it tempts: smaller quanta do improve response time, and response time is what users notice.

Minimal counterexample: q = 1 with s = 1 gives 50 per cent utilisation, so half the CPU is spent switching. Waiting times rise even as response times fall.

Correct rule: name the metric first. Response improves as q falls; utilisation and average turnaround worsen.

"Round robin has better average waiting time than SJF"

Why it tempts: round robin is "fair", and fair sounds efficient.

Minimal counterexample: on this set round robin with q = 4 gives 10.75 against SJF's 7.5, and even FCFS beats it at 8.25.

Correct rule: round robin optimises response time. Its waiting time is typically worse than SJF and often worse than FCFS.

"A lower priority number always means higher priority"

Why it tempts: many GATE questions say "0 is the highest priority", which makes the convention feel universal.

Minimal counterexample: this lesson's own priority example uses larger numbers as higher, and states it. With the opposite reading, the chart reverses.

Correct rule: the convention is part of the question. Read the sentence, then draw.

Five question shapes, and the reasoning each demands

These are categories, not predictions. The counts quoted are one archive's tagging of its own index, not an official weightage.

1. Draw the chart, average a metric

The commonest shape: a table of arrivals and bursts, a named algorithm, and "the average waiting time is ____". NAT answers are usually one decimal place.

Demands: the correct preemption rule, every resume drawn, and the right divisor. Common wrong approach: sorting the whole set by burst length and ignoring arrivals.

2. Compare two algorithms on one set

"Which of the following gives the lowest average turnaround time?" with FCFS, SJF, SRTF and RR as options. GATE 2015 Set 3 and GATE 2016 Set 1 are of this shape.

Demands: the theorem that SJF/SRTF minimises average waiting (hence turnaround), which settles the comparison without a full simulation when bursts are known. Common wrong approach: choosing round robin because it is "fairest".

3. Constrained round robin

"There is exactly one switch from S to Q, exactly two from Q to R…" — GATE 2022 Q42. The options are burst tables, and exactly one is inconsistent with the stated switch pattern.

Demands: simulating each option and counting transitions, including the switch after a termination. Common wrong approach: trying to derive a closed-form condition on the bursts.

4. Missing value, given the average

"If the average waiting time is 1 ms, then Z is ____" — GATE 2019. One burst is unknown and the average pins it down.

Demands: the chart as a function of the unknown, then one linear equation. Common wrong approach: assuming the order does not depend on Z. It usually does not, but that must be checked, because a different Z can change the order.

5. Property and definition questions

"Which algorithm cannot be preemptive?" (GATE 2026 Set 2, reported), "which can cause starvation?" (GATE 2023), "which statements are correct about CPU scheduling?" (GATE 2021 Set 2), "which minimises average waiting for jobs submitted together?" (GATE 2016 Set 1).

Demands: the definition of each algorithm and of starvation, not arithmetic. Common wrong approach: treating "preemptive" as a property of the system rather than of the algorithm.

The five traps that cost marks

  1. Dividing by the number of processes that had arrived rather than by the set size.
  2. Forgetting that the axis advances through idle time before the first arrival.
  3. Preempting on a tie in remaining time when the question gives no tie rule.
  4. Computing turnaround when waiting was asked, so the answer is off by the burst.
  5. Adding the switch cost to the numerator, or forgetting it entirely when one is given.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q37

Four processes A (arrival 0, burst 10), B (2, 6), C (4, 3) and D (6, 7) run on one CPU. Which option gives the average waiting times under preemptive SRTF and non-preemptive SJF?

(A) SRTF = 6, NP-SJF = 7    (B) SRTF = 6, NP-SJF = 7.5    (C) SRTF = 7, NP-SJF = 7.5    (D) SRTF = 7, NP-SJF = 8.5

Answer: (B) SRTF = 6, NP-SJF = 7.5

SRTF: A 0–2, B 2–4, C 4–7, B 7–11, D 11–18, A 18–26. Waiting 16, 3, 0, 5 — average 6.

NP-SJF: A 0–10, C 10–13, B 13–19, D 19–26. Waiting 0, 11, 6, 13 — average 7.5.

(A) and (C) come from losing a resume segment. (D) comes from computing turnaround instead of waiting.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2025 Set 2, Q26

P1, P2, P3, P4 arrive at 0, 1, 2 and 8 ms with execution times 10, 13, 6 and 9 ms. SRTF is used and context switches are ignored. Which one gives the average turnaround time?

(A) 22    (B) 15    (C) 37    (D) 19

Answer: (D) 19

Chart: P1 0–2, P3 2–8, P1 8–16, P4 16–25, P2 25–38. Completions 16, 38, 8, 25; turnarounds 16, 37, 6, 17; sum 76, average 19.

(C) 37 is P2's own turnaround, not the average. (A) 22 is close to the average waiting plus something arbitrary — the usual sign that the wrong column was averaged.

NATEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2021 Set 1, Q25

Three processes arrive at time zero with CPU bursts of 16, 20 and 10 ms. If the scheduler has prior knowledge of the burst lengths, the minimum achievable average waiting time for a non-preemptive scheduler is ______ ms.

Answer: 12

With all three available at zero, SJF is available and optimal: 10, then 16, then 20. Waiting times 0, 10, 26; average 36/3 = 12.

"Prior knowledge" is the licence for SJF. Without it, the question has no defined answer, because the optimal order depends on information the scheduler would not have.

MCQHardPYQ — GATE CSE 2022, Q42

Four processes P, Q, R, S arrive in that order at t = 0; the quantum is 4. There is exactly one switch S→Q, exactly one R→Q and exactly two Q→R, and no switch S→P. Switching to a ready process after a termination also counts. Which is NOT a possible burst table?

(A) P = 4, Q = 10, R = 6, S = 2    (B) P = 2, Q = 9, R = 5, S = 1    (C) P = 4, Q = 12, R = 5, S = 4    (D) P = 3, Q = 7, R = 7, S = 3

Answer: (D)

Option D's chart is P 0–3, Q 3–7, R 7–11, S 11–14, Q 14–17, R 17–20. Transitions: P→Q, Q→R, R→S, S→Q, Q→R. There is no R→Q, because R finishes at 20 while Q has already completed.

A, B and C each produce exactly one S→Q, one R→Q and two Q→R. Counting transitions is the only safe method here; the burst lengths interact with the quantum in ways no simple inequality captures.

MSQEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2023, Q27

Which one or more of the following CPU scheduling algorithms can potentially cause starvation?

(A) First-in First-out    (B) Round Robin    (C) Priority scheduling    (D) Shortest Job First

Answer: (C) and (D)

Priority scheduling postpones a low-priority process while higher-priority ones keep arriving. SJF postpones a long job while shorter ones keep arriving. In both cases the wait has no finite bound.

FIFO advances every process through a fixed position, and round robin gives every process a slice in turn, so neither can postpone one indefinitely. A long wait under FIFO is not starvation.

MCQEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2016 Set 1, Q20

An arbitrary set of CPU-bound processes with unequal burst lengths is submitted at the same time. Which scheduling algorithm minimises the average waiting time in the ready queue?

(A) Shortest remaining time first    (B) Round robin with a quantum shorter than the shortest burst    (C) Uniform random    (D) Highest priority first, with priority proportional to burst length

Answer: (A)

With all processes available at once, SRTF behaves exactly as SJF, and SJF minimises average waiting by the exchange argument. (B) is dominated by switch overhead and is not optimal for waiting.

(D) is the worst of the four: priority proportional to burst length runs the longest job first, which is exactly the convoy effect generalised.

MCQOriginalMedium

Using A (0, 10), B (2, 6), C (4, 3), D (6, 7), what is the average waiting time under round robin with quantum 4?

(A) 8.25    (B) 10.75    (C) 13.0    (D) 12.5

Answer: (B) 10.75

Chart: A 0–4, B 4–8, C 8–11, A 11–15, D 15–19, B 19–21, A 21–23, D 23–26. Waiting 13, 13, 4, 12; sum 43; average 10.75.

(A) is the FCFS answer for the same set. (C) and (D) come from forgetting that a preempted process rejoins the tail rather than resuming immediately.

NATOriginalEasy

A system switches context in 2 ms and uses a quantum of 18 ms. What percentage of CPU time is spent on useful work, to one decimal place?

Answer: 90.0 per cent

One cycle is 18 + 2 = 20 ms, of which 18 ms is user work: 18/20 = 0.9.

The trap is dividing 18 by 18, which gives 100 per cent. The switch is part of the cycle, always.

MCQOriginalMedium

Which statement is false?

(A) A quantum at least as long as the longest burst makes round robin identical to FCFS.    (B) SRTF can give a longer job a larger waiting time than FCFS does.    (C) Round robin always has a lower average turnaround time than SJF.    (D) Aging can turn priority scheduling into something close to round robin.

Answer: (C) is false

On this lesson's set, round robin q = 4 gives average waiting 10.75 against SJF's 7.5, and turnaround follows waiting plus a constant, so round robin is worse.

(A) is the degenerate limit, checked by simulation at q = 10 and q = 20. (B) is scene 4: A waits 16 under SRTF and 0 under FCFS. (D) is the GATE 2013 observation that priority proportional to waiting time behaves like round robin.

MSQOriginalMedium

For the multilevel feedback queue in scene 7 (quanta 2, then 4, then FCFS) applied to A, B, C, D, which statements are true?

(A) Every process has a response time of 0.    (B) The average waiting time is lower than round robin with q = 4.    (C) Every process uses its full quantum in the top queue at least once.    (D) The average waiting time is higher than FCFS.

Answer: (A) and (D)

Every process is served in Q0 on arrival, so all four response times are 0. Average waiting is 11.5, which is above round robin's 10.75 and above FCFS's 8.25.

(B) is false for exactly that reason. (C) is false because C needs only 3 units in total: it uses 2 in Q0 and finishes with 1 unit in Q1, never completing a Q0 slice.

NATOriginalHard

Processes arrive at 0, 1 and 2 with bursts 8, 3 and 2 and priorities 2, 1 and 3, where a larger number is higher. Under preemptive priority, what is the average waiting time?

Answer: 11/3, about 3.67

P1 runs 0–2; P3 arrives with the highest priority and preempts, running 2–4 and finishing; P1 resumes 4–10; P2 runs 10–13.

Waiting: P1 2, P2 9, P3 0. Sum 11, average 3.67. Under non-preemptive priority the same set averages 5.00 — the difference is entirely the one preemption at t = 2.

MCQOriginalEasy

A process arrives at time 5 with a burst of 4 and is scheduled first. It completes at time 9. Its turnaround time is

(A) 4    (B) 5    (C) 9    (D) 0

Answer: (A) 4

Turnaround is completion minus arrival: 9 − 5 = 4. Its waiting time is 0, and its response time is 0.

(C) 9 is the completion time, which is what a chart shows directly; the subtraction is the step people skip. (B) 5 is the arrival. (D) is the waiting time.

Revision sheet

Definitions, the rules per algorithm, the formulas and the traps, in the order a question uses them.

Definitions, one line each

  • Scheduler — chooses one ready process when the CPU becomes free.
  • Dispatcher — performs the context switch to the chosen process.
  • Preemptive — the system may take the CPU away; non-preemptive may not.
  • Quantum — the maximum slice a process gets before rejoining the queue.
  • Turnaround — completion minus arrival.
  • Waiting — turnaround minus burst.
  • Response — first service minus arrival.
  • Throughput — completions per unit time.
  • Starvation — no finite bound on a process's wait.
  • Aging — raising priority with waiting time to bound the wait.

The rules, per algorithm

  • FCFS — arrival order, never preempts, never starves, convoy effect.
  • SJF — shortest burst among arrived, never preempts, starves long jobs.
  • SRTF — shortest remaining time, preempts at arrivals.
  • Round robin — fixed quantum, FIFO queue, timer preemption.
  • Priority — highest priority; preemptive or not as stated.
  • MLFQ — several queues, demote on full slice, promote periodically.

Formulas, with their licences

T = C − A, W = T − B, R = first − A

Licence: none needed; these are definitions.

sum(T) = sum(W) + sum(B)

Licence: a fixed process set. This is why SJF's optimality for one metric carries to the other.

utilisation = q / (q + s)

Licence: one switch per cycle, fixed cost s, no other overhead. It is a model, not a measurement.

Decision procedure for any question

  1. Read the rules: preemptive or not, bursts known or not, which metric. Justification: all three change the chart.
  2. Mark arrivals on the axis, including any gap before the first one.
  3. Walk the timeline, choose by the stated rule, and break the bar at every choice.
  4. Read completion times, then subtract: T, then W, then R.
  5. Average over the set size; check no waiting time is negative.

Numbers from this lesson, for recall

On A(0,10), B(2,6), C(4,3), D(6,7): FCFS average waiting 8.25; NP-SJF 7.5; SRTF 6.0 with response 1.25; RR q=4 10.75 with response 3.75; MLFQ 11.5 with response 0. Priority example: preemptive 3.67, non-preemptive 5.00. Utilisation with s = 1: q = 10 → 90.9%, q = 4 → 80%, q = 2 → 66.7%, q = 1 → 50%.

The five traps

  1. Dividing by the number of arrived processes instead of the set size.
  2. Ignoring idle time before the first arrival.
  3. Preempting on a tie in remaining time.
  4. Answering turnaround when waiting was asked.
  5. Forgetting to add the switch cost to the cycle.
