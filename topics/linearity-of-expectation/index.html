Topic and objective

TOPIC: Linearity of expectation and indicator variables — the mean of a sum, and the one technique that turns almost every "expected number of" question in GATE into arithmetic.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Compute the mean of a sum from the separate means, with no joint distribution and no independence. Write a count as a sum of indicators and read its mean as a sum of probabilities. Apply that to fixed points, inversions, hashing loads and collisions, quicksort comparisons and the tail-sum identity. State precisely where the rule stops — variance, products, and functions of the mean — and prove the three counterexamples that stop it.

I am reading this microtopic as one licence and one habit: the licence to add means without asking about dependence, and the habit of choosing the right unit — pairs, positions or slots — before writing a single indicator.

Mean, median, mode and standard deviation. Conditional probability and Bayes theorem." The mean of a random variable is named; this lesson teaches the property of the mean that makes a count computable.

Prerequisites

  • Probability, random variables and distributions — sample spaces, conditional probability, the definition of expectation as a weighted average, and variance.
  • Counting and combinatorics — C(n,2) and the product rule. Every unit in this lesson is counted with them.
  • Hashing: hash functions and collision resolution — simple uniform hashing, load factor, and what a collision is.
  • Sorting algorithms — quicksort, its partition step, and the fact that comparisons dominate its cost.
  • Finite sums. The whole derivation is one swap of two summation signs.

Nothing here needs software. Every number is computed on the page, and the enumeration that backs it is real: all 5040 permutations of seven items, all 125 assignments of three keys to five slots.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • The mean of a sum is the sum of the means. Always, dependent or not.
  • An indicator's mean is its probability. E[I] = P(A), exactly.
  • Count by unit. One indicator per pair, position or slot — never one per outcome.
  • Independence is never asked for. The method needs n probabilities, not a joint distribution.
  • The licence covers sums only. Variance needs a covariance term; a product needs independence.

Intuition first: the average of a total is the total of the averages

Five people in a room earn 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 thousand rupees. Add the salaries and divide by five: the total is 200 thousand, so the average is 40. Now do it the other way. Each person's salary is itself an average — of one number — and 20 + 30 + 40 + 50 + 60 divided by five is the same 40. Nothing surprising: dividing a sum by a constant distributes over the sum.

The exam version is the same identity with the average taken over randomness instead of over people. A total is a sum of parts; the average of the total is the average of the parts. What makes this a technique rather than a tautology is that the parts can be chosen to be events rather than amounts. Educational interpretation The salary picture carries the additivity and nothing else; it says nothing about how randomness enters.

Formal vocabulary, every term in words

Random variable — a number attached to each outcome. The sum of two dice is a random variable on the 36 pairs.

Expectation, or mean, E[X] — the sum over all values x of x times the probability that X equals x. For a die: 1·(1/6) + 2·(1/6) + … + 6·(1/6) = 3.5.

Joint distribution — the probability of every pair of values at once. For two dice that is 36 numbers; for twelve events it is 4096.

Independent — the joint probability factorises into the product of the separate probabilities.

Indicator I(A) — the variable that is 1 on outcomes where event A happens and 0 elsewhere.

The rule, stated once

For any two random variables X and Y on the same sample space, and any constants a, b, c:

E[aX + bY + c] = a·E[X] + b·E[Y] + cNo assumption about X and Y appears in this statement. They may be independent, perfectly correlated, or anything in between.

Mathematical derivation Write the expectation of the sum over the joint distribution: sum over all pairs (x, y) of (x + y) times P(X = x, Y = y). Split into two sums. In the first, sum over y first — the probabilities over y add to the marginal probability of x, so the inner sum is P(X = x) and the outer sum is E[X]. The second is E[Y] by the same move. Both moves are reorderings of a finite sum of non-negative terms, so they are always legal. That is the entire proof, and it is why no independence is needed.

The indicator lemma

For an event A with probability p, let I(A) be 1 when A happens and 0 otherwise. Then

E[I(A)] = 1·p + 0·(1−p) = p = P(A)The mean of an indicator is the probability of its event. Nothing is approximated.

Combine the lemma with the rule and the whole technique appears. If a count X equals the sum of indicators I₁ … Iₙ — one per unit — then E[X] = P(A₁) + … + P(Aₙ). Verified fact This is Lemma 5.1 and the indicator technique of CLRS, Introduction to Algorithms, chapter 5, and it is the standard treatment in university probability-for-computing courses.

The tail-sum form, a second route

When X takes only values 0, 1, 2, … then

E[X] = P(X ≥ 1) + P(X ≥ 2) + P(X ≥ 3) + …An outcome with value k contributes to the first k of these terms, so it is counted exactly k times — its own weight.

Mathematical derivation The right-hand side is a sum over k of a sum over outcomes with X ≥ k. Swap the two sums: each outcome with value m is counted for k = 1 … m, so m times. Reordering needs the terms to be non-negative, which is exactly the hypothesis. Used in scene 9 on the larger of two dice, where the six tails give 161/36 = 4.472.

The four counts this lesson computes, and the unit each one counts in

Every one of these is "an expected number of" something. The only decision is which unit to write an indicator for; once the unit is right, the rest is adding probabilities.

Fixed points — unit: a position. n positions, each fixed with probability 1/n.

E = n · (1/n) = 1

Inversions — unit: a pair. C(n,2) pairs, each inverted with probability 1/2.

E = C(n,2)/2 = n(n−1)/4

Hash slot load — unit: a key. n keys, each landing in a given slot with probability 1/m.

E[load] = n/m, E[collisions] = C(n,2)/m

Quicksort comparisons — unit: a pair. Each pair compared with probability 2/(j−i+1).

E = 2(n+1)H(n) − 4n

Mathematical derivation Each of the four is derived on this page, and each is checked against a full enumeration or a recurrence: the fixed-point and inversion totals over every permutation up to n = 7, all 125 assignments for hashing, and the quicksort recurrence against its closed form at every n from 2 to 120 with a largest gap under 10⁻⁹.

Where the licence stops, in one table

Sums. E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] — always.

Scaling. E[aX] = a·E[X] — always.

Products. E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y] — only with independence. Counterexample: X = Y a fair die gives 91/6 against 49/4.

Variance of a sum. Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X,Y) — the last term vanishes only when the two are uncorrelated. With X = Y the variance is 4× a single die, not 2×.

Functions. E[g(X)] = g(E[X]) — only for affine g. For a die, E[1/X] = 49/120 = 0.4083 while 1/E[X] = 2/7 = 0.2857.

Why this is worth a whole lesson

Almost every "expected number of" item is a count of small units inside a random structure: pairs in a permutation, keys in a slot, comparisons in a sort, collisions in a hash table. Writing the joint distribution is hopeless — 4096 outcomes for twelve events, 52! shuffles for a deck — but writing one indicator per unit costs one probability per unit.

The other half of the lesson is knowing what the technique does not buy. It buys the mean and nothing else: not the variance, not the distribution, not the probability that the count is near its mean. Questions that ask for a spread or a probability are answered by different tools, and the exam relies on students confusing the two.

Five worked items, each with its arithmetic shown

Items 1 and 2 are previous-year questions with their paper given; each answer was recomputed here from the question text rather than copied from a key. Example 1 — GATE CSE 2002, the random search

An unsorted array of n distinct values contains x. The algorithm picks an index uniformly at random, checks it, and repeats if it was wrong, with no memory of earlier picks.

The unit: a trial. Each trial succeeds with probability 1/n, and trials are independent because nothing is remembered.

Let X be the number of trials. Then P(X = k) = (1 − 1/n)^(k−1) · (1/n), and the mean of that distribution is 1/p = n. Averaging the infinite sum directly gives the same n.

Answer: n. The wrong options are n−1 (treating the search as sequential and stopping one short), n/2 (assuming the element is equally likely in either half) and 2n (counting a comparison and a move).

Example 2 — GATE CSE 2022 Q16, two hash functions

n keys, m slots, two simple uniform hash functions h₁ and h₂; h₁ is used for the odd keys and h₂ for the even ones. What is the expected number of keys in a slot?

The unit: a key. Fix slot j. For any key, whichever function it uses, the probability of landing in slot j is 1/m, because both functions are simple uniform.

Let I(i) be 1 when key i lands in slot j. The load of slot j is the sum of the n indicators, so E = n · (1/m) = n/m. No case split over odd and even is needed.

Answer: n/m. The correct option in the official paper is n/m. The trap is to split into two sums and expect a different answer; the split changes nothing because each key's probability is the same 1/m.

Example 3 — a 52-card deck, how many cards stay put?

Shuffle a standard deck. How many cards land back in their original position, on average?

The unit: a position, of which there are 52. Position i is fixed with probability 1/52, because the card that ends there is uniform over the 52 cards.

E = 52 · (1/52) = 1. The answer does not depend on 52 at all: it is 1 for a deck of three cards and 1 for a deck of a million.

The page checks the same claim by enumeration for n = 1 to 7: the total number of fixed points over all n! shuffles is exactly n! each time, so the mean is exactly 1 and not merely close to it.

Example 4 — ten keys into twenty slots

Simple uniform hashing, n = 10 keys, m = 20 slots. Three different questions, three different units.

Load of one slot: 10 · (1/20) = 0.5 keys.

Colliding pairs: C(10,2) = 45 pairs, each colliding with probability 1/20, so 45/20 = 2.25 collisions on average.

Occupied slots: 20 · (1 − (1 − 1/20)¹⁰) = 20 · (1 − 0.5987) = 8.03 slots, using the same indicator method on the event "slot j is empty".

All three are checked on the page by enumerating every one of the 125 assignments of three keys to five slots, where the same three formulas come out as 0.6, 0.6 and 2.44 exactly.

Example 5 — quicksort on ten keys

Randomised quicksort on ten distinct keys. How many comparisons on average?

The unit: a pair of keys. Keys i and j are compared exactly when the first pivot chosen among the j−i+1 keys from i to j is one of the two, which happens with probability 2/(j−i+1).

Summing over all 45 pairs and using H(10) = 2.928968 gives 2 · 11 · 2.928968 − 40 = 24.4373 comparisons.

The recurrence E(n) = (n−1) + (2/n)·Σ_{k<n} E(k) gives the same 24.4373, and the page checks the two against each other at every n from 2 to 120. For comparison, 2n ln n at n = 10 is 46.05, so the bound is loose by nearly a factor of two at this size.

What the five items have in common

Each one names a unit — a trial, a key, a position, a pair — writes one indicator per unit, and asks a single question of each: what is the probability that this unit's event happens? None of the five ever writes a joint distribution, and none of the five ever needs independence. The difference between the items is only which unit was chosen, and choosing the unit is the part that requires thought.

The boundaries: where each rule stops working

Six boundaries, each of which turns a correct-looking calculation into a wrong answer. The rule stated in each card is the one the exam tests, and the example is the smallest one that shows the difference.

1. The mean may not exist

Linearity needs the expectations to be finite numbers. If E[X] is infinite, "E[X] − E[X]" is not zero — it is undefined, and adding and subtracting infinite means produces nonsense.

A variable with P(X = 2^k) = 2^−k has E[X] = 1 + 1 + 1 + … = ∞. Its mean is not a number, so it cannot be added to anything. Exam questions avoid this by giving a finite sample space; the boundary matters when a question involves an unbounded number of trials.

2. The mean need not be attainable, or an integer

A fair die has mean 3.5, which no face shows. Ten keys in twenty slots average 0.5 keys per slot, which no slot holds. A count's mean need not be a count.

So an option is never eliminated for being fractional or unattainable. 4.4722 for the larger of two dice is correct even though the variable takes only whole values; the mean is a balance point, not an outcome.

3. Uncorrelated is not independent

Cov(X,Y) = 0 is enough to make variances add, and it is not enough to make means of products multiply. Take X uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X²: the covariance is 0 by symmetry, yet E[XY] = E[X³] = 0 while E[X]E[Y] = 0 · (2/3) = 0 — equality here is a coincidence, and the standard example with a shifted variable breaks it.

The safe statement: independence implies E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]; zero covariance does not. If a question says only "uncorrelated", the product rule is not available.

4. An indicator takes only 0 and 1

E[I] = P(A) holds because the two values are 1 and 0. If a variable takes 1 and 2 — a common slip when counting "the item and its partner" — then E = 1·p + 2·(1−p) = 2 − p, which is not P(A).

Check before applying the lemma: does this variable take exactly the values 0 and 1? If not, it is not an indicator and the lemma does not apply to it.

5. Exactly n−1 fixed points is impossible

In a permutation of five items, no shuffle has exactly four fixed points: if four positions hold their own cards, the fifth card is the only one left and its position is the only one left, so it is fixed too.

The n = 5 distribution is 44, 45, 20, 10, 0, 1 — the zero is at four. A mean of 1 therefore does not make one fixed point typical: 44 of the 120 shuffles have none, and 89 have zero or one. The mean and the shape are different facts.

6. The half-probability argument needs distinct keys

"Each pair is inverted in exactly half the permutations" holds because swapping the two values pairs up the permutations that invert the pair with those that do not. That bijection needs the two values to be different.

With ties the probability depends on how many equal values there are, and n(n−1)/4 is no longer the answer. GATE states "distinct" in such questions; when it does not, the argument has to be redone.

The one boundary to carry into the exam

The licence is additive over sums of expectations and over nothing else. Before using a formula, name the quantity: if it is a mean of a sum, add freely; if it is a variance, a product, or a mean of a transformed variable, stop and check the hypothesis — independence, or affine-ness, or non-negativity — that the formula needs.

Five beliefs that produce a plausible wrong answer

Each belief below is stated as a student would state it, then broken by the smallest counterexample that fits on one line. The last card is the single distinction that separates the four correct uses from the four incorrect ones.

1. "Linearity only works when the variables are independent"

Why it feels right: independence is the hypothesis of the product rule and of the variance rule, so it seems to be the hypothesis of everything.

Counterexample: Y = X for a fair die. The joint distribution is six points on a diagonal, the two variables are perfectly dependent, and E[X + Y] = 2 · 3.5 = 7 exactly matches the enumeration of 2X.

Repair: the derivation of linearity only reorders a finite sum. No factorisation of the joint probability ever appears, so no independence is ever assumed.

2. "The mean of a function is the function of the mean"

Why it feels right: for a linear function it is exactly true, and most functions met in a first course are linear.

Counterexample: a fair die has E[1/X] = 49/120 = 0.4083, while 1/E[X] = 1/3.5 = 0.2857. Squaring fails too: E[X²] = 91/6 = 15.1667 against (E[X])² = 12.25.

Repair: E[g(X)] = g(E[X]) only for affine g. For anything else, average g over the distribution. Jensen's inequality says which way the error goes for convex g: the true value is larger.

3. "The expected count must be a whole number"

Why it feels right: the variable counts things, and things come in whole numbers.

Counterexample: three keys in five slots average 0.6 keys per slot. The larger of two dice averages 4.4722. Both are correct means of variables that never take those values.

Repair: an expectation is a weighted average over the values, so it lies between the smallest and the largest value and is usually none of them. Never round unless the question asks for an integer and says so.

4. "If the means multiply, the variances add"

Why it feels right: both are called "the rule for independent variables", so they are remembered as one rule.

Counterexample: X = Y a fair die. Var(X + Y) = Var(2X) = 4 · 2.9167 = 11.667, which is neither 2 · 2.9167 = 5.833 nor 4 times anything the product rule predicts. The product E[XY] is 15.1667, not 12.25.

Repair: independence gives both rules, but they are different rules with different hypotheses. Variance of a sum needs zero covariance; a mean of a product needs independence. Neither implies the other.

5. "Each pair being dependent spoils the sum over pairs"

Why it feels right: the indicators in quicksort, inversions and fixed points are visibly dependent — one comparison changes many pairs — and dependence is the thing students have been warned about.

Counterexample: the inversion indicators of a permutation are strongly dependent, and the sum over the C(n,2) pairs still gives exactly n(n−1)/4, checked against every permutation up to n = 7.

Repair: dependence is what linearity is for. It is the reason the method is used on counts of dependent units rather than on the joint distribution.

The one distinction to carry into the exam

Mean of a sum: always additive. Anything else: check the hypothesis.

If an option claims additivity of the mean, it is true. If an option claims additivity of a variance without saying the variables are independent or uncorrelated, it is false. If an option claims multiplicativity of means without saying independent, it is false. Those three sentences settle most statement-set questions on this topic.

How this clause gets tested, and what each pattern demands

Four patterns, in the order they appear across papers. Each card says what the question looks like, what it is really asking for, and the single step that decides the answer.

Pattern 1 — expected value of a count in a random structure

Shape: "A random permutation of n items. What is the expected number of …?" or "n keys are hashed into m slots. What is the expected number of …?"

Really asking: choose the unit, then one probability per unit. The count is fixed points, inversions, descents, colliding pairs or occupied slots.

The deciding step: the probability attached to one unit. For a position it is 1/n; for a pair it is 1/2; for a key-slot pair it is 1/m. Get that number and the rest is multiplication.

Pattern 2 — expected comparisons or probes

Shape: "the expected number of comparisons made by randomised quicksort", "the expected number of probes in a hash table", "the expected number of trials before success".

Really asking: the same method, applied to algorithm events rather than combinatorial objects. For quicksort the event is "this pair is compared"; for a repeated trial it is "this trial succeeds".

The deciding step: state the event precisely. "The first pivot among these keys is one of the two endpoints" gives 2/(j−i+1); a vague event gives a wrong probability and a wrong constant.

Pattern 3 — statement sets on dependence

Shape: an MSQ listing four or five statements about E[X+Y], E[XY], Var(X+Y), and which hypotheses each needs.

Really asking: whether you can separate the three rules and their three hypotheses.

The deciding step: apply the three sentences from the misconceptions tab. Additivity of means: always. Additivity of variances: needs zero covariance. Multiplicativity of means: needs independence. A statement missing its hypothesis is false.

Pattern 4 — a small numerical answer (NAT)

Shape: "the expected number of inversions in a random permutation of 8 elements is ______", with a numeric field.

Really asking: the closed form, evaluated carefully, with no rounding until the end.

The deciding step: substitute into the formula, then check the magnitude. 8 elements give 8·7/4 = 14, an integer; a fractional answer where the formula must give an integer is a signal that the wrong unit was counted.

The four-step procedure, and where each step can go wrong

Step 1 — name the unit. Positions, pairs, keys, slots, trials, comparisons. Wrong unit: counting keys when the question counts colliding pairs, which changes C(n,2)/m into n/m.

Step 2 — write one indicator per unit. Wrong: one indicator per outcome, which reintroduces the joint distribution the method exists to avoid.

Step 3 — one probability per indicator. Wrong: computing a joint probability, then getting stuck. Only the marginal probability of each event is ever needed.

Step 4 — add, and read the unit back. Wrong: reporting a mean with the wrong unit attached, or rounding a fractional mean to an integer.

Where marks are actually lost

Three losses dominate. First, refusing to use linearity because the indicators look dependent, then attempting the joint distribution and running out of time. Second, extending linearity to a variance or a product, which turns a correct method into a false statement. Third, answering the mean when the question asked for a probability, or the probability when it asked for the mean: "what is the expected number of collisions" and "what is the probability of at least one collision" are different questions with different answers, and the birthday-problem numbers make them look alike.

MCQGATE CSE 2002, 2 marks

Consider the following algorithm for searching for a given number x in an unsorted array A[1..n] having n distinct values: (1) choose an i uniformly at random from 1..n; (2) if A[i] = x then stop, else go to (1). Assuming that x is present in A, what is the expected number of comparisons made by the algorithm before it terminates?

  1. n
  2. n − 1
  3. 2n
  4. n / 2

Answer: A — n

Each trial succeeds with probability 1/n, and the trials are independent because the algorithm keeps no memory of the indices it has tried. The number of trials therefore has mean 1/p = n. Summing the arithmetico-geometric series 1·(1/n) + 2·(n−1)/n² + 3·(n−1)²/n³ + … gives the same n.

Why n−1 appears. It is the answer for a sequential search that stops one short, or for "the number of failures". Why n/2 appears. It assumes the element is equally likely in either half, which is a binary-search intuition applied to an unsorted array. Why 2n appears. It counts a comparison and a loop step as two comparisons.

Numbering note: catalogues disagree on the question number — GATE Overflow files it as Q2.10, ExamSIDE as Q53, and a chapter-wise compilation prints it as Q12. The paper and the marks are certain; the number is not, so only the paper is quoted.

NATGATE CSE 2022 Q16

Suppose we are given n keys, m hash table slots, and two simple uniform hash functions h₁ and h₂. Our hashing scheme uses h₁ for the odd keys and h₂ for the even keys. What is the expected number of keys in a slot? Give the answer as an expression in n and m.

The official paper offers this as a single-correct MCQ whose correct option is n/m; the option list is not reproduced here because only the correct option could be confirmed.

Answer: n/m

Fix a slot j. Each key, odd or even, lands in j with probability 1/m, because both h₁ and h₂ are simple uniform. Let I(i) be the indicator that key i lands in j; the load is the sum of the n indicators, so E[load] = n · (1/m) = n/m.

Why a case split is tempting and useless. Splitting into n_odd and n_even gives n_odd·(1/m) + n_even·(1/m), which is the same n/m. Why n/2m appears. It divides by the number of functions, which is not a quantity the load depends on.

NATOriginal practice

A random permutation of 8 distinct elements is chosen uniformly from all 8! permutations. What is the expected number of inversions?

Answer: 14

C(8,2) = 28 pairs, and each pair is inverted in exactly half of the permutations, so E = 28 · (1/2) = 14, which is also 8·7/4.

Why 28 appears. That is the number of pairs, not the number of inversions — the factor of one half has been dropped. Why 56 appears. Multiplying the pair count by 2 instead of by 1/2.

MSQOriginal practice

Let X and Y be random variables on the same sample space, with finite means and variances. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

  1. E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] whether or not X and Y are independent.
  2. If X and Y are uncorrelated then Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y).
  3. If X and Y are uncorrelated then E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y].
  4. If X and Y are independent then E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y].
  5. E[X²] ≥ (E[X])² always.

Answer: A, B, D and E

A is linearity, which assumes nothing. B follows from Var(X+Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X,Y), and uncorrelated means the covariance is zero. D is the definition of independence applied to the product. E is Var(X) ≥ 0 rewritten, since Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])².

Why C is false. Uncorrelated only kills the covariance; the product rule needs the full factorisation of the joint distribution. Independence implies uncorrelated, never the reverse.

NATOriginal practice

Ten keys are inserted into a hash table of 20 slots under simple uniform hashing, with collisions resolved by chaining. What is the expected number of colliding pairs of keys?

Answer: 2.25

There are C(10,2) = 45 pairs. Each pair collides with probability 1/20, so the expected number of colliding pairs is 45/20 = 2.25.

Why 0.5 appears. That is the expected load of one slot, n/m — a different quantity. Why 45 appears. That is the pair count with the probability omitted.

NATOriginal practice

Randomised quicksort sorts 4 distinct keys, choosing each pivot uniformly at random from the current subarray. What is the expected number of key comparisons, correct to two decimal places?

Answer: 4.83

The recurrence is E(n) = (n−1) + (2/n)·Σ_{k=0}^{n−1} E(k), with E(0) = E(1) = 0. It gives E(2) = 1, E(3) = 2 + (2/3)(1) = 2.6667, and E(4) = 3 + (2/4)(0 + 0 + 1 + 2.6667) = 3 + 1.8333 = 4.8333. The closed form 2(n+1)H(n) − 4n gives 2·5·2.08333 − 16 = 4.8333 as well.

Why 3 appears. That is only the first partition's comparisons. Why 6 appears. That is the worst case, where the pivots are always extreme.

Revision sheet

Definitions, formulas, the decision procedure and the traps, in the order you would use them. Nothing here needs the animation, and every number was computed on this page.

The facts worth memorising

  • E[aX + bY + c] = aE[X] + bE[Y] + c — for any joint distribution.
  • E[I(A)] = P(A) for an indicator.
  • E[count] = Σ P(Aᵢ) when the count is a sum of indicators.
  • E[X] = Σ_{k≥1} P(X ≥ k) for a non-negative integer X.
  • Var(X+Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X,Y).
  • E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] needs independence; zero covariance is not enough.
  • E[g(X)] = g(E[X]) only for affine g.

The counts, with their units

  • Fixed points of a random permutation: 1, for every n. Unit: a position, probability 1/n.
  • Inversions: n(n−1)/4. Unit: a pair, probability 1/2.
  • Descending positions (descents): (n−1)/2. Unit: an adjacent pair, probability 1/2.
  • Hash slot load: n/m. Colliding pairs: C(n,2)/m. Occupied slots: m(1 − (1 − 1/m)ⁿ).
  • Keys to fill m slots (coupon collector): m·H(m), which is 14.7 for six slots.
  • Colliding birthday pairs among 23 people: 253/365 = 0.693.
  • Quicksort comparisons: 2(n+1)H(n) − 4n, which is 24.44 at n = 10.

Decision procedure, in four lines

1. Name the unit being counted: position, pair, key, slot, trial, comparison.

2. Write one indicator per unit, so the count is their sum exactly.

3. For each indicator, compute only the probability of its event. Never a joint probability.

4. Add, then check the magnitude and the unit against the question.

The five slips, one last time

  • Believing independence is required — it never is, for means of sums.
  • Writing E[1/X] = 1/E[X]: 0.4083 against 0.2857 for a die.
  • Writing Var(X+Y) = Var X + Var Y with no hypothesis: 11.667 against 5.833 for X = Y.
  • Writing E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] from uncorrelatedness alone.
  • Rejecting a fractional or unattainable mean: 0.6 keys per slot is correct.

Numbers from this lesson worth remembering

Two dice: mean 7, variance 35/6 independent, 35/3 when the same die is doubled. Larger of two dice: 161/36 = 4.4722. Fixed points at n = 5: 44, 45, 20, 10, 0, 1. Inversions: 5 at n = 5, 14 at n = 8. Hashing at n = 3, m = 5: load 0.6, collisions 0.6, occupied 2.44. Quicksort: 24.4373 at n = 10 against 46.05 for 2n ln n.

One question to ask before writing anything

"Is this quantity a mean of a sum of things I can define one at a time?" If yes, the method applies and independence is irrelevant. If no — if it is a variance, a probability, or a mean of a product — the method does not apply and a different tool is needed. That single question separates every correct use in this lesson from every incorrect one.
