Topic and objective

TOPIC: The normal (Gaussian) distribution — its density and its two parameters, the standardisation that turns every normal into one curve, the table and its symmetries, the empirical rule as an integral rather than a slogan, the affine map and the sum of independent normals, the median when a point carries mass, and the central limit theorem that explains why the curve keeps appearing.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read mu and sigma off a printed density. Standardise with the standard deviation, never the variance. Turn any left tail, right tail or band into Phi. Quote 68-95-99.7 as computed areas and say what lies beyond three sigma. Apply aX + b and add independent normals. Find a median of a variable that has an atom. Say when normality is a conclusion (a sum or an average) and when it is an assumption (one measurement). Measure how good the binomial approximation is, with and without the continuity correction.

I am reading this microtopic as one habit: standardise first, then decide which tail the words asked for. Every scene and every example on the page is that habit applied to a different question.

Prerequisites

Three lessons, all published in this repository:

  • Probability and random variables — density against mass, expectation and variance. The normal is the second continuous distribution in the syllabus, so the density idea must already be comfortable.
  • Mean, median, mode and standard deviation — needed for the median definition with atoms, and for the difference between a variance and a standard deviation, which is the single most common arithmetic slip in this topic.
  • The exponential and Poisson distributions — not required, but it is the sibling lesson: the Poisson is the discrete limit the binomial approaches, and the normal is the other limit of the same binomial, so the two lessons are read together.

Approximate learning time

About 40 to 55 minutes for the animation, the worked examples and the practice set, if the prerequisites are already comfortable. This is an estimate, not a measurement.

Educational interpretation The five ideas to leave with: sigma is the standard deviation and a variance is not; Phi is always a left tail; the 68-95-99.7 rule is an integral; a linear map shifts the mean and scales the spread, and independent normals add in variance; and the bell is derived for sums and assumed for single measurements.

From a shape to a table

The normal distribution is one curve with two dials. Turn the first dial and the curve slides; turn the second and it widens. Then one substitution — subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation — collapses every setting of both dials onto a single curve, and that single curve is the only thing any table ever contains.

Level 1 — the intuition

A machine fills packets to a target weight. Most packets are close to the target, a few are a little light, a few a little heavy, and the errors are symmetric: overshooting and undershooting are equally likely and equally common.

Two numbers then describe the whole picture. The target is the centre. The typical size of the error is the width. Everything else — the fraction too light, the fraction within a tolerance, the cutoff for the worst one per cent — is an area under that one curve.

Educational interpretation The machine is a good picture of symmetry and a poor one of independence: real filling errors drift with temperature, which is exactly the failure the last scene computes.

Level 2 — the formal definition

A random variable X is normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared, written N(mu, sigma squared), when its density is

f(x) = 1/(sigma root 2 pi) · exp( -(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2) ), for every real x.

Every symbol in words: mu is the centre, in the units of the data; sigma is the standard deviation, also in the units of the data; sigma squared is the variance; exp means e raised to the power of; the leading constant 1/(sigma root 2 pi) is what makes the area exactly one.

The standard normal is the case mu = 0, sigma = 1, written Z, with density phi(z) = exp(-z squared / 2) / root 2 pi and cumulative function Phi(z) = the area to the left of z.

Level 3 — standardisation, and why one table is enough

Put z = (x - mu)/sigma. Substituting into the density shows that Z has density phi, exactly, with no approximation: the substitution is a change of variable, and a change of variable preserves area.

P(X at most x) = P(Z at most (x - mu)/sigma) = Phi((x - mu)/sigma)

The page checks the consequence that makes the table usable: Phi(z) + Phi(-z) = 1 at six values of z, to within one in ten to the twelfth. Derivation

The page also computes Phi by two different quadrature rules — composite Simpson and a 40-node Gauss-Legendre rule — and requires them to agree to one in ten to the ninth, because there is no elementary closed form to check against.

Level 4 — the peak, and reading parameters off a printed density

The density reaches its maximum at x = mu, and the height there is 1/(sigma root 2 pi). That is the fastest way to recover sigma from a printed formula: 0.3989 at sigma 1, 0.1330 at sigma 3, 0.0266 at sigma 15.

The two dials can be read independently. The exponent is -(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2), so the coefficient of x squared gives 2 sigma squared; the constant gives sigma. They must agree, and if they do not, the printed formula is not a normal density.

Derivation On the GATE CSE 2026 Set 2 item both routes give sigma = 3: the exponent gives 2 sigma squared = 18, and the constant 1/(3 root 2 pi) gives sigma = 3. The variance is therefore 9, not 18.

Level 5 — the empirical rule as an integral

The familiar 68, 95, 99.7 are roundings of three integrals of phi between -k and k:

k = 1: 0.682689 · k = 2: 0.954500 · k = 3: 0.997300 · k = 4: 0.999937

Because the statement is about standard deviations from the mean, standardising removes both parameters, and the same three numbers hold for a weight in grams and a score out of two hundred. The two-sided tails are the complements: 0.3173, 0.0455, 0.0027 and 0.0000633.

Level 6 — linear maps and sums

If X is N(mu, sigma squared) and a, b are constants with a not zero, then aX + b is N(a mu + b, a squared sigma squared). A shift moves the centre; a scale multiplies distances from the centre, and a variance is a squared distance, so it picks up a squared factor.

If X and Y are independent and normal, X + Y is normal with the means added and the variances added. The page checks that by convolving the two densities and integrating: N(1,4) + N(2,9) gives 0.7973 at 6, and Phi(3/root 13) is 0.7973.

Derivation Standardising is the same rule with a = 1/sigma and b = -mu/sigma, which is why Z is always N(0,1) — it is the one affine map that fixes both parameters at once.

Level 7 — the assumption you are making

A density of this exact shape is a strong claim. It says the tails decay like exp(-x squared / 2), which is faster than any exponential decay, so it assigns astronomically small probability to very large deviations: beyond four sigma, 6.33 times ten to the minus five.

Where the shape comes from a theorem — a sum, an average, a measurement error that is itself a sum of small independent effects — the assumption is earned. Where it comes from someone looking at a histogram and saying "bell-shaped", it is an assumption, and the last scene shows two distributions that look plausible and are not normal.

Level 8 — one boundary before the practice set

Mean and variance do not determine a distribution. A uniform on minus root 3 to plus root 3, a two-point distribution at plus and minus 1, and a standard normal all have mean 0 and variance 1, and their probabilities of lying within one unit are 0.5774, 1 and 0.6827.

So a question that gives you only a mean and a variance has not given you a normal distribution; it has given you two moments, and a distribution has to be named before its areas can be computed.

Example 1 — identifying the family (GATE CSE 2026, Set 2)

"The probability density function f(x) of a random variable X which takes real values is f(x) = 1/(3 root 2 pi) · exp(-x squared / 18), for x in (-infinity, +infinity). Which one of the following statements is correct about the random variable X?" Options: exponential, normal, Poisson, uniform.

Answer: normal, option B. Compare with 1/(sigma root 2 pi) exp(-(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2)). The exponent gives mu = 0 and 2 sigma squared = 18, so sigma squared = 9. The constant gives sigma = 3, which agrees. So X is N(0, 9).

Why the others fail. An exponential density is zero for negative x and has a linear exponent. A Poisson variable is discrete and has a mass function, not a density. A uniform density is constant on a finite interval. Only a normal density is positive on the whole line with a quadratic exponent.

Example 2 — two curves, one equality (GATE CSE 2008, Q29)

"Let X be a random variable following normal distribution with mean +1 and variance 4. Let Y be another normal variable with mean -1 and variance unknown. If P(X at most -1) = P(Y at least 2), the standard deviation of Y is…" Options: 3, 2, root 2, 1.

Answer: 3, option A. For X, sigma is 2 and the threshold -1 is one sigma below the mean, so the left tail is Phi(-1) = 0.158655. For Y the threshold 2 is 3 above the mean, so the right tail is 1 - Phi(3/sigma).

Symmetry says Phi(-1) = 1 - Phi(1), and Phi is strictly increasing, so 3/sigma = 1 and sigma = 3. The page evaluates the right tail at sigma 2, 3 and 4: 0.066807, 0.158655 and 0.226627. Only sigma 3 matches.

Example 3 — the median of a positive part (GATE CSE 2017, Set 1, Q19)

"Let X be a Gaussian random variable with mean 0 and variance sigma squared. Let Y = max(X, 0), where max(a,b) is the maximum of a and b. The median of Y is ______."

Answer: 0. Every negative value of X is mapped to 0, so P(Y = 0) = P(X at most 0) = 0.5. Then P(Y at most 0) = 0.5 and P(Y at least 0) = 1, and both are at least one half, so 0 is a median by the definition. It is the only one: for any m above 0, P(Y at least m) = 1 - Phi(m), which is below one half.

The trap. The tempting route — "X is symmetric so its median is 0, and Y inherits it" — reaches the right number for the wrong reason and gives no way to see that the mean of Y is not 0. It is sigma / root 2 pi, which is 0.3989 for sigma 1. Derivation

Example 4 — two groups, one sigma (GATE 2016, General Aptitude, GA-6)

"Students taking an exam are divided into two groups, P and Q, such that each group has the same number of students… the mean of group P was 105, while that of group Q was 85. The standard deviation of group P was 25, while that of group Q was 5. Assuming that the marks were distributed on a normal distribution, which of the following statements will have the highest probability of being TRUE?"

Answer: most students of group Q scored in a narrower range than students in group P. One sigma around the mean is 80 to 130 for P and 80 to 90 for Q, and one sigma holds about 68 per cent of each group by the empirical rule.

Why the others fail. A smaller sigma says nothing about where the scores lie relative to the other group, so neither "no student in Q scored less than any in P" nor its reverse follows. And the median of P is its mean, 105, not the midpoint 100 of the two means. Community observation The item is a General Aptitude question, and catalogues file the same aptitude item under more than one GATE 2016 paper.

Example 5 — a tolerance question, worked in full

Packets are filled to a mean of 500 g with a standard deviation of 4 g, normally distributed. What fraction lies within 500 plus or minus 6 g?

z = 6/4 = 1.5. The answer is 2 Phi(1.5) - 1 = 2(0.933193) - 1 = 0.866386. So about 86.6 per cent.

What fraction is below 492 g? z = (492 - 500)/4 = -2, so Phi(-2) = 0.022750, about 2.3 per cent. The two questions use the same curve and different rearrangements; neither needs a new table.

Example 6 — the approximation, measured three ways

With n = 100 and p = 0.5, the exact binomial probability of at most 45 successes is 0.184101. The normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 5 gives Phi((45.5 - 50)/5) = Phi(-0.9) = 0.184060, wrong by 0.000041. Without the half-step it gives Phi(-1) = 0.158655, wrong by 0.025446 — about 600 times worse.

At n = 25 and p = 0.2 the mean is 5 and the skew is visible. The exact value at most 3 is 0.233993; the corrected normal gives 0.226627 (error 0.0074) and the uncorrected 0.158655 (error 0.0753). The half-step helps by a factor of ten and the residual is the price of a small mean. Derivation

The lesson's own checks confirm both rows, and the rule of thumb the research supports is that np and n(1-p) should both be at least five before the approximation is trusted at all.

Boundaries, counterexamples and the condition each one violates

Each card names the condition that fails, not merely the answer that changes. A worked example is not a proof, and a picture is not a proof; the numbers below are computed by the lesson's own model and re-checked against a second route.

Counterexample 1 — same mean, same variance, different curve

The uniform on minus root 3 to plus root 3 and the standard normal both have mean 0 and variance 1. Their probabilities of lying within one unit are 0.577350 and 0.682689.

Condition violated: nothing — which is the point. Two moments do not determine a distribution, so "mean 0, variance 1" is not a description of a curve.

A two-point distribution at plus and minus 1 has the same two moments and gives probability 1 for the same event.

Counterexample 2 — symmetric, unimodal in outline, not normal

Half the mass at N(-2, 1) and half at N(2, 1) gives a symmetric curve with mean 0, median 0 and variance 5.

Its probability within one unit of the centre is 0.157305, against 0.682689 for a normal with the same centre. It has two modes at plus and minus 2, so mean, median and mode are not equal.

Condition violated: a single peak at the centre, which is what "bell-shaped" was supposed to mean.

Counterexample 3 — a transform that destroys mean equals median

X is standard normal, so mean = median = 0. Let Y = max(X, 0). Then the median of Y is 0, but the mean of Y is 1/root 2 pi = 0.398942.

Condition violated: symmetry. Taking a positive part folds half the mass onto one point, and the result is neither symmetric nor continuous.

The variance of Y is 0.5 - 1/(2 pi) = 0.340845, not 0.5, because the mean is not zero.

Edge case — a variance where a sigma belongs

The GATE 2008 item gives variance 4, so sigma is 2. Using 4 in the denominator gives 1 - Phi(3/4) = 0.226627 instead of 0.158655.

The wrong answer is a printed option in some versions of the question, which is exactly why the slip survives.

Edge case — the sign a percentile carries

"Top 5 per cent" means the left-tail area is 0.95, so z = +1.6449. "Bottom 5 per cent" means the left-tail area is 0.05, so z = -1.6449.

The page inverts its own Phi by bisection and gets 1.2816 for 0.90, 1.6449 for 0.95, 1.9600 for 0.975 and 2.3263 for 0.99. The table gives the area, and the words give the side.

Closely confused pair 1 — normal against exponential

Both are written with exp. The normal has a quadratic exponent and lives on the whole line; the exponential has a linear exponent, a density lambda e to the minus lambda x, and lives on the non-negative half-line.

The distinction that matters in an exam: a printed density whose exponent contains x squared is normal, whatever else it contains. That single test answers the 2026 item without computing anything.

Closely confused pair 2 — normal approximation to the binomial, against the Poisson approximation

Both approximate a binomial. The Poisson is the right limit when n is large and p is small with np moderate; the normal is the right limit when np and n(1-p) are both comfortably large, so the mass sits away from both ends.

The distinction that matters: with n = 25 and p = 0.2, np is 5 and the normal is already off by 0.0074 even with the correction, while a Poisson with mean 5 would be closer. Read the size of np before choosing.

"N(mu, sigma squared) means the second number is sigma"

Why it tempts: the notation puts the two parameters in the same bracket, and half the textbooks write the variance while the other half write the standard deviation.

The minimal correction: the density carries 2 sigma squared in the exponent and 1/(sigma root 2 pi) in the constant. Variance 4 means sigma 2, and the 2008 item is decided by that step.

The rule: write both numbers on separate lines before substituting — variance, then its square root.

"A bell-shaped histogram means the data are normal"

Why it tempts: the eye reads symmetry and a single hump as the same thing as the family.

The minimal counterexample: the half-and-half mixture of N(-2,1) and N(2,1) is symmetric, has mean and median 0, and puts only 0.1573 within one unit of the centre against 0.6827.

The rule: the family is a statement about the tails, not only about the middle. Two moments never fix it.

"Mean equals median equals mode, so a median is a mean"

Why it tempts: the three coincide for every normal curve, and the syllabus sentence names all three together.

The minimal counterexample: for Y = max(X, 0) the median is 0 and the mean is 0.3989. A single atom is enough to separate them.

The rule: the equality is a consequence of symmetry and continuity, not a definition. Check the two inequalities when a point carries mass.

"The central limit theorem says the data become normal"

Why it tempts: the theorem is usually introduced with a picture of a bell, and the picture is drawn around the data.

The minimal correction: it is the standardised sum that converges. A single exponential observation stays exponential at every n; the gap measured on a sum of one copy is 0.1587, and that is the size of the error of pretending otherwise.

The rule: the theorem governs sums and averages, with spread sigma / root n. It says nothing about one observation.

"A four-sigma event is impossible"

Why it tempts: 0.999937 sounds like certainty, and the 68-95-99.7 rule is remembered as a bound.

The minimal correction: beyond four sigma lies 6.33 times ten to the minus five, which is about six in a hundred thousand — rare, and common enough to happen in a large system. Beyond six sigma it is about two in a thousand million.

The rule: the empirical rule is a description of areas under an assumed curve, not a guarantee about the world.

"Add the standard deviations when you add normals"

Why it tempts: the means add, so the other parameter looks as if it should add too.

The minimal correction: N(1,4) + N(2,9) is N(3,13), so the standard deviation is root 13 = 3.6056, not 2 + 3 = 5. The page checks it by convolving the two densities.

The rule: variances add for independent variables. Standard deviations add only in the degenerate case where one of them is zero.

What the paper actually asks, and the reasoning each kind demands

Six patterns cover every question this topic has produced in the papers the research reached. Each card gives the reasoning the pattern requires and the wrong approach it invites. Nothing here predicts 2027; the patterns describe past papers.

Pattern 1 — identify the family from a printed density

Shape: a formula with a constant and an exponent, and four names to choose from.

Reasoning demanded: read the support and the degree of the exponent. Quadratic on the whole line is normal; linear on the half-line is exponential; constant on an interval is uniform; a mass function is discrete.

Wrong approach: seeing "exp" and answering exponential. Seen in GATE CSE 2026 Set 2.

Pattern 2 — equate two tails of two different normals

Shape: two normals, two thresholds, one equality, and an unknown parameter.

Reasoning demanded: standardise both sides, then convert one side with symmetry so that both are left tails, then match the arguments because Phi is strictly increasing.

Wrong approach: dropping the sign that symmetry supplies, or using a variance as a sigma. Seen in GATE CSE 2008 Q29.

Pattern 3 — a median or a mode of a transformed variable

Shape: a normal variable, a transformation with a fold or a cap, and a request for a median.

Reasoning demanded: write P(Y at most m) and P(Y at least m) at the candidate, and use the definition with weak inequalities. A fold puts mass on a single point and that point usually wins.

Wrong approach: arguing from the symmetry of X. Seen in GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 Q19.

Pattern 4 — a spread comparison stated in words

Shape: two groups, two means, two standard deviations, and four verbal statements.

Reasoning demanded: translate sigma into a range, then check each statement against the ranges. Remember that a normal curve has median equal to mean, which kills the "median is the midpoint" distractor.

Wrong approach: treating a smaller sigma as a higher or lower score. Seen in GATE 2016 General Aptitude.

Pattern 5 — a statement set (MSQ)

Shape: four statements about a normal variable, of which some are true.

Reasoning demanded: test each against a definition. Usually true: symmetry, mean equal to median, variances adding for independent copies, Phi(0) = 0.5. Usually false: sigma equals the variance, the density being a probability, the tails being bounded, the distribution being determined by two moments.

Wrong approach: accepting a statement because it sounds familiar.

Pattern 6 — an approximation question with a boundary

Shape: a binomial, a threshold, and a table of Phi, sometimes with the correction and sometimes without.

Reasoning demanded: compute np and root npq, standardise with the half-step, and check that np and n(1-p) are both at least five. Where the answer is given to two places, the correction is usually the difference between the printed options.

Wrong approach: standardising k rather than k + 0.5, which costs 0.025 at n = 100 in the worked example.

MCQGATE CSE 2026, Set 2

The probability density function f(x) of a random variable X which takes real values is f(x) = 1/(3 root 2 pi) · exp(-x squared / 18), for x in (-infinity, +infinity). Which one of the following statements is correct about the random variable X?

  1. X is an exponential random variable
  2. X is a normal random variable
  3. X is a Poisson random variable
  4. X is a uniform random variable

Answer: B — X is a normal random variable, N(0, 9)

Match against 1/(sigma root 2 pi) exp(-(x - mu)^2/(2 sigma^2)). The exponent gives mu = 0 and 2 sigma^2 = 18, so sigma^2 = 9 and sigma = 3; the constant 1/(3 root 2 pi) confirms sigma = 3.

Why A is wrong. An exponential density is zero for negative x and has a linear exponent. Why C is wrong. Poisson is discrete: it has a mass function and no density. Why D is wrong. A uniform density is constant on a finite interval; this one varies over the whole line.

The paper and question text are confirmed; catalogues file this item as Q4 and as Q14. This is a CSE paper item.

MCQGATE CSE 2008, Q29

Let X be a random variable following normal distribution with mean +1 and variance 4. Let Y be another normal variable with mean -1 and variance unknown. If P(X at most -1) = P(Y at least 2), the standard deviation of Y is

  1. 3
  2. 2
  3. root 2
  4. 1

Answer: A — 3

For X, sigma = 2, so P(X at most -1) = Phi((-1 - 1)/2) = Phi(-1) = 0.158655. For Y, P(Y at least 2) = 1 - Phi(3/sigma). By symmetry Phi(-1) = 1 - Phi(1), so 3/sigma = 1 and sigma = 3.

Why B, C and D are wrong. They give right tails of 0.066807, 0.105650 and 0.001350 at sigma 2, root 2 and 1, none of which is 0.158655. The page evaluates sigma 2, 3 and 4 and only sigma 3 matches.

The paper and text are confirmed; catalogues file this item as Q29 and as Q52. This is a CSE paper item.

NATGATE CSE 2017, Set 1, Q19

Let X be a Gaussian random variable with mean 0 and variance sigma squared. Let Y = max(X, 0), where max(a, b) is the maximum of a and b. The median of Y is ______.

Answer: 0

Every negative X maps to 0, so P(Y = 0) = P(X at most 0) = 0.5. Then P(Y at most 0) = 0.5 and P(Y at least 0) = 1, so 0 satisfies both halves of the median definition. It is unique, because for m above 0 the probability P(Y at least m) = 1 - Phi(m) is below one half.

The shortcut, with its assumption. "X is symmetric, so the median is 0" gives the right number only because the fold happens at the median of X. The assumption is that the fold point is 0; fold at 1 instead and the median moves to 0 while the mean moves to about 0.51, and the argument from symmetry of X no longer applies.

Official key 0, no tolerance. This is a CSE paper item.

MCQGATE 2016, General Aptitude, GA-6

Two groups P and Q have the same number of students. Marks are out of 200. The mean of P is 105 and its standard deviation 25; the mean of Q is 85 and its standard deviation 5. Assuming the marks are normally distributed, which statement has the highest probability of being true?

  1. No student in group Q scored less than any student in group P
  2. No student in group P scored less than any student in group Q
  3. Most students of group Q scored in a narrower range than students in group P
  4. The median of the marks of group P is 100

Answer: C

One sigma is 80 to 130 for P and 80 to 90 for Q, and by the empirical rule about 68 per cent of each group lies within one sigma of its own mean. So most of Q lies in a much narrower range.

Why A and B are wrong. A smaller sigma says nothing about the ordering of two students drawn from different groups; the curves overlap. Why D is wrong. A normal curve has median equal to mean, so the median of P is 105, not the midpoint 100 of the two means.

This is a General Aptitude item; catalogues file the same aptitude question under more than one GATE 2016 paper.

OriginalNAT

X is normal with mean 50 and variance 100. Find P(X at most 65), given Phi(1.5) = 0.933193. Give the answer to 4 decimal places.

Answer: 0.9332

sigma = root 100 = 10, so z = (65 - 50)/10 = 1.5 and the answer is Phi(1.5) = 0.933193.

The trap. Using the variance 100 in the denominator gives z = 0.15 and Phi(0.15) = 0.559618, which is a completely different answer and a plausible-looking one.

OriginalNAT

Marks are normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 12. What is the 90th percentile of the marks, given z = 1.281552 for Phi(z) = 0.90? Give the answer to 2 decimal places.

Answer: 75.38

The percentile is a value, not a z: x = mu + z sigma = 60 + 1.281552 times 12 = 75.3786.

The trap. Reporting 1.2816, which is the z and not the mark. A second trap is using the bottom 10 per cent cutoff by mistake, which is 60 - 15.3786 = 44.62.

OriginalMCQ

A binomial has n = 100 and p = 0.5. Using a normal approximation, which route gives the closest value to P(X at most 45)?

  1. Phi((45 - 50)/5) = 0.158655
  2. Phi((45.5 - 50)/5) = 0.184060
  3. Phi((44.5 - 50)/5) = 0.135666
  4. Phi((45 - 50)/25) = 0.420740

Answer: B — 0.184060, against the exact 0.184101

The bar at 45 covers 44.5 to 45.5, so the boundary moves up by half a unit: z = -0.9, and Phi(-0.9) = 0.184060.

Why A is wrong. No half-step, so it is 0.0254 too small. Why C is wrong. The half-step applied in the wrong direction, which is the same error with the opposite sign. Why D is wrong. It uses the variance 25 where the standard deviation 5 belongs.

OriginalMSQ

X and Y are independent normal variables, X with mean 1 and variance 4, Y with mean 2 and variance 9. Which of the following are TRUE?

  1. X + Y is normal with mean 3
  2. Var(X + Y) = 13
  3. The standard deviation of X + Y is 5
  4. P(X + Y at most 6) = Phi(3 / root 13)

Answer: A, B and D

A: means add, 1 + 2 = 3. B: variances add for independent variables, 4 + 9 = 13. D: standardise, (6 - 3)/root 13, and the page's numerical convolution agrees to 4 decimal places.

Why C is false. The standard deviations are 2 and 3, and 2 + 3 = 5, but standard deviations do not add. The standard deviation of the sum is root 13 = 3.6056.

OriginalNAT

Averages of 25 independent observations are taken from a population with standard deviation 20. What is the standard deviation of the sample mean?

Answer: 4

The standard error is sigma / root n = 20 / 5 = 4. No normality assumption is needed for this step: the spread of a mean is sigma over root n for any independent observations with a finite variance.

The trap. 20/25 = 0.8 divides by n instead of by root n, and 20 times root 25 = 100 multiplies in the wrong direction. Only the square-root scaling is consistent with averaging two independent copies, where the variance halves.

The one-screen revision sheet

Everything on this page is either a definition or a consequence of one. If you can rebuild the right-hand column from the left, you can answer every question in the topic.

The density and its parameters

f(x) = 1/(sigma root 2 pi) exp(-(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2))

E[X] = mu · Var(X) = sigma squared · SD = sigma

Peak at x = mu, height 1/(sigma root 2 pi)

Positive on the whole line, symmetric about mu, no closed form for its integral.

Standardising, and Phi

z = (x - mu)/sigma, and P(X at most x) = Phi(z)

Phi(0) = 0.5 · Phi(1) = 0.8413 · Phi(2) = 0.9772 · Phi(3) = 0.9987

Phi(-z) = 1 - Phi(z) · P(a<Z<b) = Phi(b) - Phi(a) · P(|Z|<z) = 2 Phi(z) - 1

Percentiles: 1.2816 at 0.90, 1.6449 at 0.95, 1.9600 at 0.975, 2.3263 at 0.99.

Areas worth knowing

Within 1 sigma 0.682689 · within 2 sigma 0.954500 · within 3 sigma 0.997300

Two-sided tails: 0.3173, 0.0455, 0.0027, and 6.33 times ten to the minus five at four sigma

Sum of independent normals: means add, variances add. Standard deviations do not add.

The four-step procedure

  1. Write the parameters — mu, then sigma, taking the square root of any variance.
  2. Standardise — subtract the mean, divide by sigma.
  3. Read the words for the tail — left, right or band — and use symmetry before arithmetic.
  4. Name the assumption — is this curve given, derived from a sum, or assumed?

Six traps, one line each

  • Divide by sigma, never by the variance.
  • A quadratic exponent means normal, not exponential.
  • Phi is always a left tail; the words fix the sign.
  • Mean = median = mode needs symmetry and continuity; an atom breaks it.
  • Variances add for independent normals; standard deviations do not.
  • The spread of a mean is sigma over root n, not sigma over n.
