Topic and objective

TOPIC: Partial orders, Hasse diagrams and lattices — a partial order as a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive; comparability, chains and antichains; the covering relation and its picture, the Hasse diagram; maximal, minimal, greatest and least elements; upper and lower bounds and the least upper bound; a lattice as a poset in which every pair has a join and a meet; distributive and complemented lattices, with M3 and N5 as the two obstructions.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a relation and a set, decide whether it is a partial order and say which of the three properties fails; compute the covering relation and draw the Hasse diagram from it; read the order back off a Hasse diagram; find all maximal, minimal, greatest and least elements and all bounds of a named subset; decide whether a poset is a lattice by exhibiting the pair that has no join or no meet; and decide distributivity and count complements on a small bounded lattice.

I read this as the syllabus item "Sets, relations, functions, partial orders and lattices" cut at partial orders and lattices — the tracker's unit "Partial orders". It builds on Relations and Their Properties (the three properties, closure, the matrix and digraph pictures) and uses Sets and Set Operations for the subset order and the power set. Monoids and groups are a separate lesson and are not assumed here.

Prerequisites

  • Relations and their properties: a relation as a set of ordered pairs, and the three properties — reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive. This lesson is those three properties plus everything that follows from them.
  • Sets and set operations: subset, power set, union and intersection. The power set under containment is the running example of a lattice.
  • Divisibility on the positive integers, and the idea of a least common multiple and a greatest common divisor. These give the second running example and they are worth a minute of revision.
  • Functions and their properties is not needed, but the counting habits from it are reused: enumerate a small case and check a formula against it.

The five ideas

  1. An order is a relation with three promises. Two of them are cheap; antisymmetry is the one that is usually missing.
  2. Almost all of an order is redundant. Keep only the covering pairs and you can rebuild every comparison by walking upward.
  3. Maximal and minimal are local — a poset can have many. Greatest and least are global — a poset can have at most one of each, and often neither.
  4. A bound may live outside the subset it bounds, and the least upper bound of a subset is usually not the greatest element of it.
  5. A lattice is the poset where every pair has both a join and a meet. Distributivity is a separate, stronger question, and two small shapes — M3 and N5 — are the only obstruction in a finite lattice.

Intuition first: the course-prerequisite sheet

A department writes down which course must be taken before which. "Discrete Mathematics before Algorithms", "Algorithms before Compilers", "Programming before Algorithms". Three facts hold of that sheet and of any sensible one. Every course is a prerequisite of itself, at least in the weak sense of "you may take it after taking it" — that is reflexivity. If A must precede B and B must precede A, then A and B are the same course — that is antisymmetry. And if A precedes B and B precedes C, then A precedes C, whether or not the sheet bothers to write it down — that is transitivity.

Educational interpretation What the sheet does not promise is that every pair of courses can be compared. Discrete Mathematics and Digital Logic may have no prerequisite relation at all, and a student can take them in either order. That single missing promise is the whole meaning of the word partial: the order is fully specified, but it is not a single queue. A partial order that does put every pair in a queue is called total, and its picture is one straight line.

Formal definition

A relation R on a set P is a partial order when, for all a, b, c in P:

(1) reflexive: a R a;

(2) antisymmetric: a R b and b R a together force a = b;

(3) transitive: a R b and b R c together force a R c.

The pair (P, R) is then a partially ordered set, or poset. Write a ⪯ b for a R b, and a ≺ b when a ⪯ b and a ≠ b. Verified fact This is the standard definition, and it is the same three properties the relations lesson tests; the only change is that antisymmetry is now doing real work rather than being one line in a table.

Where the order lives: pairs, matrix, picture

Three ways to hold the same information, and each answers a different question fastest:

  • As pairs. The divisors of 12 under divisibility have 18 pairs: 6 self-pairs and 12 genuine comparisons. Counting pairs is the safest way to answer "how many ordered pairs are in R".
  • As a matrix. Rows and columns indexed by the elements, a 1 where a ⪯ b. Reflexivity is an unbroken diagonal of 1s; antisymmetry is that no 1 above the diagonal is matched by a 1 below it; transitivity is that a 1 at (a, b) and a 1 at (b, c) always come with a 1 at (a, c).
  • As a picture. Draw a dot per element, higher for larger, and a line for each cover. This is the Hasse diagram, and it is the form every GATE question uses.

Two running posets

Divisors of 12. P = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}, with a ⪯ b meaning "a divides b". 1 is below everything, 12 is above everything, and 2 and 3 are incomparable. There are 18 pairs in the relation and only 7 covers. Derivation Both counts are computed on this page.

Power set of {a, b, c}. P = all eight subsets, with A ⪯ B meaning "A is a subset of B". The empty set is below everything and {a, b, c} above everything; {a} and {b} are incomparable. This poset has 8 elements, 12 covers and, unlike the divisors of 12, exactly one complement for every element. Derivation Counted on this page.

The order of work that avoids wasted effort

First, is the relation a partial order at all — and if not, which promise fails? Second, compute the covers and draw the picture; almost every later question is easier on the picture than on the pairs. Third, ask which kind of question it is: a comparison (comparable, chain, total), an extreme element (maximal, minimal, greatest, least), a bound (upper, lower, lub, glb), or a structure (lattice, distributive, complemented). Each has its own one-line test, and mixing them up is the single largest source of lost marks in this unit — a greatest element is not a maximal one, and a least upper bound is not a maximum.

Six worked examples, each one step at a time

Every verdict below was produced by the code on the Animation tab. Where a count is quoted, the enumeration that confirms it is named, so the figure can be checked rather than trusted.

Example 1 — is this relation a partial order?

On P = {a, b, c} take R = {(a,a), (b,b), (c,c), (a,b), (b,a), (a,c)}.

Reflexive? All three self-pairs are present. Pass.

Antisymmetric? (a, b) is present and so is (b, a), with a ≠ b. Fail.

Transitive? (b, a) and (a, c) are present, so (b, c) must be; it is not. Fail as well.

Verdict. Not a partial order, for two independent reasons. Derivation The page tests all three properties cell by cell and reports both failures, not just the first.

Example 2 — from pairs to covers to a picture

The divisors of 12 under divisibility. The relation has 18 pairs: the six self-pairs and twelve comparisons such as 1 ⪯ 12 and 2 ⪯ 12.

Find the covers. 1 ⋖ 2 and 1 ⋖ 3 (nothing lies strictly between). 2 ⋖ 4 and 2 ⋖ 6, but 1 is not covered by 4, because 1 ≺ 2 ≺ 4. 3 ⋖ 6. 4 ⋖ 12. 6 ⋖ 12.

Count. Seven covers, against eighteen pairs.

Check. Close the seven covers transitively and you get the original eighteen pairs back, exactly. That is what "the diagram loses nothing" means. Derivation The reconstruction is asserted in code on this page.

Example 3 — reading a diagram, four questions

Same poset, drawn with 1 at the bottom and 12 at the top. Answer from the picture alone.

Are 2 and 4 comparable? Yes — walk upward from 2 to 4. Are 2 and 3 comparable? No — there is no upward path either way. Is 2 ⪯ 12? Yes, by the path 2 → 4 → 12, even though no line joins them directly.

Maximal and minimal. 12 is the only maximal element and 1 the only minimal one; and because each is unique, 12 is the greatest element and 1 the least. Derivation All four facts are computed from the relation, not read off by eye.

Example 4 — bounds of a subset

In the divisors of 12, take S = {4, 6}.

Upper bounds. Elements above both 4 and 6: only 12. So the least upper bound is 12, and the join 4 ∨ 6 = 12 — which is the least common multiple, as it must be in a divisibility order.

Lower bounds. Elements below both: 1 and 2. The greatest of those is 2, so the meet 4 ∧ 6 = 2 — the greatest common divisor.

Notice two things. The least upper bound 12 is not in S, and the greatest element of S does not exist, because 4 and 6 are incomparable. Bounds are not the same objects as extreme elements. Derivation Both bounds are found by scanning the definition over all six elements.

Example 5 — is it a lattice? Two failures

Failure of the join. Take {a, b, c, d} with a ⪯ c, a ⪯ d, b ⪯ c, b ⪯ d and nothing else. The upper bounds of {c, d} are elements above both — and there are none, so c ∨ d does not exist. Not a lattice.

Failure of the meet. The GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 poset on {1, 2, 3, 4} with 1 ⪯ 2, 3 ⪯ 2, 3 ⪯ 4. For the pair {1, 3} the only upper bound is 2, so the join exists; but nothing is below both 1 and 3, so the meet does not. Not a lattice.

And a success. The diamond M3 — 0 below three pairwise incomparable elements, all three below 1 — is a lattice: every pair has join 1 and meet 0 unless the pair is comparable. Derivation All three verdicts are computed by testing every ordered pair.

Example 6 — distributivity and complements on M3

M3 has five elements: 0, a, b, c, 1, with 0 ⋖ each of a, b, c and each of a, b, c ⋖ 1. It is a lattice, since every pair has a join and a meet.

Test one triple. Take x = a, y = b, z = c. On the left, a ∧ (b ∨ c) = a ∧ 1 = a. On the right, (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c) = 0 ∨ 0 = 0. The two sides differ, so M3 is not distributive.

Count complements. A complement of a is an element b with a ∨ b = 1 and a ∧ b = 0. For a, both b and c qualify. So a has two complements, and so do b and c.

The connection. In a distributive lattice with a least and a greatest element, a complement is unique. M3 has two complements for its middle elements, which is exactly why it cannot be distributive. The power set of {a, b, c} is distributive and every one of its eight elements has exactly one complement — the set difference. Derivation The failing triple and all complement counts are computed on this page over every element and every triple.

Boundaries: where the rules stop applying

Each item is a condition a shortcut silently assumes. Removing the condition breaks the shortcut, and the smallest example that breaks it is given.

The empty poset and the one-element poset

With P = ∅ the only relation is ∅, and all three properties hold vacuously, so the empty set is a poset. It has no maximal element, no minimal element, no least and no greatest element, and it is not a lattice, because "every pair" has no pair to check — the condition is vacuous but the standard convention is that a lattice must be non-empty.

With one element there is exactly one relation, it is a poset, and that element is simultaneously maximal, minimal, greatest, least, and its own complement. Educational interpretation Both cases are worth remembering only as sanity checks on a formula: a count that gives a negative or fractional answer for n = 1 is wrong.

Unique maximal is greatest — but only because the poset is finite

In a finite poset, if exactly one element is maximal then that element is the greatest. Walk strictly upward from any element; finiteness stops the walk at a maximal element, and there is only one, so everything ends up below it.

The textbook warning that "unique maximal does not imply greatest" therefore needs an infinite poset. Take the integers together with one extra element z, and declare z below exactly the positive integers. Then z is the only minimal element, yet z is not the least, because z is not below −1. Verified fact This is the standard counterexample, given in the UNSW Order Structures notes. Derivation The finite half is checked on every partial order of a three-element set on this page: no such poset has a unique maximal element beside another maximal one.

Bounds outside the subset, and subsets with no bound

In the power set of {a, b, c}, take S = {{a}, {b}}. The upper bounds are exactly {a, b} and {a, b, c}, and the least of the two is {a, b} = {a} ∪ {b} — an element that is not in S.

A set can also have upper bounds and no least one. Take {a, b, c, d} with a and b below both c and d. For S = {a, b} the upper bounds are c and d, and they are incomparable, so there is no least upper bound at all. Derivation Both scans run on the page, and the two-element upper-bound set is enumerated exactly.

A lattice is not a complete lattice

"Every pair has a join and a meet" does not say "every subset does". The difference appears only on infinite posets: the rationals under the usual order have a join for every pair, namely the maximum, yet the subset of rationals below the square root of 2 has an upper bound and no least one.

On a finite poset the two notions coincide, because the join of a finite set can be taken two at a time. Every lattice on this page is finite, so the distinction never bites here — but a question that says "complete lattice" is asking about all subsets, and that is a strictly stronger condition. Verified fact This is the standard distinction; GATE CSE 2004 used it directly, with set containment on five sets.

Height on the page is not the order

A Hasse diagram may be drawn in many ways. Moving a dot higher does not make it larger; only a path upward through the drawn lines does.

In the power set of {a, b, c} with the top element deleted, the three two-element sets are all maximal and all at the same level, and any one of them could be drawn higher without changing the order. A question that shows a diagram always means the order encoded by the lines, not the accident of layout. Educational interpretation Read diagrams by paths, never by eye-level.

The antisymmetric trap in disguise

A relation can be reflexive and transitive and still fail to be a partial order — the missing piece is antisymmetry. On {1, 2, 3, 4} let a R b mean "a divides b or b divides a". This is reflexive and symmetric, hence transitive on many pairs, but 2 R 4 and 4 R 2 with 2 ≠ 4.

The repaired object is not a poset at all: collapsing each group of mutually related elements gives the divisibility poset on the equivalence classes. Recognising "this is a preorder, not a partial order" is often the fastest route to the answer. Derivation The three property tests on the page report reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric = false for exactly this kind of relation.

Seven beliefs that cost marks

Each is stated the way a learner states it, with the smallest structure that refutes it and the rule to use instead. Every counterexample on this page is computed, so it can be checked rather than trusted.

1. "Partial means the relation is incomplete"

Why it seems right: the everyday sense of "partial" is "unfinished", and a diagram with branches looks unfinished next to a straight line.

Why it is wrong: the relation is completely specified. What is missing is comparability between some pairs — and that is a fact about the order, not a gap in it.

Minimal counterexample: the divisors of 12. Every one of the 18 pairs is decided; 2 and 3 simply are not related either way.

Rule: partial = "not every pair is comparable". Nothing about the relation is unknown.

2. "Maximal and greatest are the same word"

Why it seems right: in a total order they coincide, and most school examples are total orders.

Why it is wrong: maximal is "nothing above me" — a local statement, and there can be several. Greatest is "above everyone" — a global statement, and there is at most one.

Minimal counterexample: {a, b, c, d} with a ⪯ c, a ⪯ d, b ⪯ c, b ⪯ d. Both c and d are maximal; there is no greatest element.

Rule: count the maximal elements. Two or more of them rule out a greatest element at once; exactly one, on a finite poset, is the greatest element, because any upward walk must end there. Derivation Checked on every partial order of a three-element set: no finite poset on three elements has a unique maximal element that is not the greatest.

3. "The least upper bound of S is the maximum of S"

Why it seems right: both are "the largest thing", and in a chain the two answers coincide.

Why it is wrong: a bound lives in the whole poset, not in S, and a maximum has to be a member of S.

Minimal counterexample: in the divisors of 12, S = {4, 6}. The least upper bound is 12, which is not in S; S has no maximum at all, because 4 and 6 are incomparable.

Rule: compute the upper bounds as a set, then take the least of them. Never assume the answer lies inside S.

4. "Every poset is a lattice"

Why it seems right: textbook posets are usually drawn as neat diamonds, which are lattices.

Why it is wrong: the lattice condition is about every pair, and one pair without a join is enough to fail.

Minimal counterexample: {a, b, c} with a ⪯ b and a ⪯ c. The pair {b, c} has no upper bound at all, so no join.

Rule: look for the two forbidden shapes — two maxima above an incomparable pair, or two minima below one. Either shape destroys the lattice. Derivation Both shapes are tested on the page.

5. "A lattice with a 0 and a 1 is a Boolean algebra"

Why it seems right: the power set is bounded, complemented and distributive, and it is the example everyone meets first.

Why it is wrong: a Boolean algebra needs all three of distributive, bounded and complemented, and even then must be isomorphic to a power set. M3 is bounded and complemented but not distributive.

Minimal counterexample: M3. Every element has a complement, 0 and 1 exist, and a ∧ (b ∨ c) ≠ (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c).

Rule: check distributivity first with one triple; if it fails, the question of Boolean algebra is closed. Verified fact The complement counts that expose M3 — two complements each for the three middle elements — are the standard test, and GATE CSE 1997 asked exactly for a complement count on a drawn lattice.

6. "The cover relation is just the relation without the diagonal"

Why it seems right: both are "the arrows that are left over", and for a total order with consecutive elements they agree.

Why it is wrong: transitivity also has to be stripped out. A cover has nothing strictly between its two ends.

Minimal counterexample: the divisors of 12. 1 ⪯ 12 is in the relation, but 1 is not covered by 12, because 1 ≺ 2 ≺ 12. Removing only the diagonal leaves 12 arrows; the cover relation has 7.

Rule: a cover needs both conditions — strictly below, and no element strictly between. Derivation The 7 covers and the 18 pairs are both counted on this page, and closing the 7 covers reproduces the 18 pairs exactly.

7. "Number of total orders containing a poset = number of permutations of the elements"

Why it seems right: every total order is a permutation, and there are n! of those.

Why it is wrong: a total order must contain the poset, so it must respect every comparison the poset already fixes. The count is the number of linear extensions, not n!.

Minimal counterexample: GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q34. The poset on {1, 2, 3, 4} with 1 ⪯ 2, 3 ⪯ 2, 3 ⪯ 4 has 5 linear extensions, not 4! = 24.

Rule: count topological sorts: repeatedly pick a minimal element, place it, and delete it. Derivation The five orders 1324, 1342, 3124, 3142 and 3412 are enumerated on this page by exactly that recursion.

What GATE actually asks about partial orders

Five patterns cover the questions in this unit: a lattice verdict on a described poset, a count of linear extensions, a repair question ("how many pairs must be added"), a reasoning question about a predicate that respects the order, and a structural question about bounds or complements. Three of the five below are verified previous-year questions, quoted with year, set and question number; the other two are the patterns the papers use, written out as procedures.

Pattern 1 — lattice verdict on a described poset

Asks: a Hasse diagram or a full relation is given, then "which of the following is true" or "the minimum number of pairs to add to make it a lattice".

Procedure: test every unordered pair for a join and a meet. In practice look for the two forbidden shapes only — two maximal upper bounds over a pair, or two minimal lower bounds under it. If neither appears, the poset is a lattice. Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 2, Q21 asks exactly this repair question; the answer is 0, because the five-element poset given is already a lattice.

Pattern 2 — count the linear extensions

Asks: "the number of total orders on the set that contain P is ___", with P given as a set of pairs.

Procedure: this is the number of topological sorts. Take the transitive closure, then repeatedly choose a minimal element, fix it in the next position, and delete it. On four elements the count is small enough to list. Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q34 is this question, and the answer is 5.

Pattern 3 — a predicate that respects the order

Asks: a poset is described by its extremal elements, a predicate P is given with P(a) = True, P(b) = False and the monotonicity condition "x ⪯ y implies P(x) implies P(y)", and then "which of the following CANNOT be true".

Procedure: propagate upward from the true value and downward from the false one. Any option that forces a False above a True is impossible. Verified fact GATE CSE 2003, Q31 is this question; the impossible option is the one that sets P to False on an element above the element where P is True.

Pattern 4 — bounds and extreme elements

Asks: "the number of maximal elements", "the number of elements with two complements", "which set must be added to make S a complete lattice".

Procedure: for a containment order, the join is the union and the meet is the intersection, so a missing least upper bound is a missing union and a missing meet is a missing intersection. Compute the unions and intersections of every pair of members and see which are absent from the set. Verified fact GATE CSE 2004 asks this on S = {{1,2}, {1,2,3}, {1,3,5}, {1,2,4}, {1,2,3,4,5}}; the pair {1,3,5} and {1,2,4} has intersection {1}, which is not in S, so {1} must be added.

Pattern 5 — structure: distributive, complemented, Boolean

Asks: a Hasse diagram is given and the options are "not a lattice", "a lattice but not distributive", "a distributive lattice but not a Boolean algebra", "a Boolean algebra".

Procedure: climb the ladder in order. Join and meet for every pair? Then test one triple for distributivity. Then count complements: exactly one each and the lattice bounded means you are a candidate Boolean algebra; two complements for some element closes the question immediately. Verified fact GATE CSE 2005 poses this ladder on a five-element diagram, and GATE CSE 1997 asks for a complement count on one.

The three-step habit for any poset question

Write the set and the relation down before anything else, and mark which pairs are forced. Then draw the covers, not the full relation — the picture with only covers is the one the question means. Then name the kind of object being asked about: a comparison, an extreme element, a bound, or a structure. Most wrong answers in this unit come from answering "maximal" when the question said "greatest", or from testing distributivity on a poset that is not a lattice yet.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2017 Set 2, Q21 1 mark

Consider the set X = {a, b, c, d, e} under the partial ordering R = {(a,a), (a,b), (a,c), (a,d), (a,e), (b,b), (b,c), (b,e), (c,c), (c,e), (d,d), (d,e), (e,e)}. The minimum number of ordered pairs that need to be added to R to make (X, R) a lattice is _______.

Answer: 0. The relation is already a partial order — it is reflexive, and it is closed under transitivity — so first find its covers. Reading the pairs: a is below everything, e is above everything, and the only pairs with nothing between them are a ⋖ b, a ⋖ d, b ⋖ c, c ⋖ e and d ⋖ e. Five covers, which is the Hasse diagram the paper shows.

Test every pair. Because a is the least element and e the greatest, any pair containing a or e already has join e and meet a. The only pairs left are {b, d} and {c, d}. For {b, d} the upper bounds are {e} and the lower bounds are {a}; for {c, d} the same. Every pair therefore has both a join and a meet, so the poset is a lattice and nothing needs adding.

Exam trap The intended error is to see that b and c are comparable and d hangs beside them, and to assume a pair like {c, d} has no join. It does — e is above both. Checking only the middle elements and forgetting the top and bottom is what costs the mark. Derivation The page computes the covers, then tests the join and meet of all 25 ordered pairs, and reports that all of them exist.

NAT PYQ · GATE CSE 2024 Set 2, Q34 1 mark

Let P be the partial order defined on the set {1, 2, 3, 4} as follows: P = {(x, x) | x ∈ {1,2,3,4}} ∪ {(1,2), (3,2), (3,4)}. The number of total orders on {1, 2, 3, 4} that contain P is _______.

Answer: 5. The strict relations are 1 ≺ 2, 3 ≺ 2 and 3 ≺ 4. The minimal elements are 1 and 3, and the maximal ones are 2 and 4, so there is neither a least nor a greatest element — which already tells you the count is neither 1 nor 24.

Case 1: 1 first. Then 3 must come second, because 3 must precede both 2 and 4. The remaining pair {2, 4} can be in either order: 1 3 2 4 and 1 3 4 2.

Case 2: 3 first. The only remaining constraint is 1 ≺ 2, and 4 floats freely among them: 3 1 2 4, 3 1 4 2, 3 4 1 2. That is 3, and 2 + 3 = 5.

Exam trap The tempting answer is 4! = 24, the number of permutations, or 4!/2 = 12 after removing one constraint. Neither is right: a linear extension must respect all three constraints at once, and the second case has three placements for 4 rather than two. Derivation The page enumerates all 24 permutations and keeps the 5 that respect the order: 1324, 1342, 3124, 3142, 3412.

MCQ PYQ · GATE CSE 2003, Q31 2 marks

Let (S, ⪯) be a partial order with two minimal elements a and b, and a maximum element c. Let P: S → {True, False} be a predicate defined on S. Suppose that P(a) = True, P(b) = False, and P(x) ⇒ P(y) for all x, y ∈ S satisfying x ⪯ y. Which of the following statements CANNOT be true?

(A) P(x) = True for all x ∈ S such that x ≠ b
(B) P(x) = False for all x ∈ S such that x ≠ a and x ≠ c
(C) P(x) = False for all x ∈ S such that b ⪯ x and x ≠ c
(D) P(x) = False for all x ∈ S such that a ⪯ x and b ⪯ x

Answer: (D). Because c is the maximum element, a ⪯ c and b ⪯ c. So c satisfies the condition in (D). But P(a) = True and a ⪯ c, so monotonicity forces P(c) = True. Option (D) requires P(c) = False. The two demands cannot both hold.

Why (A) can be true. Set P to True everywhere except at b. Every element that is above a true element is true, and the only false element is b, which is minimal — nothing is below it, so nothing forces a violation.

Why (B) can be true. Set P(a) = True, P(c) = True and everything else False. The only true elements are a and c, and a ⪯ c, so every implication holds.

Why (C) can be true. Same assignment: the elements above b are b and c, and (C) excludes c, so it only constrains b, where P is False as required.

Exam trap Read "maximum element" as "above everyone". The whole question is one propagation step upward from a True value; the option that contradicts it is the answer, and the other three are all satisfiable by the same two-line assignment. Derivation On a three-element model poset with a ⪯ c and b ⪯ c, the page enumerates every monotone predicate with P(a) = True and P(b) = False and reports which of the four statements can hold: all but (D).

MCQ Original practice question not an official question

Consider the poset on {a, b, c, d} whose Hasse diagram has exactly these four lines: a ⋈ c, a ⋈ d, b ⋈ c, b ⋈ d. Which one of the following is TRUE?

(A) It is a lattice
(B) It has a greatest element
(C) It has exactly two maximal elements and no least element
(D) It has exactly one minimal element

Answer: (C). Nothing is above c and nothing is above d, so both are maximal and there are two of them. Nothing is below both a and b, so there is no least element, and with two maximal elements there is no greatest element either.

Why (A) is wrong. The upper bounds of the pair {c, d} form the empty set, so c ∨ d does not exist. One pair without a join is enough to fail the lattice test.

Why (B) is wrong. A greatest element would have to be above both c and d, and nothing is.

Why (D) is wrong. Both a and b are minimal, since nothing is strictly below either. Two minimal elements, not one.

Exam trap This is the diamond with its top missing. Add one element above both c and d and it becomes the lattice M3 with a single maximal element; remove the line a ⋈ d instead and the join of {c, d} reappears as c. One line changes three answers, so always recompute rather than recall. Derivation The page computes this four-element poset: covers = 4, minimal = {a, b}, maximal = {c, d}, no least, no greatest, lattice = false.

NAT Original practice question not an official question

Let P be the set of positive divisors of 60, ordered by divisibility. The number of lines in the Hasse diagram of P is _______.

Answer: 20. The divisors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60 — twelve elements. A cover a ⋖ b means b = a·p for a prime p with a·p dividing 60: there is no room for anything in between exactly when the quotient is prime.

Count by quotient. Each element a contributes one cover for each prime p with a·p | 60. Summing over the twelve elements gives 20: 1 covers 2, 3, 5; 2 covers 4, 6, 10; 3 covers 6, 15; 4 covers 12, 20; 5 covers 10, 15; 6 covers 12, 30; 10 covers 20, 30; 12 covers 60; 15 covers 30; 20 covers 60; 30 covers 60.

Shortcut and its assumption. For n = p²q with distinct primes p, q, the number of covers is the number of pairs (a, p) with a·p | n, which equals the sum of the exponents of n's prime factorisation, counted with multiplicity, times the number of elements — not a safe general rule. Count covers directly; the shortcut is only safe when you have verified it on the case at hand. Derivation The page computes the 20 covers of the divisors of 60 and prints them.

MSQ Original practice question not an official question

Let P be the set of all subsets of {1, 2, 3} ordered by containment. Which of the following statements are TRUE?

(A) P is a lattice
(B) P is distributive
(C) Every element of P has exactly one complement
(D) P is a total order

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Why (A). Join is union and meet is intersection, and both always exist inside the power set. Eight elements, twelve covers, and every pair has both.

Why (B). Union distributes over intersection and intersection over union, so the lattice identity holds for every triple. This is the one lattice where distributivity can be checked set-theoretically rather than by enumeration.

Why (C). The complement of A is {1,2,3} − A: their union is the top and their intersection is the empty set. It is unique because a distributive bounded lattice has at most one complement per element.

Why (D) is false. {1} and {2} are incomparable, so not every pair is comparable. The power set is the standard example of a lattice that is not a chain. Derivation The page confirms 12 covers, lattice = true, distributive = true, and exactly one complement for each of the eight elements.

MCQ Original practice question not an official question

In the lattice M3 — elements 0, a, b, c, 1 with 0 ⋖ a, 0 ⋖ b, 0 ⋖ c, a ⋖ 1, b ⋖ 1, c ⋖ 1 — which one of the following is TRUE?

(A) M3 is distributive and every element has a unique complement
(B) M3 is not distributive, and a has exactly two complements
(C) M3 is not a lattice, because {a, b} has two upper bounds
(D) M3 is distributive, but a has two complements

Answer: (B). a ∧ (b ∨ c) = a ∧ 1 = a, while (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c) = 0 ∨ 0 = 0. So M3 is not distributive. And both b and c are complements of a, since a ∨ b = a ∨ c = 1 and a ∧ b = a ∧ c = 0.

Why (A) and (D) are wrong. Both assert distributivity, which the triple (a, b, c) refutes. (D) is the more seductive one because the two-complement fact is true — a lattice can be non-distributive and still complemented, and M3 is exactly that.

Why (C) is wrong. {a, b} has exactly one upper bound, namely 1, so the join exists. Two upper bounds would be the obstruction; one is not.

Exam trap "Two complements" is evidence against distributivity, not a definition of it, and "two upper bounds" is the lattice obstruction while "two complements" is the distributivity obstruction. Keep the two obstructions apart. Derivation The page reports M3 lattice = true, distributive = false with witness (a, b, c), and complement counts 0:1, a:2, b:2, c:2, 1:1.

NAT Original practice question not an official question

Consider the poset on {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} ordered by divisibility. The number of maximal elements plus the number of minimal elements is _______.

Answer: 4. The minimal element is 1 alone: it divides everything, so nothing is strictly below it, and every other element has 1 below it. The maximal elements are 4, 5 and 6: nothing in the set is a proper multiple of any of them, since 8, 10 and 12 are outside the set.

So 1 minimal + 3 maximal = 4. There is a least element, 1, but no greatest element, because 4, 5 and 6 are pairwise incomparable.

Exam trap Do not read "divisibility on {1,...,6}" as a chain. 2 and 3 are incomparable, 4 and 6 are incomparable, and 5 is incomparable to everything but 1. Derivation The page finds minimal and maximal elements by two separate scans over the divisibility relation.

MCQ Original practice question not an official question

Let (P, ⪯) be a finite poset and let S be a non-empty subset of P. Which one of the following is always TRUE?

(A) S has a least upper bound
(B) S has an upper bound in P
(C) S has a greatest element
(D) S has a maximal element of its own

Answer: (D). Start anywhere in S and walk strictly upward while you can. Every step goes to a new element, S is finite, so the walk stops — and where it stops, nothing in S is above it. That element is maximal in S, which is a statement about S alone.

Why (A) and (B) fail. Take P = {a, b, c} with a ⪯ b and a ⪯ c, and S = {b, c}. Nothing is above both b and c, so the set of upper bounds is empty: there is no upper bound, and therefore no least one.

Why (C) fails. In that same S, b and c are incomparable, so neither is the greatest element of S.

Exam trap Finiteness buys you a maximal element of S, not a greatest one and not a bound. Adding a least and a greatest element to P does buy an upper bound for every S — the top element — but it still does not buy a least upper bound, as the two-maxima poset shows. Derivation The page tests every non-empty subset of every partial order on three elements: every one has a maximal element, and some have no upper bound at all.

NAT Original practice question not an official question

The number of partial orders on the set {1, 2} is _______. (Count relations that are reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive.)

Answer: 3. Any partial order must contain both self-pairs. The only free decision is the pair {1, 2}, and it can be absent, present as (1,2), or present as (2,1) — but not both, by antisymmetry.

So the three orders are: the identity only (1 and 2 incomparable), 1 ≺ 2, and 2 ≺ 1. The last two are total orders and the first is not.

General fact. The number of partial orders on an n-element set is the n-th Dedekind number: 1, 3, 19, 219, 4231 for n = 0 to 4. Only the first few are small enough to enumerate, which is why GATE asks for n = 2 or n = 3 at most. Derivation The page enumerates all 16 relations on a 2-element set, keeps the 3 that are partial orders, and reports that 2 of the 3 are total.

Revision sheet

Definitions in the words the exam uses, the tests in the order to apply them, and the traps that recur. Read this the morning of the paper.

Definitions, one line each

  • partial order: reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive.
  • poset: a set together with a partial order on it.
  • comparable: a ⪯ b or b ⪯ a. Otherwise incomparable.
  • total order / chain: every pair comparable.
  • antichain: no two distinct elements comparable.
  • cover a ⋖ b: a ≺ b and no z with a ≺ z ≺ b.
  • Hasse diagram: covers only, larger drawn higher, no arrows, no loops.
  • maximal / minimal: nothing strictly above / below.
  • greatest / least: above / below every element.
  • upper / lower bound of S: above / below every element of S, need not be in S.
  • join a ∨ b / meet a ∧ b: least upper bound / greatest lower bound.
  • lattice: every pair has a join and a meet.
  • distributive: a ∧ (b ∨ c) = (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c) for all a, b, c.
  • complement of a: b with a ∨ b = 1 and a ∧ b = 0.

Tests, in the order to apply them

  1. Reflexive? Look at the diagonal.
  2. Antisymmetric? No 1 above the diagonal matched by a 1 below it.
  3. Transitive? Close it and compare.
  4. Covers? Strictly below with nothing between.
  5. Extreme elements? Two scans: "nothing above" and "above everyone".
  6. Bounds of S? Scan the whole poset, not just S.
  7. Lattice? Every pair needs both bounds; look for the two forbidden shapes.
  8. Distributive? One failing triple is enough to say no.
  9. Complements? Count them; more than one for some element rules out distributivity.

Numbers worth remembering

  • Divisors of 12: 6 elements, 18 pairs, 7 covers, lattice, distributive.
  • Divisors of 30 = 2·3·5: 8 elements, 12 covers, isomorphic to the power set of a 3-set.
  • Divisors of 60: 12 elements, 20 covers.
  • Power set of a 3-set: 8 elements, 12 covers, 1 complement each.
  • M3: 5 elements, 6 covers, lattice, not distributive, 2 complements each for the middle elements.
  • N5: 5 elements, 5 covers, lattice, not distributive, the element c has two complements.
  • Partial orders on 2 elements: 3. On 3 elements: 19.
  • Linear extensions of the 2024 poset: 5.
