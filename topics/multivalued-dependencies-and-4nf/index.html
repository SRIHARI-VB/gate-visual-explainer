Topic and objective

TOPIC: Multivalued dependencies, fourth normal form, and the chase — the rung of the normalisation ladder that sits immediately above BCNF and that functional dependencies cannot reach.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State the multivalued dependency $$X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ in both of its standard forms and know they are one definition; prove to yourself that an FD is an MVD and that the converse fails; decide whether a given schema is in 4NF by computing its keys from the functional dependencies only; split a violating schema into two fragments and know why that split cannot lose a tuple; and test a decomposition of three or more fragments with the chase instead of guessing.

I read the topic as one dependency and one test: the dependency says two multi-valued facts are independent, and the test asks whether its left side is already a key. Everything else — the rules, the decomposition, the chase — is machinery around those two sentences. The page computes every number it shows and reports how many of its own checks passed.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A, with a caveat worth knowing. The GATE 2027 CS syllabus, Section 9 (Databases), names normal forms. Fourth normal form is a named rung of that ladder, but the syllabus document does not list 4NF or multivalued dependencies separately. So the topic is inside the declared boundary through "normal forms", and it is not named in its own right.

Verified fact Direct MVD questions exist on GATE papers, but not on a GATE CSE paper that I could verify: GATE IT 2007 asked which of four implications relating functional and multivalued dependencies are valid, and GATE DA 2024 (sample paper) asked which conditions hold on a printed instance. Both are used in this lesson and both are labelled with their own paper, never as CSE.

Verified fact On CSE papers the concept appears obliquely: GATE CSE 2004 offered 4NF as one of four options for "the highest normal form of this schema", and the answer was 3NF. Class A means a question here is inside the boundary, not that one is guaranteed. No marks distribution is claimed for 2027.

Prerequisites

  • Functional dependencies and normal forms — Armstrong's axioms, attribute closure, candidate keys, BCNF, lossless join. This lesson assumes all of it.
  • Sets and set operations — the MVD is a statement about sets of values.
  • Relational algebra — projection and natural join, which the theorem is stated in.

Approximate learning time: 50 to 70 minutes, including the practice set. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

The five ideas

  1. $$X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$: fixing x fixes the whole set of Y-values, whatever else is fixed beside it.
  2. Every FD is an MVD; an MVD is not an FD. That gap is the entire topic.
  3. 4NF: every non-trivial MVD has a superkey on its left. Keys come from the FDs alone.
  4. An MVD is exactly a lossless two-way split — so that split needs no join test.
  5. Three or more fragments: run the chase. More fragments is not more safety.

Intuition first: two lists that have nothing to do with each other

A restaurant offers some pizza varieties and delivers to some areas. Those are two separate lists. If you want to record both in one table, you cannot record them as two lists — a table has one value per cell. So you write every variety against every area, and the table grows as the product of the two lists.

That is the whole phenomenon. Two multi-valued facts about the same thing, independent of each other, forced into one relation, become a product. A functional dependency can never describe this, because a functional dependency is a statement that one value is determined — one, not a set.

Educational interpretation The restaurant is the standard illustration (it is the one on Wikipedia's 4NF page, and the page recomputes it rather than copying it). The arithmetic underneath is not an analogy: for c varieties and s areas the flat table really does need c·s rows and the split really does need c + s.

Formal definition

Let R be a relation over attribute set U, and let X, Y, Z be pairwise disjoint subsets with X ∪ Y ∪ Z = U. Write Y(x, z) for the set of Y-values appearing in R alongside that x and that z.

The multivalued dependency $$X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ holds on R when Y(x, z) depends only on x: for every x and every pair z, z′ that actually occur with x, Y(x, z) = Y(x, z′).

Equivalently — and this is the same statement — if two tuples agree on X, then the tuple carrying X and Z from the first and Y from the second must also be present, and so must its mirror image.

Verified fact Both forms are Fagin's, from Multivalued dependencies and a new normal form for relational databases, ACM TODS 2(3), September 1977. The page checks that the two forms decide identically on all 4,864 small cases, so you may use whichever a question gives you.

Symbols, in words

  • X, Y, Z — three disjoint groups of columns. X is the left side, Y what is multi-determined, Z everything else.
  • $$X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ — "X multi-determines Y". The double arrow is not a typo for a single one.
  • Y(x, z) — the set of Y-values in the rows where X is x and Z is z.
  • X ∪ Y = R — the left and right sides together are every column of the schema.
  • superkey — a set of columns whose closure under the FDs is the whole schema.
  • π — projection: keep some columns, drop duplicates.
  • ⋈ — natural join: match on the shared columns.
  • chase — the tableau procedure that decides whether a join loses a tuple.

The rules, and which ones hold

RuleHolds?
$$X \to Y \;\Rightarrow\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ (replication)yes
$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y \;\Rightarrow\; X \twoheadrightarrow Z$$ (complementation)yes
$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \;\Rightarrow\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y \cup Y'$$ (union)yes
$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \;\Rightarrow\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y \cap Y'$$ (decomposition)yes
$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\; X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \;\Rightarrow\; X \to Y$$no
$$X \twoheadrightarrow YZ,\; X \to Y \;\Rightarrow\; X \to Z$$no

The two failures are not curiosities: they are exactly the four implications GATE IT 2007 asked about, and the page finds a witness for each by exhaustive search.

The conditions each statement carries

The MVD is defined for disjoint X, Y, Z. The triviality test has two limbs, Y ⊆ X or X ∪ Y = R, and a question that checks only the first will mark a tautology as a violation. The 4NF superkey test uses the functional dependencies only, because an MVD never contributes to a closure. And the lossless two-way split needs the MVD — without it the join of XY and XZ invents rows.

Each of these is a place where a correct-looking answer is wrong. The Edge cases tab takes them one at a time, with the computed number attached.

Worked example 1 — the employee table, and one deletion that breaks everything

Attributes Employee, Child, Salary, Year. Pythagoras has children Peter and Paul, and salary-years (20K, 1976) and (25K, 1977). Gauss has children Gwendolyn and Greta, and salary-years (40K, 1976) and (50K, 1976). That is four rows each, eight in total.

  1. Keys. There are no functional dependencies. Closing any proper subset of the four attributes adds nothing, so the only candidate key is all four.
  2. BCNF. Every functional dependency is trivial, because there are none. BCNF holds, vacuously.
  3. The MVD. For Pythagoras, the children at 20K are {Peter, Paul} and at 25K also {Peter, Paul}. For Gauss, both salary-years give {Gwendolyn, Greta}. The sets do not depend on the salary, so Employee $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Child.
  4. 4NF. Employee $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Child is non-trivial and Employee is not a superkey, so the schema is not in 4NF.
  5. Delete one tuple — Gauss, Greta, 50K, 1976. Now Gauss at 40K has {Gwendolyn, Greta} and at 50K has {Gwendolyn}. The sets differ, the MVD fails, and the seven-row relation is not even an instance of the schema.
  6. Split. Into (Employee, Child) and (Employee, Salary, Year): four rows and four rows, and the join returns the original eight.

Every count above is recomputed by the page: the key by closure over the empty FD set, both verdicts by the normal-form tests, the MVD by both definitions, and the join by projecting and joining back.

Worked example 2 — the pizza table, and what an update costs

Restaurant, Variety, Delivery area. A1 Pizza: 2 varieties, 3 areas, 6 rows. Elite: 2 varieties, 1 area, 2 rows. Vincenzo's: 2 varieties, 2 areas, 4 rows. Twelve rows in total.

The only candidate key is all three attributes, so BCNF holds. Restaurant $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Variety holds because each restaurant's variety list is the same at every area it serves, and Restaurant $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Area holds by complementation.

Split into (Restaurant, Variety) — 6 rows — and (Restaurant, Area) — 6 rows. Joining them back gives exactly the twelve, which the page checks rather than asserts.

The update. A1 adds Cheese Crust. Flat: one row per area, so three rows. Split: one row. Do the flat update for only two of the three areas and the table stops satisfying the MVD — the page computes that violation too.

Worked example 3 — Maier's five-attribute schema

R(A, B, C, D, E) with A → BC and C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE.

  1. Closure of {A} under A → BC is ABC. Not the whole schema, so A is not a superkey.
  2. No FD has a superkey on its left, and A → BC is non-trivial, so R is not in BCNF. It is therefore not in 4NF either.
  3. C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE is non-trivial and C is not a superkey, so it is a 4NF violation in its own right.
  4. Split into R1 = ABC and R2 = CDE.
  5. R1: A → BC, and the closure of {A} is ABC, which is all of R1. So A is a key of R1, and the MVD A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B that R1 inherits is harmless. R1 is in 4NF.
  6. R2: C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE has C ∪ DE = CDE, the whole of R2. Trivial. R2 is in 4NF.

The lesson of the example: the fragment still carries a non-trivial MVD, and it is fine, because in the smaller schema its left side became a key. A 4NF check must be redone per fragment.

Worked example 4 — the chase on three decompositions

R(A, B, C, D) with A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B, so Z = CD. Three decompositions, three answers.

  1. {AB, ACD}. Two rows. They agree on A, so the MVD applies and adds the row with A and CD from the first and B from the second — a fully distinguished row. Lossless.
  2. {AB, AC, AD}. Three rows. The chase runs to nine rows and adds six, and no row becomes fully distinguished. Lossy.
  3. {AB, CD}. The fragments share no attribute, so the MVD never applies and no row is added at all. Lossy.

And the damage in rows: the instance {(a,b1,c1,d1), (a,b2,c2,d2), (a,b1,c2,d2), (a,b2,c1,d1)} satisfies A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B, and the join of its AB, AC and AD projections has eight tuples. Four in, eight out.

The boundaries, each with the number that shows it

An edge case here is a case where a rule that is right almost everywhere returns the wrong verdict. Each card below names the condition that has failed and what the correct answer is instead.

When Z is empty

If X ∪ Y is already every attribute, then Z is empty and there is only one "z" per x. The condition Y(x, z) = Y(x, z′) is then vacuous, so the MVD holds in every relation of that schema.

On R(A, B, C), the dependency A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC is therefore true of everything. That is the triviality limb X ∪ Y = R, and it is what makes the third GATE IT 2007 implication false rather than merely unproven.

When the two counts are 1

For c children and s salary-years the flat table needs c·s rows and the split needs c + s. The saving is (c−1)(s−1) − 1, which is 0 at c = s = 2 and negative when either count is 1.

So with one child there is nothing to split, and splitting costs exactly one row more. The eight-row employee table, with two children and two salary-years per employee, saves nothing at all — which is why a row count is not a test for 4NF.

When there are three fragments

The two-fragment shortcut — the shared attributes must determine one whole fragment — says nothing about three or more. A decomposition can pass every pairwise check and still be lossy.

Computed: {AB, ACD} is lossless, and {AB, AC, AD} is not, for the same schema and the same MVD. The chase gives 9 rows and 6 additions on the second, and the witness instance joins 4 tuples to 8.

A table is not a schema

An MVD is a claim about every legal instance. One printed table can refute it and can never establish it.

The page exhibits both directions on the same data: eight rows satisfy Employee $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Child, and deleting a single tuple produces seven rows that violate it. GATE DA 2024's sample-paper question asks about a printed instance and says so; a question about a schema is a different question.

The half-done update

In the flat pizza table, adding a variety means adding a row per area. Doing it for two of three areas leaves a table that no longer satisfies the MVD.

The page checks the two-thirds update and reports the violation. This is the practical reason 4NF exists: the bad state is not merely redundant, it is reachable by a perfectly ordinary edit.

Keys from MVDs

An MVD does not add attributes to a closure, so it never creates a key. Treating Employee $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Child as though it determined Child makes Employee a superkey, and then the schema looks like it is in 4NF.

The page computes the candidate key both ways: over the empty FD set the only key is all three attributes, and the verdict is "not 4NF". That is the whole difference between the right answer and the tempting one.

Six wrong beliefs, each with the smallest thing that refutes it

The format is: the belief, why it is tempting, the minimal counterexample, and the rule to use instead. Every counterexample below is one the page builds and checks.

"X $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Y means X determines Y"

Why it tempts: the arrow looks like the functional arrow with an extra stroke, and the phrase "multi-determines" sounds like "determines, but more".

Refutation: two tuples with the same x and different y. The set of y-values for that x has two members, which an MVD allows and an FD forbids. The page counts 1,915 such instances.

Rule: an FD is an MVD; an MVD is not an FD. When you need a value, you need a single arrow.

"If it is in BCNF it has no redundancy left"

Why it tempts: every normal form up to BCNF is defined by functional dependencies, and those are what textbooks use to explain redundancy.

Refutation: Employee, Child, Salary — no FD at all, so BCNF holds vacuously, and the table still repeats every child against every salary-year.

Rule: BCNF removes redundancy caused by functional dependencies. 4NF is the form that removes the rest.

"4NF is 1NF for multi-valued attributes"

Why it tempts: both are described with the words "multi-valued", and both are about repeating data.

Refutation: 1NF forbids one cell holding a set; 4NF forbids a relation pairing two independent sets. A schema can be perfectly atomic in every cell and still fail 4NF — the employee table is exactly that.

Rule: ask whether a cell holds a set (1NF) or whether a row repeats a fact (4NF).

"An MVD with a superkey on the left is a violation"

Why it tempts: the definition is easy to half-remember as "there must be no MVDs".

Refutation: R(A, B, C) with A → BC and A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B. The MVD is non-trivial, and A is a key, so nothing is wrong: this schema is in 4NF. Maier's R1 = ABC is the same shape.

Rule: 4NF forbids non-trivial MVDs whose left side is not a superkey. A key on the left makes the dependency harmless.

"Splitting further is always safer"

Why it tempts: more decomposition sounds like more normalisation.

Refutation: {AB, ACD} is lossless and {AB, AC, AD} is not, on the same schema with the same MVD. A four-tuple instance joins to eight.

Rule: the MVD licenses exactly the two-way split X∪Y and X∪Z. Anything finer needs the chase before you may call it lossless.

"The option list tells you the answer"

Why it tempts: if 4NF is offered, the question must be about 4NF.

Refutation: GATE CSE 2004 offered 2NF, 3NF, BCNF and 4NF for the highest normal form of a given schema, and the answer was 3NF.

Rule: compute the form from the dependencies given. The option list is not evidence.

What a question on this topic looks like, and what it demands

Four shapes account for almost everything. Each is listed with the reasoning it demands and the approach that looks right and is not.

Shape 1 — the highest normal form

Demands: keys by closure, then each rung in order, then the MVD test only if every FD-based rung passed.

Wrong approach: reading the options and picking the most advanced one that is not obviously false. GATE CSE 2004 is the counterexample, with 4NF offered and 3NF correct.

Shortcut and its assumption: "if every attribute is prime, it is in 3NF" is safe; "if every attribute is prime, it is in BCNF" is not, and "so it is in 4NF" is worse.

Shape 2 — is this dependency implied?

Demands: for FDs, a closure computation. For MVDs, either the rules or the chase — and a witness if the answer is no.

Wrong approach: assuming the arrow type carries over. Union and decomposition do carry over; changing $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ into $$\to$$ does not.

Discipline: to answer "not implied", produce an instance. The page's own answer to implication (i) is the eight-tuple cube, which is small enough to write out in an exam.

Shape 3 — is this decomposition lossless?

Demands: two fragments — check that the shared attributes determine one fragment, or quote Fagin's theorem. Three or more — run the chase.

Wrong approach: checking pairs and declaring the whole thing lossless. {AB, AC, AD} passes no such check and is lossy.

Time saver: for a two-way split on an MVD you are given, losslessness is free — it is the same statement as the MVD.

Shape 4 — how many relations?

Demands: one split per violating dependency, then re-test each fragment, because an MVD that violated in the parent may be trivial in a fragment.

Wrong approach: counting violations in the parent and stopping. Maier's example needs one split for two violations, because the second disappears inside the fragment.

Check: join the fragments back mentally, or in the margin, and confirm you can rebuild every original row.

Practice set

Seven questions. Answers are hidden until you reveal them, one at a time; the mock-test switch hides them all and reveals them together. Every answer is derived here, and the page computes the figures it quotes.

Q1 Original practice MCQ

R(A, B, C) has no functional dependencies and satisfies A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B, with B and C disjoint from A. Which statement is true?

(A) in BCNF and in 4NF   (B) in BCNF but not in 4NF   (C) in 4NF but not in BCNF   (D) in neither

Answer: (B). With no FDs, every proper subset of {A, B, C} has a closure equal to itself, so the only candidate key is all three attributes. Every FD is therefore trivial and BCNF holds vacuously. A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B is non-trivial — B is not inside A, and A ∪ B = AB is not all of ABC — and A is not a superkey, so 4NF fails.

Why the others are wrong: (A) misses the MVD violation. (C) is impossible in general, because 4NF implies BCNF. (D) ignores that BCNF is satisfied vacuously.

Trap: computing a key by treating A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B as though it were A → B gives A as a key and turns (B) into (A).

Q2 GATE IT 2007 MCQ

Consider four implications relating functional and multivalued dependencies: (i) if A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B and A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ C then A → BC; (ii) if A → B and A → C then A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC; (iii) if A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC and A → B then A → C; (iv) if A → BC and A → B then A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ C. Exactly how many are valid?

(A) 0   (B) 1   (C) 2   (D) 3

Answer: (C), 2. Valid: (ii) and (iv). Invalid: (i) and (iii).

(i) fails: take all eight tuples of {0,1}³. Both MVDs hold because every A appears with every (B, C) pair, and A → B fails because one A appears with both B values.

(iii) fails: on R(A, B, C), A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC is trivial since A ∪ BC is everything, so it is satisfied by every relation. Take two A-values, each with its own B, and give each two C-values. A → B holds, A → C does not.

(ii) holds: union gives A → BC, then replication gives A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC. (iv) holds: decomposition gives A → C, then replication gives A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ C.

Note: this is a GATE IT paper, not GATE CSE. The page decides all four by search over 256 instances and finds no counterexample to (ii) or (iv), and 20,000 random three-value instances agree.

Q3 Original practice NAT

An employee has 4 children and 3 salary-years, recorded in one flat relation (Employee, Child, Salary, Year). How many fewer rows does the 4NF decomposition need than the flat relation? Give the difference as an integer.

Answer: 5. Flat: 4 × 3 = 12 rows. Split: (Employee, Child) has 4 rows and (Employee, Salary, Year) has 3, so 7. 12 − 7 = 5.

Shortcut and its assumption: the saving is (c−1)(s−1) − 1 = 3 × 2 − 1 = 5. The formula assumes every combination appears in the flat table, which is exactly what the MVD guarantees.

Edge case: at c = s = 2 the same formula gives 0, so "the split saves rows" is not a universal claim — it needs both counts at least 2, and even then it can save nothing.

Q4 Original practice MCQ

On R(A, B, C, D), which one of these multivalued dependencies is not trivial?

(A) A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BCD   (B) AB $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ CD   (C) ABC $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ D   (D) A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B

Answer: (D). Triviality has two limbs: Y ⊆ X, or X ∪ Y = R.

(A) A ∪ BCD = ABCD = R — trivial by the second limb. (B) AB ∪ CD = R — trivial. (C) ABC ∪ D = R — trivial. (D) B is not inside A, and A ∪ B = AB is not ABCD — non-trivial, and therefore the only one that could be a 4NF violation.

Trap: checking only Y ⊆ X makes (A), (B) and (C) all look non-trivial, and then every one of them looks like a violation.

Q5 Original practice MSQ

Which of these schemas are in 4NF?

(A) R(Employee, Child, Salary), no FDs, Employee $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ Child

(B) R(A, B, C), A → BC

(C) R(A, B, C, D, E), A → BC and C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE

(D) R(C, D, E), C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE

Answer: (B) and (D).

(A) not 4NF: the only key is all three attributes, so Employee is not a superkey and the MVD is non-trivial.

(B) 4NF: A → BC makes A a superkey of ABC, so the MVD A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B that comes with it is harmless. A key on the left of an MVD is exactly what 4NF permits.

(C) not 4NF: the closure of {A} is ABC, not ABCDE, so A is not a superkey and A → BC already breaks BCNF; and C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE is a violation on its own.

(D) 4NF: C ∪ DE = CDE, which is the whole schema, so C $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ DE is trivial and constrains nothing. This is Maier's R2.

Q6 Original practice MCQ

R(A, B, C, D) satisfies A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B, with Z = CD. Which of these decompositions is lossless?

(A) {AB, ACD} only   (B) {AB, AC, AD} only   (C) both   (D) neither

Answer: (A). By Fagin's theorem, A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B holds exactly when the relation is the join of its AB and ACD projections, so {AB, ACD} is lossless — the chase produces a fully distinguished row in four rows with two additions.

{AB, AC, AD} is lossy: the chase runs to nine rows, adds six, and never produces a fully distinguished row. A concrete witness is {(a,b1,c1,d1), (a,b2,c2,d2), (a,b1,c2,d2), (a,b2,c1,d1)}, which satisfies A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B and whose three projections join to eight tuples.

Trap: assuming a finer decomposition inherits losslessness. It does not, and this is the case where it fails.

Q7 Original practice MCQ

R(A, B, C) satisfies A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B. Which functional dependency must necessarily hold?

(A) A → B   (B) A → C   (C) BC → A   (D) none of them

Answer: (D). Two tuples with the same A and different B satisfy A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B and break A → B, which disposes of (A). The same two tuples break A → C only if C differs, so for (B) use two tuples with the same A and different C. For (C), take a single tuple plus a second with the same (B, C) and a different A.

Rule: an MVD constrains which sets must appear; it never forces a single value. Only a single arrow does that. The page counts 1,915 three-attribute instances where the MVD holds and the FD does not.

Revision sheet

One screen. The definitions in the words an exam wants, the rules that hold, the rules that do not, and the four-step procedure.

Definitions

MVD. $$X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ holds on R(X, Y, Z) when Y(x, z) = Y(x, z′) for every x and every z, z′ occurring with it.

Equivalent form. Tuples agreeing on X may swap their Y-parts, and the results must be present.

Trivial. Y ⊆ X, or X ∪ Y = R.

4NF. Every non-trivial MVD has a superkey on its left. Keys from the FDs alone.

5NF. Every non-trivial join dependency has a superkey on its left. A different condition.

Rules that hold

$$X \to Y \Rightarrow X \twoheadrightarrow Y$$ (replication)

$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y \Leftrightarrow X \twoheadrightarrow Z$$ (complementation)

$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\ X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \Rightarrow X \twoheadrightarrow Y \cup Y'$$ (union)

$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\ X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \Rightarrow X \twoheadrightarrow Y \cap Y'$$ (decomposition)

$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y \Leftrightarrow r = \pi_{XY}(r) \bowtie \pi_{XZ}(r)$$ (Fagin's theorem)

Rules that do not

$$X \twoheadrightarrow Y,\ X \twoheadrightarrow Y' \nRightarrow X \to Y$$

$$X \twoheadrightarrow YZ,\ X \to Y \nRightarrow X \to Z$$

And: 4NF $$\Rightarrow$$ BCNF, but not conversely. And: three lossless-looking fragments are not thereby lossless.

Procedure

  1. Keys by closure under the FDs only.
  2. List non-trivial MVDs; test each left side against the superkeys.
  3. Split on a violation into X∪Y and X∪Z; re-test each fragment.
  4. Three or more fragments, or an implied MVD: run the chase.

Quick checks

1. Is A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ BC on R(A,B,C) a violation? No — trivial.

2. Does BCNF imply 4NF? No.

3. Does 4NF imply BCNF? Yes.

4. Is {AB, ACD} lossless for A $$\twoheadrightarrow$$ B? Yes, by Fagin.

5. Does an MVD create a key? No — never.

GATE 2027 CS syllabus, IIT Madras, CS_GATE2027_Syllabus.pdf, the source this repository records for the exam year. Section 9 (Databases) names normal forms; it does not name multivalued dependencies or 4NF separately. That is the whole of the Class A claim made above, and nothing more is claimed.

GATE IT 2007 and GATE DA 2024 (sample paper) — the two past-paper items used, each labelled with its own paper. Neither is a GATE CSE paper and neither is presented as one.

Textbook and university material

R. Fagin, "Multivalued dependencies and a new normal form for relational databases", ACM Transactions on Database Systems 2(3), September 1977 — the definition, Proposition 1 (an FD is an MVD), Proposition 2 (complementation), Theorem 1 (MVD iff the two projections join back), Theorem 2 (4NF implies BCNF), Theorem 3 (every schema splits into 4NF without loss), and the employee/child/salary illustration including the one-tuple deletion used in scene 4.

D. Maier, The Theory of Relational Databases, chapter 7 — Definition 7.7 of 4NF and Examples 7.11 and 7.12, the second of which is the five-attribute A→BC, C$$\twoheadrightarrow$$DE example in Worked example 3.

J. Ullman, Stanford course slides on multivalued dependencies — the swap reading of the definition, the "MVDs are invisible to BCNF" framing, and the note that the superkey test still uses FDs only.

What the page computes for itself

Nothing on the canvas is copied. The page implements both definitions of the MVD, the natural join, projection, attribute closure, candidate keys, the BCNF and 4NF tests, the tableau chase and a three-way join, and then cross-checks them against each other: the two MVD definitions against each other on 4,864 cases, replication on 753, complementation on every case with a non-empty Z, Fagin's theorem on all 4,864, and each of the four past-paper implications against an exhaustive search of all 256 three-attribute instances plus 20,000 random three-value instances. It reports how many of its checks passed on the Animation tab.

What could not be confirmed

No GATE CSE paper question naming multivalued dependencies or 4NF was found. The topic's CSE presence is through the syllabus phrase "normal forms" and through option lists, not through a question of its own that I could verify.

The question number of the GATE IT 2007 item differs between catalogues, so this lesson quotes the year and the paper only.

No claim is made about how many marks this topic will carry in GATE CSE 2027, and no historical weightage is asserted. The 4NF check on this page uses the MVDs given plus their complements plus the MVDs implied by the FDs; it does not enumerate the full MVD closure, and every verdict it reports is of that restricted form.

Where this connects

This is the rung directly above functional dependencies and normal forms, which builds 1NF to BCNF, attribute closure, the chase for three fragments and dependency preservation; this lesson assumes that chase and applies it to MVDs. The projection and join it is stated in are from relational algebra. The decomposition is the same idea as the ER mapping rule that a multivalued attribute gets its own relation — but 4NF is about rows repeating a fact, while that rule is about cells holding a set. And the whole ladder is a worked instance of a theme from sets: a constraint is a property of every member of a family, not of one member you happen to be looking at.
