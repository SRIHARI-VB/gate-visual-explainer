Topic and objective

TOPIC: Tenses, adjectives and conjunctions — the three items named in Section 1 of the GATE General Aptitude syllabus that no earlier lesson in this repository covers. The syllabus sentence is: Basic English grammar: tenses, articles, adjectives, prepositions, conjunctions, verb-noun agreement, and other parts of speech. Articles, prepositions and verb-noun agreement are taught in the verbal aptitude lesson; these three are what remains of that sentence.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Decide a tense blank from the time expression and the aspect the sentence requires; decide an adjective blank from the number of things compared, or from whether the adjective can take a degree at all, or from its conventional position; and decide a conjunction blank from the class of the connector and the polarity it imposes on the other clause. In each case the deciding feature is printed in the sentence, so the blank is read off the sentence rather than chosen by ear.

I read the three items as three different kinds of form question: a tense question asks which of twelve forms; an adjective question asks which degree, whether a degree is possible, or which position; a conjunction question asks which class of link and therefore which polarity. The animation is built around those three, not around a list of rules to memorise.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus.

Basic vocabulary: words, idioms, and phrases in context. Reading and comprehension, Narrative sequencing." Tenses, adjectives and conjunctions are named one by one.

Verified fact General Aptitude is 15 of the 100 marks in every paper, in 10 questions out of 65 — five of one mark and five of two marks. The paper is set in English.

Class A means a question here is inside the declared boundary, not that a particular shape is guaranteed. Every previous-year item quoted carries its year, question number and published key.

Prerequisites

  • School English. Terms such as clause, subject and modal are glossed the first time each appears.
  • Verbal aptitude — supplies articles, prepositions and the head-noun agreement rule. This lesson builds on that rule and, in its last scene, shows exactly where it stops.
  • Analytical aptitude — supplies the idea of a condition and its contrapositive, which is what an if-clause is. The conditional scene uses it.
  • Nothing from the CS syllabus. This section is deliberately independent of the rest of the paper.

The three decisions, in one line each

  • Tense. Find the time expression and the reference point; the aspect follows from the relation to it.
  • Adjective. Count the things compared, ask whether the adjective grades at all, then place it in the conventional order.
  • Conjunction. Name the class, then let the connector set the polarity of the clause it introduces.

Intuition first: a blank is a slot with a shape

The verbal aptitude lesson pictures a sentence as a machine with slots, where each slot asks for one feature. These three items are three more slots, and each asks for a form rather than a meaning. The tense slot asks which of twelve forms the sentence has placed itself in. The adjective slot asks which degree the count of things allows — or whether a degree is allowed at all — or where in a fixed sequence the adjective belongs. The conjunction slot asks which class of link is being made, and that class then dictates the polarity of the clause that follows. Educational interpretation Under that picture, the commonest wrong answer in all three is a form that is correct English somewhere else: had wished is correct English, just not after after; cheapest is correct English, just not with two things; good is correct English, just not after but.

The other thing the three share is that each has a small arithmetic core. Twelve cells, a count of things, a count of inversions, a count of markers against relations. That is why the page can compute the answer instead of asserting it, and why the counts are shown on the canvas.

Tense is when; aspect is how

Tense places an event in time: past, present or future. Aspect says how the event sits against that time. The two are independent, and the independence is what makes the grid.

simple: walks (a whole event)
perfect: has walked (completed before a reference point)
continuous: is walking (in progress)
perfect continuous: has been walking (in progress up to a reference point)"Reference point" means the moment the sentence is measured from — usually now, sometimes another event. "Perfect" does not mean "very good"; it is the grammatical name for the completed-before relation.

Mathematical derivation The perfect is marked by has, have or had. The continuous is marked by the -ing form. Two markers, each present or absent, give four combinations — which is why there are four aspects and not three. Across three tenses that is twelve cells, and the page builds all twelve and checks that no two of the twelve strings are equal.

The perfect as a relation to a reference point

The perfect is a statement about order, not about importance. Had walked puts the walking before the reference point; walked puts it at the reference point; has walked puts a span of walking up to now.

After he returned, he wished … (the wish follows the return)
After he had returned, he wished … (the return follows nothing; it is the earlier event)Only one clause may be moved back. If the subordinate clause is already in the simple past, the main clause stays in the simple past too, because the order is carried by the word "after".

Verified fact This is the rule the 2017 item turns on, and it is the rule the published key follows: GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, GA Question 1, key wished. Community observation Some published solutions argue for had wished on the grounds that the past perfect "sounds more precise"; the paper's key does not accept that, and the argument is a good illustration of why the key, not the ear, is the standard.

The future marker belongs to the main clause

In a sentence with an if-clause or a time clause, the future is marked once, in the clause that can stand alone. The subordinate clause stays in the present even though it refers to the future.

If it rains, we will cancel.
When she arrives, we will start."If it will rain" is the error. The subordinate clause is already future by position; adding will marks the future twice and is not English.

Mathematical derivation The page marks each printed sentence with where the future verb sits — main clause or subordinate clause — and decides legality from that position alone. Three of the six put it in the main clause and are legal; three put it in the if- or when-clause and are not. No other feature is consulted, so the count is a test of that one rule.

Unreal conditions take would

A second conditional describes an unreal present: if you were a bird, you would fly. The condition takes the past subjunctive were, and the result takes would plus the base verb. Dropping if and inverting the verb keeps the meaning.

If you were a bird, you would fly.
Were you a bird, you would fly.The inverted form is the same sentence. The absence of "if" is a matter of word order, not of meaning, so the same result clause is required.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2013, GA Question 59 asks exactly this, with the options would fly, shall fly, should fly and shall have flown. The published key is would fly. Mathematical derivation The page labels each option by its modal — conditional, future, obligation, future perfect — and counts the conditional ones. The count is one.

Degree is decided by how many things are compared

The positive form names a quality, the comparative compares two, and the superlative picks one out of three or more. The sentence almost always prints the number.

the taller of the two (comparative, n = 2)
the tallest of the three (superlative, n = 3)
the most accurate of all (superlative)"Of the two" and "of the three" are the whole question. A superlative with two things is the commonest degree error in the papers, and a comparative with three is its mirror image.

Mathematical derivation The rule is a function from the count to the degree: two gives comparative, three or more gives superlative. The page applies it to five printed contexts and finds three that obey it and two that do not, which is a check on the rule rather than on the reader's taste.

Absolute adjectives, and comparisons marked twice

A gradable adjective names a quality that comes in amounts: careful, tired, tall. An absolute adjective names a state that either holds or does not: unique, dead, fatal, square, unanimous, entire, infinite, pregnant.

much cheaper · never very cheaper
unique · never more unique
dead · never very deadThe first line is the double-marking error: "cheaper" already carries the comparison, so "very" marks it a second time. The second and third are the absolute error: the modifier assumes a scale the adjective has already closed.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2012, GA Question 59 asks for the grammatically incorrect sentence, and the key is This country's expenditure on educational reforms is very less — the double-marking error, not the absolute one. Mathematical derivation The page applies more, most and very to eight absolute adjectives and two gradable controls: 24 rejections, all on the absolutes, and 6 acceptances, all on the controls.

Adjective order is a rank sequence

When several adjectives modify one noun they take a conventional order, running from the most subjective to the most intrinsic.

opinion · size · age · shape · colour · origin · material · purpose
a lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knifeThe purpose adjective sits nearest the noun because it is almost part of the noun: a whittling knife is a kind of knife. The opinion adjective sits furthest away because it is the speaker's judgement and not a property of the object.

Mathematical derivation Give each class its rank. A correct order is a strictly increasing sequence of ranks, and the number of pairs out of order is its inversion count. On four printed orders of four adjectives the counts are 0, 3, 1 and 1, so exactly one order is correct and the others are wrong by measurable amounts. Unverified claim Published descriptions do not agree on whether shape precedes age; see the Edge cases tab, where the disagreement is named rather than hidden.

The -ed and -ing pair

Both forms are adjectives made from one verb, and they point in opposite directions. The -ed form describes the one who feels; the -ing form describes what produces the feeling.

the students were bored (the students feel it)
the lecture was boring (the lecture causes it)"The film was bored" parses and means nothing, because a film cannot feel. The sentence is grammatical, so only the role question catches it.

Mathematical derivation Each sentence carries its subject and that subject's role. The rule requires -ed for an experiencer and -ing for a cause, and the page counts the disagreements: four of six match, two swap.

Conjunctions: class, then polarity

Seven coordinating conjunctions join two clauses of equal standing: and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so. Nine subordinating conjunctions attach a clause that cannot stand alone: because, although, though, whereas, while, since, unless, if, that.

Because it rained, we stayed in. (a sentence)
But it rained, we stayed in. (a fragment)The fronting test: a subordinating conjunction can be moved to the front with its clause and followed by a comma; a coordinating one cannot. That test sorts all sixteen without needing their names.

Mathematical derivation Once the class is known, the connector fixes the polarity of what follows: but and although demand a contrast, because and so demand a consequence. The page scores the four options of the 2020 item by polarity and finds exactly one negative, and the four nouns of the 2012 despite item the same way.

Agreement: the one place proximity wins

The verbal aptitude lesson establishes that a verb agrees with the head of its subject and not with the nearest noun, and offers the list of items is on the desk as the counterexample that kills the nearest-noun guess. That counterexample is correct and it is not the whole story, because one construction reverses it.

Neither the manager nor the clerks are available. (nearer noun: clerks, plural)
Neither the clerks nor the manager is available. (nearer noun: manager, singular)
The manager, as well as the clerks, is available. (first noun: manager, singular)"Neither … nor" and "either … or" are correlative constructions: the verb follows the nearer noun. "As well as", "along with" and "together with" are additive phrases: they are set aside and the verb follows the first noun, exactly as in "the list of items is".

Verified fact Standard usage references give both rules: when one subject is singular and one plural, a verb joined by or, nor, either … or or neither … nor agrees with the nearer subject, while a phrase introduced by as well as, along with or together with does not change the number of the subject. Mathematical derivation The page runs both rules on seven sentences. Proximity scores four of four on the correlatives, zero of two on the additives and zero on the modifier sentence; the head rule scores the exact reverse. The construction, not the distance, decides — and a diagram of two rules over seven sentences is what makes that visible.

Example 1 — a tense, from the order of two events

GATE CSE 2017 Set 1, GA Question 1 (one mark). After Rajendra Chola returned from his voyage to Indonesia, he ______ to visit the temple in Thanjavur. (A) was wishing (B) is wishing (C) wished (D) had wished

Step 1. The word after fixes the order: the return comes first, the wish second. Step 2. The second event therefore takes the simple past, because the past perfect is the form that moves an event back from the reference point.

Mathematical derivation The answer is C — wished. Verified fact The published key marks wished. The page gives each option a relation to the reference point — before, now, after — and finds that exactly one is after. Note that D is also past tense: pastness is not the test, position relative to the reference point is.

Example 2 — an unreal condition

GATE CSE 2013, GA Question 59 (one mark). Were you a bird, you ______ in the sky. (A) would fly (B) shall fly (C) should fly (D) shall have flown

Step 1. The sentence begins with Were and contains no if, so it is an inverted condition: if you were a bird. Step 2. An unreal present takes would plus the base verb in its result clause.

Mathematical derivation The answer is A — would fly. Verified fact The published key marks would fly. The page labels the four options by modal and counts the conditional ones: one. Shall describes a real future and should describes an obligation, so both answer a question the sentence has already ruled out.

Example 3 — the incorrect sentence, found by one clash

GATE CSE 2012, GA Question 59 (one mark). Choose the grammatically INCORRECT sentence: (A) They gave us the money back less the service charges of Three Hundred rupees. (B) This country's expenditure is not less than that of Bangladesh. (C) The committee initially asked for a funding of Fifty Lakh rupees, but later settled for a lesser sum. (D) This country's expenditure on educational reforms is very less.

Step 1. Three sentences contain less or lesser, so the word itself decides nothing. Step 2. Look for a degree modifier sitting on a comparative. Very cannot modify a comparative; much can.

Mathematical derivation The answer is D. Verified fact The published key is D. The page tests all four for that clash and flags exactly one, which is a check on the reasoning rather than a restatement of the key. In A, less is a preposition meaning minus; in B it is a comparative with not; in C, lesser is an attributive adjective. None of the three is intensified.

Example 4 — a conjunction sets the polarity

GATE CSE 2020, GA Question 2 (one mark). His knowledge of the subject was excellent but his classroom performance was ______. (A) extremely poor (B) good (C) desirable (D) praiseworthy

Step 1. But is a coordinating conjunction of contrast, so the second clause must point the opposite way from the first. Step 2. The first clause is positive — excellent — so the blank must be negative.

Mathematical derivation The answer is A — extremely poor. Verified fact The published key is A. The page records the polarity of each option and finds exactly one negative among the four. B, C and D are all correct English; each of them simply fails to contrast, which is why they are the distractors.

Example 5 — a concession, and the noun it forces

GATE CSE 2012, GA Question 57 (one mark). Despite several ______ the mission succeeded in its attempt to resolve the conflict. (A) attempts (B) setbacks (C) meetings (D) delegations

Step 1. Despite marks a concession: what follows works against the success. Step 2. The noun must therefore be an obstacle, not a neutral event.

Mathematical derivation The answer is B — setbacks. Verified fact The published key is B. The page assigns each noun a polarity and finds exactly one negative. Attempts and meetings are neutral and delegations is neutral too, so the concession has nothing to concede.

Example 6 — an order, scored by inversion count (written for this lesson)

Original practice. Put the adjectives of a ______ table in the conventional order, choosing from wooden, small, old and round. Step 1. Rank each class: small is size (2), old is age (3), round is shape (4), wooden is material (7). Step 2. The correct order is the one whose ranks increase.

Mathematical derivation small old round wooden scores 0 inversions and is correct. old small round wooden scores 1, because age has been moved before size. small round old wooden scores 1, because shape has been moved before age. wooden small old round scores 3, because material has been moved to the front. The counts are 0, 3, 1, 1 in the order the options are printed on the canvas, and they are computed, not assigned.

The boundaries: where each rule stops being safe

Each card names a condition the rule depends on and says what happens when it fails. Where a size can be computed, it is.

Two clauses, only one of which may move back

A sentence can hold two past events in the simple past if the order is carried by a word: after, before, then. The past perfect is needed when the order is not stated and must be inferred from the forms alone: when she arrived, he had left.

Mathematical derivation One ordering word and one moved-back clause is one marker too many. The rule is: state the order with a word, or state it with the form, never both and never neither.

The perfect with "since" needs a point, and with "for" a span

This is the preposition rule of the verbal aptitude lesson seen from the tense side. Since 2019 and for three years both force the present perfect because both describe a span reaching now. In 2019 and last year force the simple past because both close the span.

Mathematical derivation Six expressions, typed as open or closed, give three perfects and three simple pasts. The type is a property of the expression, so the same expression always forces the same tense — which is why the blank is decidable without reading the options.

Shape against age: the one order pair the sources disagree on

Some published descriptions place shape before age (opinion, size, shape, age, colour, nationality, material); others place age before shape (opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material). Both orders are in current teaching material, and both sound acceptable in most phrases.

Unverified claim This lesson teaches opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose — the order of the common OSASCOMP mnemonic — and marks the age/shape pair as unsettled. A question that turned on that pair alone would be unfair, and no item quoted here does.

Absolute adjectives on the boundary

Complete, perfect and whole sit on the boundary. A more perfect Union is written into a founding document and more complete is common in edited prose, so a strict reading and a permissive reading both have support.

Mathematical derivation A well-set question uses an adjective on which careful users agree — unique, dead, square — and this lesson tests only those eight. The three boundary words are named on the canvas and tested nowhere, which is a deliberate limit rather than an oversight.

Some verbs resist the grid

Stative verbs — know, own, believe — do not normally take the continuous: I am knowing is not English. The grid still describes them, but two of its twelve cells are unused in practice.

Mathematical derivation The grid is a classification of forms, not a licence to use all twelve with every verb. A question that offered is knowing would be testing this restriction rather than the tense, and no item quoted in this lesson does.

"Neither" with two singular nouns is singular

The proximity rule only bites when the two nouns differ in number. Neither Meera nor Gopal knows is singular because both are singular; the nearest-noun and first-noun rules agree and the question cannot discriminate between them.

Mathematical derivation A discriminating item needs one singular and one plural subject. All four correlative sentences on the canvas are built that way on purpose, which is why both rules can be scored separately.

A counterexample to "the verb agrees with the nearest noun" — and a counterexample to its opposite

The verbal aptitude lesson refutes the nearest-noun rule with the list of items is on the desk: the nearest noun is plural and the verb is singular. That refutation is correct. But the rule it replaces — always agree with the head — fails on neither the manager nor the clerks are available, where the head is singular and the verb is plural.

Mathematical derivation Two rules, seven sentences, and neither rule is right on all seven: proximity scores four, the head rule scores three. What separates the seven is not distance but construction — correlative, additive, or modifier. The lesson is therefore not "trust the head" but "identify the construction, then apply the rule that construction takes", which is the same lesson as the article slot asking for a sound rather than a letter.

Six beliefs that cost marks, and the smallest thing that corrects each

Each entry gives the belief as it is usually held, the correction, and a counterexample small enough to check in the exam hall.

1. "The past perfect is the careful choice for a past event."

Correction. The past perfect is the earlier choice, not the careful one. It moves an event back from the reference point, so using it after after moves the wrong event.

Counterexample. After he returned, he had wished says the wish came before the return, which contradicts after. Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 GA Q1, key wished.

2. "Will belongs wherever the meaning is future."

Correction. Will belongs in the main clause only. An if-clause or a time clause is already future by position.

Counterexample. If it will rain, we will cancel marks the future twice. Mathematical derivation Three of the six sentences on the canvas fail on this and nothing else.

3. "The superlative is the emphatic version of the comparative."

Correction. They are not two strengths of one form; they take different counts. Two things take the comparative, three or more take the superlative.

Counterexample. The tallest of the two is wrong even though it is emphatic. Mathematical derivation Five contexts on the canvas, three legal; the two failures are exactly the count mismatches.

4. "Very can intensify any adjective."

Correction. Very cannot modify a comparative, and it cannot modify an absolute adjective. Comparatives take much.

Counterexample. Very less and more unique. Verified fact GATE CSE 2012 GA Q59 keys the sentence containing very less as the incorrect one.

5. "Bored and boring mean the same thing."

Correction. They are opposites in role: -ed describes the one who feels, -ing describes what causes the feeling.

Counterexample. The film was bored parses and means nothing. Mathematical derivation Two of six sentences on the canvas swap the roles, and both are grammatical, so only the role test finds them.

6. "A conjunction is a conjunction; the class does not matter."

Correction. The class decides what the sentence may do. Only a subordinator can be fronted with its clause; only a contrast word licenses a negative second clause after a positive first one.

Counterexample. But it rained, we stayed in is a fragment; because it rained, we stayed in is a sentence. Verified fact GATE CSE 2020 GA Q2 keys the one negative option after but.

Closely confused pairs, side by side

Tense versus aspect. Past is a tense; perfect is an aspect. Had walked is past and perfect, and the two facts answer different questions: when it happened, and where it sits relative to a reference point.

Past perfect versus simple past. Had wished puts the wish before the reference point; wished puts it at it. The difference is one word and the whole meaning.

Comparative versus superlative. Two things against three or more. The taller of the two against the tallest of the three.

Gradable versus absolute. Careful comes in degrees, so more careful works. Unique does not, so more unique contradicts itself.

Double marking versus absolute marking. Very less fails because the comparison is already there; very dead fails because there is no scale at all. Both are wrong, for different reasons, and the 2012 paper tests the first.

-ed versus -ing. The feeler against the cause. The students were bored against the lecture was boring.

Coordinating versus subordinating. Equal standing against dependency, and the fronting test tells them apart: Because it rained, … is a sentence, But it rained, … is not.

Proximity versus the head. Neither the manager nor the clerks are takes the nearer noun; the manager, as well as the clerks, is takes the first. The same distance, two different answers, because the construction differs.

How these three are actually asked

What follows describes the shapes these questions take in the papers named, with a source for each. The paper does not label its questions by syllabus heading, so the grouping is mine and rests on what each question tests. Nothing here is a forecast of the 2027 paper.

Shape 1 — the single-blank form question

One sentence, one blank, four forms of the same verb. One mark. The work is to read the time expression and the reference point, then pick the form that sits correctly against them.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2017 Set 1 GA Q1 (four past forms of wish, key wished) and GATE CSE 2013 GA Q59 (four modals of fly, key would fly) are both this shape, and both are one-mark items. Community observation A public archive of worked GA solutions lists "sentence completion (articles, prepositions, tenses)" as one of its recurring shapes across 2011–2026; the grouping is the archive's.

Shape 2 — the grammatically incorrect sentence

Four complete sentences, one of which breaks a rule. One mark. The work is to name the rule first, then test all four against it — because three of the four will contain the same suspicious word and be correct.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2012 GA Q59 keys the sentence with very less. GATE CSE 2013 GA Q60 asks the same shape and keys She is an European — the article rule, which the verbal aptitude lesson covers. Two consecutive years, one shape, two different rules.

Shape 3 — the polarity completion

A sentence whose first clause is marked positive or negative, a conjunction joining it to a second clause with a blank, and four options that are all correct English but differ in polarity. One mark. The work is to let the conjunction set the polarity, then find the one option that matches.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2020 GA Q2 (after but, key extremely poor) and GATE CSE 2012 GA Q57 (after despite, key setbacks) are both this shape. Community observation Worked discussions of the 2020 item single out the same reasoning — the conjunction creates the contrast and the second clause must be negative — which is why the shape is worth a procedure rather than a guess.

Shape 4 — the mixed-rule cloze

Several blanks in one sentence or short passage, each testing a different feature. Two marks. The work is one decision per blank, and a tuple is right only if every blank is right, so a single confident blank is not enough.

Verified fact GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 GA Q6 (four idiom blanks, two marks) and GATE CSE 2025 Set 1 GA Q1 (three article blanks, one mark) are this shape; both are worked in the verbal aptitude lesson. A tense or conjunction blank can appear in the same tuple, which is why the three items in this lesson share one procedure.

The procedure that covers all four shapes

1. Name the feature. The time expression, the count of things, the adjective's class, or the conjunction. Write it down in one word.

2. Name what it forces. Perfect or simple; comparative or superlative or no degree; -ed or -ing; a positive or a negative clause. Say it before looking at the options.

3. Test each option against that one requirement. Not against whether it sounds right — against the requirement you just named.

4. Strike the grammatical but off-feature options. This is where most marks are lost: three distractors are usually correct English in some other sentence.

5. Check once against the sentence. Read the completed sentence and confirm the feature you named is the one the sentence prints. If two options survive, you named the wrong feature; go back to step 1 rather than choosing between them.

MCQGATE CSE 2017 Set 1 GA Q1

After Rajendra Chola returned from his voyage to Indonesia, he ______ to visit the temple in Thanjavur.

  1. was wishing
  2. is wishing
  3. wished
  4. had wished

Answer: C — wished

After fixes the order: the return first, the wish second. The second event takes the simple past.

Why D appears. The past perfect moves an event back from the reference point, so had wished would put the wish before the return, contradicting after. It is the strongest distractor because it sounds more careful. Why A appears. The past continuous describes an ongoing wish and does not place it in the sequence. Why B appears. The present is outside the past frame the sentence has set.

The rule. Name the reference point, then ask whether the blank is before it or after it. Do not ask which form sounds more precise.

MCQGATE CSE 2013 GA Q59

Were you a bird, you ____________ in the sky.

  1. would fly
  2. shall fly
  3. should fly
  4. shall have flown

Answer: A — would fly

Were you a bird is if you were a bird with the if dropped and the verb inverted. That is a second conditional: an unreal present, whose result clause takes would plus the base verb.

Why B and D appear. Shall describes a real future, and the sentence denies reality. Why C appears. Should describes an obligation or a recommendation, which is a real-world relation.

The rule. Spot the inversion: a sentence that opens with Were, Had or Should and has no if is a conditional. Then use the modal that matches which conditional it is.

MCQGATE CSE 2012 GA Q59

Choose the grammatically INCORRECT sentence:

  1. They gave us the money back less the service charges of Three Hundred rupees.
  2. This country's expenditure is not less than that of Bangladesh.
  3. The committee initially asked for a funding of Fifty Lakh rupees, but later settled for a lesser sum.
  4. This country's expenditure on educational reforms is very less.

Answer: D — very less

Less is already a comparative, so it cannot also take very. The intensifier a comparative accepts is much.

Why the others are correct. In A, less is a preposition meaning minus. In B it is a comparative with not, which is legal. In C, lesser is an attributive adjective before a noun. Only D puts a degree modifier on a comparative.

The trap. Three of the four sentences contain less or lesser, so the presence of the word decides nothing. Name the rule — degree modifier on a comparative — and test all four against it.

MCQGATE CSE 2020 GA Q2

His knowledge of the subject was excellent but his classroom performance was ______.

  1. extremely poor
  2. good
  3. desirable
  4. praiseworthy

Answer: A — extremely poor

But marks a contrast. The first clause is positive, so the second must be negative.

Why B, C and D appear. All three are positive, and all three are perfectly good English. Each fails only because it agrees with the first clause instead of contrasting with it.

The rule. Read the conjunction before the options. It sets the polarity of the clause it introduces, and polarity is what the four options differ in.

MCQGATE CSE 2012 GA Q57

Despite several ____________ the mission succeeded in its attempt to resolve the conflict.

  1. attempts
  2. setbacks
  3. meetings
  4. delegations

Answer: B — setbacks

Despite marks a concession: whatever follows worked against the success. Only setbacks is an obstacle.

Why A, C and D appear. All three are neutral events. A mission can succeed despite several meetings, but the sentence would then be making no concession at all — the contrast has nothing to contrast with.

The rule. A concession word forces a negative noun. Ask what the success had to overcome, not what the mission did.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct? Select all that apply.

  1. If it will rain tomorrow, the match will be cancelled.
  2. If it rains tomorrow, the match will be cancelled.
  3. When she will arrive, we will begin the meeting.
  4. When she arrives, we will begin the meeting.

Answer: B, D

In both, the future marker sits in the main clause and the subordinate clause stays in the present.

Why A and C are wrong. Both put will inside the if- or when-clause, marking the future twice. They are the same error in two different subordinate clauses, which is why the pair is worth learning together.

The rule. Locate the future verb. If it is in the clause that cannot stand alone, the sentence is wrong however sensible it sounds.

MSQOriginal practice

Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct? Select all that apply.

  1. Neither the manager nor the clerks are available today.
  2. Neither the manager nor the clerks is available today.
  3. The manager, as well as the clerks, is available today.
  4. The manager, as well as the clerks, are available today.

Answer: A, C

A takes the nearer noun, clerks, which is plural. C takes the first noun, manager, because as well as the clerks is an additive phrase set aside.

Why B and D are wrong. B applies the head rule where the correlative construction requires proximity; D applies proximity where the additive construction requires the head. Each is the other's mirror image, which is exactly the trap the pair is built on.

The rule. Identify the construction before choosing the verb. Neither … nor and either … or are correlative; as well as, along with and together with are additive.

NATOriginal practice

Rank the adjective classes in the order opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose as 1 to 8. For the phrase a wooden small round table, give the number of inversions in the rank sequence.

Answer: 3

The ranks are material 7, size 2, shape 4 — the sequence 7, 2, 4. The pairs out of order are (7,2), (7,4) and (2,4) is in order, so the count is 3.

Careful with the last pair. 2 before 4 is correct, so that pair is not an inversion. Counting every pair that looks out of place overcounts; the count is of pairs i < j with ranki > rankj.

Why a NAT here. The count is a number, so a numerical-answer item can ask for it directly, which is how an order question can be made unambiguous.

Revision sheet — the three items on one screen

Rules, decision procedures and traps, in the order you would want them in the last hour before the paper.

Tense in seven lines

tense = when · aspect = how (perfect bit, continuous bit)
3 tenses × 4 aspects = 12 forms, all different
past perfect = earlier than the reference point
simple past = at or after it, when a word like after states the order
since + point and for + span → present perfect
in 2019, last year, yesterday → simple past
no will inside an if- or when-clause

Each line is one feature, and a question tests one feature at a time. Name the reference point before you name the form.

Adjectives in five lines

of the two → comparative · of the three → superlative
never a superlative with two things
more / most / very never sit on a comparative
absolute: unique, dead, fatal, square, unanimous, entire, infinite, pregnant
order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose

The count of things and the class of the adjective are both printed in the sentence; neither has to be guessed.

Conjunctions and agreement in five lines

coordinating (7): and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so
subordinating (9): because, although, though, whereas, while, since, unless, if, that
only a subordinator can be fronted with its clause
but / although → contrast · because / so → consequence · despite → concession
neither … nor → nearer noun · as well as → first noun

One relation takes one marker. Two markers for one relation is the doubled sentence the paper prints.

Traps, in one line each

  • Had wished after after moves the wrong event.
  • Will inside the if-clause marks the future twice.
  • The tallest of the two is emphatic and wrong.
  • Very less marks the comparison twice.
  • More unique adds a scale the word has closed.
  • The film was bored parses and means nothing.
  • But it rained, we stayed in is a fragment.
  • Although … but doubles one contrast.
  • The nearest noun is right in neither … nor and wrong in as well as.
  • Three distractors are usually correct English somewhere else.

What this lesson does not claim

It does not claim that the eight absolute adjectives are the only ones, or that more complete and more perfect are wrong — they are named as disputed and tested nowhere. It does not claim that one published adjective-order table is the only one: the age/shape pair is marked unsettled. It does not claim that every verb uses all twelve cells of the grid: stative verbs resist the continuous. And it does not claim that any shape will appear in the 2027 paper: the shapes described are those of the papers named, and the tagging that groups them is an archive's, not the exam's.

What it does claim, and shows on the canvas, is that the counts are computed: twelve forms that are all different, thirty modifier tests with twenty-four rejections, four inversion counts of 0, 3, 1 and 1, two swapped participle sentences, sixteen connectors split 7 and 9 by the fronting test, and seven agreement sentences on which neither rule scores seven.

What is computed rather than cited

Every count on the canvas is computed by this page's model and re-checked against the definition it came from: the twelve tense-and-aspect forms are generated for two verbs and counted for distinctness; the presence of the perfect and continuous markers is tested on every cell and compared with what the aspect name promises; each option of the 2017 item is given a relation to the reference point and the satisfying ones counted; six time expressions are typed as open or closed and the tense derived from the type; six sentences are marked with the position of the future verb and legality decided from it; the four options of the 2013 conditional are labelled by modal and the conditional ones counted; five comparison contexts are scored against the degree their count requires; four sentences are tested for a degree modifier on a comparative; thirty modifier tests are run on eight absolute adjectives and two controls; four adjective orders are scored by inversion count against the rank sequence; six participle sentences are tested by matching the suffix to the subject's role; the sixteen conjunctions are sorted by the fronting test and two polarity items scored; and seven agreement sentences are scored by both rules, with the split by construction counted. The number of passing checks is printed under the canvas.
