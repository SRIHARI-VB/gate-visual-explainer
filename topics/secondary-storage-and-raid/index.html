Topic and objective

TOPIC: Secondary storage — how a disk's geometry produces a capacity and an access time — and RAID, the levels built by striping that capacity across several disks with a copy or a parity block for safety.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a drive's geometry and spin rate, compute its capacity, convert a logical block address into cylinder, surface and sector, and decompose an access into seek, rotational latency and transfer. Given a disk count, compute the usable capacity, the fault tolerance, the number of disk operations a small write costs, and the probability of losing data — for RAID 0, 1, 5, 6 and 10.

I read this topic as one arithmetic, one mapping, one sum, and one table. Every number on this page — the 2,048,000,000 bytes, the 12.1833 ms, the 42 kB/s against 12.488 MB/s, the six data bytes and their parity 121, the 22 of 64 survivable patterns and the 0.0000196 — is computed at load by the lesson's own model and re-checked against the definition it claims.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class B — a prerequisite and a neighbour of two named syllabus clauses.

The GATE 2027 CS syllabus no longer says "secondary storage" in Computer Organization and Architecture. Its memory clause now reads "Memory interfacing and hierarchy: performance, cache memory mapping", and the Operating System section still names "CPU and I/O scheduling" and "file systems". I register this lesson class B: the geometry and access-time arithmetic is what a memory-hierarchy performance question and an I/O-scheduling question both assume, and it is not taught by any other lesson in this repository.

Community observation Two widely read syllabus-comparison write-ups report that disk access-time questions appeared repeatedly in recent papers — one of them counts five appearances between 2014 and 2026, the last in 2026 Set 1 — and both advise keeping the arithmetic in practice even though the 2027 wording dropped the words. That is a report about past papers, not a promise about 2027.

Educational interpretation RAID itself is not named anywhere in the syllabus. It is here because a question about capacity, redundancy or write cost is answerable only if the levels are understood, and because the arithmetic is the same arithmetic the syllabus asks for.

Prerequisites

Nothing on this page needs a previous lesson, but three help:

  • Disk scheduling — the same seek and rotation numbers, used to choose an order.
  • Memory hierarchy and cache — why a level exists at all, and what a hit ratio buys.
  • Number representation — powers of two, and why 1 TB shows as 931 GiB.

Approximate learning time: 70 minutes, of which about 25 is the animation.

Five ideas, and the rest follows

  1. Capacity is a product of four counts; nothing else on the drive is a unit of bytes.
  2. An access is seek plus half a turn plus transfer, and for a small read the transfer is almost nothing.
  3. Throughput is the request size divided by that fixed cost, so big requests are cheaper per byte by two orders of magnitude.
  4. A RAID level trades capacity for redundancy; parity spends one block per stripe, mirroring spends a whole copy.
  5. A small write on parity costs four disk operations, because the old data and the old parity must be read first.

Intuition: a warehouse, not a desk

Think of a warehouse with one forklift. Fetching anything takes three steps: drive to the right aisle, wait for the right shelf to come round on a turning carousel, then take the box. The box itself is small, so almost all the time is the drive and the wait. If you need one box, the trip costs the same as if you need fifty from the same shelf — which is why nobody sends a forklift for one box.

Educational interpretation The analogy maps: aisle = cylinder, carousel = platter rotation, box = sector, "one trip for many boxes" = block size. It stops mapping at caching: the warehouse has no memory of your last visit, while a disk controller does.

The formal objects, with every symbol in words

Platter — one magnetic disc. Surface — one side of a platter, holding one head; a drive with $$p$$ platters has $$2p$$ surfaces. Track — one circle on one surface, at one radius. Sector — the smallest addressable unit on a track. Cylinder — the set of tracks at the same radius on every surface, reached by one arm movement.

Capacity = surfaces $$\times$$ cylinders $$\times$$ sectors per track $$\times$$ bytes per sector. Every factor is a count, so the product is exact.

Access time, in three terms

Seek time — time to move the arm to the target cylinder. Rotational latency — time for the target sector to come under the head. Transfer time — time for the sector's bits to pass the head.

$$T_{\text{access}} = T_{\text{seek}} + T_{\text{rotation}} + T_{\text{transfer}}$$

with $$T_{\text{rotation}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{60\,000}{R}$$ milliseconds for a spin rate $$R$$ in revolutions per minute, and $$T_{\text{transfer}} = k\cdot\tfrac{60\,000}{R\,S}$$ for $$k$$ sectors on a track of $$S$$ sectors.

The three are sequential, so they add. They never overlap, and taking their maximum is wrong.

RAID vocabulary

Striping unit — the block dealt to one disk in one round. Stripe — the set of blocks dealt in one round, one per disk. Parity block — the exclusive-or of the stripe's data blocks, stored so that any one missing block can be recomputed.

For a level on $$N$$ identical disks of size $$S$$: usable capacity is $$N S$$ for RAID 0, $$S$$ for RAID 1, $$(N-1)S$$ for RAID 5, $$(N-2)S$$ for RAID 6 and $$\tfrac{N}{2}S$$ for RAID 10.

Read "tolerates $$k$$ failures" as a guarantee, not a hope: RAID 10 tolerates one failure for certain, and two only when they land in different mirrors.

The assumptions this lesson runs on

The drive is 4 platters, 8 surfaces, 1,000 cylinders, 500 sectors of 512 bytes per track, spinning at 7,200 rpm, with an average seek of 8 ms taken as a given. Sectors are numbered from 1 inside a track and from 0 as block addresses. The RAID arrays are six identical 1 TB disks, failures are independent, and the parity arithmetic is over GF(256) with the polynomial 0x11D. Every figure in this lesson is computed inside those assumptions; changing any of them changes the numbers, and the lesson says so wherever a number is quoted.

Nothing here describes an SSD. A solid-state drive has no seek and no rotation, so the whole three-term decomposition collapses to a queueing problem — that is a different lesson, not a footnote to this one.

Example 1 — capacity, and the two units

Step 1. Count the sectors: $$8 \times 1000 \times 500 = 4{,}000{,}000$$. Justification: every surface carries every cylinder, and every track carries 500 sectors.

Step 2. Multiply by the sector size: $$4{,}000{,}000 \times 512 = 2{,}048{,}000{,}000$$ bytes. Justification: the sector is the smallest addressable unit, so no smaller factor exists.

Step 3. Convert twice: $$2.048$$ GB in decimal units, and $$1.9073$$ GiB in binary units. Justification: $$10^9$$ against $$2^{30}$$, a ratio of 1.0737.

The same arithmetic explains why a 1 TB drive shows 931 GiB: $$10^{12} / 2^{30} = 931.3$$.

Example 2 — the last sector, three ways

Step 1. One cylinder holds $$8 \times 500 = 4{,}000$$ sectors.

Step 2. Address $$3{,}999{,}999 \div 4{,}000 = 999$$ remainder $$3{,}999$$ — so cylinder 999.

Step 3. $$3{,}999 \div 500 = 7$$ remainder $$499$$ — surface 7, and the remainder counts from zero, so the sector is $$499 + 1 = 500$$.

Step 4. Check by inversion: $$(999 \times 8 + 7)\times 500 + (500-1) = 3{,}999{,}999$$.

The model runs this round trip on all four million addresses and finds no disagreement.

Example 3 — a 4 KB read, average and worst

Eight sectors on one track: $$8 + 4.1667 + 8 \times 0.016667 = 12.300$$ ms.

Now the same read with a worst-case rotation — the sector has just passed the head, so a whole turn is needed:

$$8 + 8.3333 + 0.1333 = 16.4667\ \text{ms}$$

And if the eight sectors are scattered, each pays its own seek and rotation: $$8 \times 12.300 = 98.4$$ ms, eight times the contiguous read.

Note what changed between the three: the data is identical in all three, and only the position differs.

Example 4 — RAID 5 on six disks, four numbers

Capacity. $$(6-1)\times 1 = 5$$ TB, so 83.33 per cent of the six disks.

Fault tolerance. One disk: the parity of every stripe it touched can be recomputed. Two disks: two unknowns, one equation, gone.

Small write. Four operations — read old data, read old parity, write new data, write new parity. The array's write rate is a quarter of its read rate.

Reliability. At a one per cent per-disk failure chance, the probability of losing the array is 0.001460, against 0.058520 for RAID 0 on the same disks.

The parity value is not a mystery number: exclusive-or of 198, 140, 83, 25, 223 and 166 is 121.

Example 5 — why the same disk is two hundred and ninety-seven times faster

Take the identical drive and change only how much data one request carries. The access time barely moves; the delivered bytes move by two orders of magnitude.

One sector: 512 bytes in 12.1833 ms is 42.02 kB/s. A full track: 256,000 bytes in 20.5 ms is 12.488 MB/s. The ratio is 297.15. Nothing about the hardware changed, which is the entire argument for large blocks, read-ahead and sequential layout — and the reason a database's random-write workload is measured in operations per second rather than in megabytes per second.

The same arithmetic from the other side: this disk serves 81.3 random 4 KB reads per second, because each one pays 12.3 ms whether it wants to or not.

Boundary 1 — the run that leaves the track

The rule "each extra sector costs 0.0167 ms" holds only while the run stays on one track. A run that crosses to the next track pays the inter-sector gap; a run that crosses to another cylinder pays a whole seek. A 1 MB read therefore costs about $$1{,}000{,}000 / 256{,}000 \approx 4$$ track transfers and 4 seeks, not one seek.

Violated condition: the sectors are contiguous on one track.

Boundary 2 — a sector is not a byte

Reading one byte and reading 512 bytes cost the same, because the sector is the unit of transfer. A program that touches one byte per 4 KB block wastes 4,095 of every 4,096 bytes it fetches, and a benchmark that counts bytes read from the device will overstate the work by that factor.

Violated condition: the requested size is at least one whole block.

Boundary 3 — RAID 5 with two disks

The capacity formula $$(N-1)S$$ gives $$S$$ at $$N=2$$, which is a mirror. But the write penalty of four does not carry over: with two disks there is no separate data disk to read, so a write is two operations. The formula survives the boundary; the performance claim does not.

Violated condition: the array has at least three disks, so that a small write has an old data block and an old parity block to read.

Boundary 4 — RAID 10 and the second failure

Six disks in three mirrors: every single failure is survivable, and of the fifteen two-disk failures exactly three are fatal — the three that take both members of one pair. So "tolerates two failures" is true for twelve patterns and false for three. The guaranteed number is one.

Violated condition: the failures are spread across different mirrors.

Boundary 5 — independent failures

Every probability on this page assumes independent disk failures. Disks bought in one batch, in one chassis, at one temperature, do not fail independently: they fail together, and the second failure often arrives during the rebuild of the first, when the array is at its most fragile. The arithmetic is right and the model is wrong, which is the honest way to state it.

Violated condition: independence, and a repair time short compared with the time between failures.

Boundary 6 — the one-based sector

Block addresses start at 0; sectors inside a track are conventionally numbered from 1. The last address of this disk is cylinder 999, surface 7, sector 500 — not sector 499. Dropping the $$+1$$ gives a mapping that still round-trips and is still wrong, which is why the check compares against the stated convention and not merely against its own inverse.

Violated condition: the convention is stated. When a question does not state it, say which one you assumed.

1. "The average rotational latency is a full turn"

Why it tempts: the spin rate is quoted as revolutions per minute, so one revolution feels like the natural unit.

Why it is wrong: the head waits for the sector to arrive, not for the platter to complete a circuit. Over a uniform arrival phase the wait is uniform on $$[0, T)$$, whose mean is $$T/2$$.

Minimal counterexample: a sector already under the head waits 0 ms; a sector just past it waits one turn. Neither is half a turn, and their average is.

Correct rule: average $$= \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{60\,000}{R}$$ ms; worst case $$= \tfrac{60\,000}{R}$$ ms. Here: 4.1667 average, 8.3333 worst.

Question that exposes it: Q10 below, where using the average where the worst case is asked halves the answer.

2. "A 200 MB/s drive reads 4 KB at 200 MB/s"

Why it tempts: the number on the box is a rate, and a rate looks like a speed for every request.

Why it is wrong: the rate is the platter streaming rate, reached only when the head is already over the data and never leaves it. A small request pays the seek and the rotation first.

Minimal counterexample: this geometry streams 30.72 MB/s and delivers 42.02 kB/s for a one-sector read — a factor of 731, on the same drive.

Correct rule: effective bandwidth is request size divided by the full access time, and it depends on the request, not on the drive alone.

Question that exposes it: Q11 below.

3. "RAID 5 on six disks survives two failures, since five disks still hold data"

Why it tempts: most of the data really is still there, so the loss looks like a small hole.

Why it is wrong: one parity block gives one equation. Two lost blocks are two unknowns, and one equation cannot fix two unknowns — the stripe is unrecoverable, and the array is gone.

Minimal counterexample: the model removes disks 1 and 4 and the pair is solvable only after adding Q. With P alone, all fifteen two-failure cases fail.

Correct rule: parity of one block tolerates exactly one failure; two check blocks tolerate exactly two.

Question that exposes it: Q7, statement III.

4. "Parity is a checksum, so it only detects damage"

Why it tempts: the word "parity" is met first in error detection, where a parity bit only raises a flag.

Why it is wrong: exclusive-or is invertible. If you know the parity and all but one operand, the missing operand is forced: $$p \oplus d_1 \oplus \dots \oplus d_{i-1} \oplus d_{i+1} \dots = d_i$$. Detection needs the flag; reconstruction needs the arithmetic, and RAID uses the arithmetic.

Minimal counterexample: six bytes and their parity 121 — remove any one and the survivor is recovered exactly in all six cases.

Correct rule: a parity block is data you can solve for, not merely a flag you can check.

Question that exposes it: Q3 in the practice set of the linked disk-scheduling lesson asks the detection question; here the recovery question is Q9.

5. "More disks means more reliable"

Why it tempts: more disks means more copies of everything, in the mirroring picture.

Why it is wrong: without redundancy, adding a disk multiplies the chance of failure. A hundred disks at 200,000 hours each have an array MTTF of 2,000 hours — three months.

Minimal counterexample: RAID 0 on six disks loses data on all six single-failure patterns; a single disk loses data on one.

Correct rule: reliability comes from redundancy, and capacity comes from disks. They are different quantities and RAID 0 buys only the second.

Question that exposes it: Q12 below.

6. "RAID 5 and RAID 6 differ only in capacity"

Why it tempts: the capacity formulas differ by exactly one disk, so the difference looks like a disk.

Why it is wrong: the write path changes too. A small write on RAID 6 reads the old data and both old check blocks and writes all three new ones — six operations against four — so a write-heavy workload loses a third of its write throughput on top of the capacity.

Minimal counterexample: same six disks, same 1 TB each: RAID 5 gives 5 TB at four operations per write, RAID 6 gives 4 TB at six.

Correct rule: compare all four numbers — capacity, tolerance, write penalty, loss probability — or the comparison is not a comparison.

Question that exposes it: Q8 below.

What a question on this topic looks like

This topic is asked in four shapes, and each has a fixed procedure. No previous-year question is quoted on this page: no official paper was read for this lesson, so every question below and in the practice set is labelled original rather than dressed as a PYQ.

Category C — calculation, the commonest shape

Shape: a geometry and a spin rate, then "what is the average time to read a block".

Procedure: write the three terms separately, convert the spin rate to milliseconds per turn, halve it, then multiply the sector time by the number of sectors. Only then add.

Common wrong approach: multiplying the transfer rate by the block size and calling the product the answer, which ignores both the seek and the rotation — here 99.86 per cent of the total.

Category A (direct definition) appears as "define a cylinder" or "how many sectors per cylinder", and is answered by the product of two counts.

Category C — RAID capacity and cost

Shape: "N disks of size S in RAID 5; usable capacity?" or "how many disk accesses does one write need".

Procedure: decide whether the question is about capacity (a subtraction of parity disks), tolerance (a count of check blocks) or operations (a count of reads plus writes). Write the unit before the number.

Common wrong approach: answering the capacity question with the efficiency, or the tolerance question with the number of parity disks, which coincide for RAID 5 and diverge for RAID 6.

Category F (multiple conditions) is the trap version: capacity, tolerance and write cost asked together, where partial satisfaction looks like a correct option.

Category B and E — verify, or refute with one case

Shape: "which of these statements are true" about RAID levels, or "the following configuration tolerates two failures".

Procedure: for each statement, name the object it is about — a capacity, a pattern count, an operation count — and test the smallest case that could break it. For RAID 10, the smallest breaking case is two failures in one mirror.

Common wrong approach: accepting "tolerates two failures" for RAID 10 because half the disks are copies, when the guarantee is one.

Category G (misconception trap) is the same question with the trap in the wording: "since RAID 5 keeps a parity block on every disk, it survives two failures".

Category H — multi-step reasoning

Shape: "a file of 1 MB is read sequentially from this disk; how long does it take" — or the same with a random layout.

Procedure: convert the file to sectors, divide by sectors per track to get the tracks crossed, multiply tracks by (one seek + one rotation + one track transfer), and add the last partial track. Then compare with the random case, where each block pays its own seek.

Common wrong approach: dividing the file size by the platter rate, which assumes the head never has to move.

The 297-times ratio between a full-track read and a one-sector read is the number this category is built on.

Practice set — answers withheld until you reveal them

Every question here is an original practice question written for this lesson; none is quoted as an official GATE question, and no marks are assigned. Each answer states the reasoning, says why the other options fail, and names the trap the question is built on. Unless a question says otherwise, use this drive: 8 surfaces, 1,000 cylinders, 500 sectors of 512 bytes per track, 7,200 rpm, average seek 8 ms.

Q1 MCQ Original

What is the formatted capacity of the drive in bytes?

(A) 2,048,000,000   (B) 1,907,348,633   (C) 256,000,000   (D) 4,000,000

Answer: (A). $$8 \times 1000 \times 500 \times 512 = 2{,}048{,}000{,}000$$ bytes.

Why the others fail: (B) is the same capacity in GiB multiplied by $$10^9$$ — a unit confusion, not a different capacity. (C) drops the cylinders and the surfaces: it is one track's worth of bytes per hundred cylinders. (D) is the number of sectors, which is a count and not a byte total.

Trap: four factors, four counts. A missing factor gives an answer that looks plausible because every option is a round number.

Q2 NAT Original

Give the average rotational latency in milliseconds, to four decimal places.

Answer: 4.1667 (accept 4.16 to 4.17). One turn is $$60{,}000/7{,}200 = 8.3333$$ ms, and the average is half of that.

Why not 8.3333: that is the worst case, which occurs only when the sector has just passed the head. The average over a uniform arrival phase is half a turn.

Trap: the halving is a statement about the distribution of arrival phases. It is not a fudge factor, and it does not apply to the seek.

Q3 MCQ Original

For a one-sector read on this drive, which term of the access time is largest?

(A) seek   (B) rotational latency   (C) transfer   (D) they are equal by construction

Answer: (A). Seek is 8 ms of the 12.1833 ms total, which is 65.66 per cent. Rotation is 4.1667 ms, 34.20 per cent. Transfer is 0.0167 ms, 0.14 per cent.

Why the others fail: (B) is the second term here, but it becomes the largest as soon as the average seek falls below 4.17 ms — which is why the answer depends on the given drive and not on a general rule. (C) is the smallest by two orders of magnitude. (D) is simply false; the three are independent quantities.

Trap: the transfer rate is the only term printed on the drive's label, and it is the only term that is negligible here.

Q4 NAT Original

Eight contiguous sectors on one track are read. Give the access time in milliseconds to three decimal places.

Answer: 12.300 (accept 12.29 to 12.31). $$8 + 4.1667 + 8 \times 0.016667 = 8 + 4.1667 + 0.1333$$.

Why not 8 × 12.1833: the eight sectors are contiguous, so the seek and the rotation are paid once, not eight times. Multiplying would be right only if the sectors were scattered.

Trap: the phrase "on one track" is the whole question. It is what licenses paying the fixed cost once.

Q5 MCQ Original

Block address 3,999,999 maps to which cylinder, surface and sector?

(A) 999, 7, 499   (B) 999, 7, 500   (C) 999, 6, 500   (D) 1000, 0, 1

Answer: (B). $$3{,}999{,}999 = 999\times4000 + 3999$$, and $$3999 = 7\times500 + 499$$, so surface 7 and sector $$499+1 = 500$$.

Why the others fail: (A) forgets that sectors inside a track are numbered from one. (C) takes the surface from the last whole track rather than from the quotient of the remainder. (D) is the first address of a disk one cylinder larger — an off-by-one at the top end.

Trap: (A) round-trips perfectly under the same wrong convention, so checking your answer by inverting it will not catch this error. Check it against the stated numbering instead.

Q6 NAT Original

A RAID 5 array has 8 disks of 2 TB each. Give the usable capacity in TB.

Answer: 14. RAID 5 spends one disk per stripe on parity, so usable is $$(N-1)S = 7 \times 2 = 14$$ TB, which is 87.5 per cent of the 16 TB raw.

Why not 16 or 8: 16 is the raw total with no redundancy; 8 is the mirroring answer, which spends half the capacity rather than one disk.

Trap: the parity cost is one disk per array here because the array is a single parity group. Split into two groups of four and the usable capacity falls to 12 TB — the same disks, a different answer, decided by the grouping.

Q7 MSQ Original

Which statements about RAID 5 on six 1 TB disks are TRUE?

I. Usable capacity is 5 TB.
II. A small write costs four disk operations.
III. It survives any two simultaneous disk failures.
IV. The parity block for a stripe is the exclusive-or of that stripe's data blocks.

(A) I and II only   (B) I, II and IV   (C) II, III and IV   (D) All four

Answer: (B). I is $$(6-1)\times1$$. II is the read-modify-write: old data, old parity, new data, new parity. IV is the definition. III is false: one parity block is one equation, and two lost blocks are two unknowns.

Why the others fail: (A) drops the definition, which is the easiest of the four. (C) and (D) include III, the standard trap.

Trap: III sounds like "since there is parity on every disk, losing two is fine". Rotating where the parity lives does not add a second equation.

Q8 MCQ Original

RAID 4 and RAID 5 have the same capacity formula and the same write penalty. What does RAID 5 change?

(A) It removes the need to read the old parity.
(B) It distributes the parity across all disks, so writes to different stripes do not collide on one disk.
(C) It adds a second parity block, allowing two failures.
(D) It changes block-level striping into bit-level striping.

Answer: (B). In RAID 4 every stripe's parity lives on one disk, so two writes to different data disks still both touch it, and it becomes the bottleneck. Rotating the parity position by one disk per stripe lets those writes proceed in parallel.

Why the others fail: (A) would be a real saving, and RAID 5 does not make it — the old parity is still read. (C) describes RAID 6. (D) describes RAID 3, which stripes at bit level with a dedicated parity disk.

Trap: a single small write is no cheaper on RAID 5 than on RAID 4. What improves is the number of writes that can be in flight.

Q9 NAT Original

How many disk operations does one small write cost on RAID 6, counting reads and writes together?

Answer: 6. Read the old data, read the old P, read the old Q; write the new data, write the new P, write the new Q. Three reads and three writes.

Why not 4: four is the RAID 5 answer, with one check block. The second check block adds one read and one write.

Trap: the count is per host write, not per disk. Do not divide by the number of disks; the six operations are sequential in the worst case and can be spread only when several writes touch different stripes.

Q10 MCQ Original

A question asks for the maximum time to read one sector on this drive, with the seek also at its maximum of 12 ms. Which is the correct total?

(A) 16.1833 ms   (B) 20.3500 ms   (C) 24.5167 ms   (D) 12.0167 ms

Answer: (B). $$12 + 8.3333 + 0.0167 = 20.3500$$ ms: maximum seek, a whole turn, one sector of transfer.

Why the others fail: (A) uses the average rotation with the maximum seek. (C) doubles the seek as well as the rotation. (D) uses neither maximum and forgets the rotation entirely.

Trap: "maximum" applies to each term independently. Halving only the rotation is the commonest version of this error, and it is exactly option (A).

Q11 MCQ Original

This drive's platter streams 30.72 MB/s. A workload of random 4 KB reads achieves which throughput?

(A) 30.72 MB/s   (B) about 12.49 MB/s   (C) about 0.333 MB/s   (D) about 42 kB/s

Answer: (C). Each 4 KB read costs 12.3 ms, so 4096 bytes in 0.0123 s is 333 kB/s, or 81.3 reads per second.

Why the others fail: (A) is the platter rate, reachable only by a stream that never leaves the data. (B) is the full-track read, which pays one seek for 256,000 bytes. (D) is the one-sector read, which is even worse.

Trap: the answer is not a property of the drive. The same drive answers all four options, and the workload decides which one is true.

Q12 NAT Original

Six disks are arranged as RAID 10 — three mirrors, striped. Of the fifteen possible two-disk failure patterns, how many lose data?

Answer: 3. A pair loses data exactly when both of its disks fail. There are three mirrors, so exactly three of the fifteen two-failure patterns are fatal, and the other twelve are survived.

Why not 0 or 15: 0 assumes RAID 10 tolerates any two failures, which is the misconception. 15 assumes every second failure is fatal, which would be true only of RAID 1 on a single mirror.

Trap: the guaranteed tolerance is one failure. Three simultaneous failures, one per mirror, would also be survivable — but that is luck, and a guarantee is not luck.

Definitions, one line each

  • Surface — one side of a platter; two per platter.
  • Track — one circle on one surface.
  • Cylinder — the tracks at one radius across all surfaces; one seek reaches them all.
  • Sector — the smallest addressable unit; here 512 bytes.
  • LBA — a single zero-based number naming a sector.
  • Stripe — the blocks dealt in one round, one per disk.
  • Parity block — exclusive-or of a stripe's data blocks.

Formulas, with the condition each needs

  • Capacity $$= S_{\text{surfaces}} \times C \times T \times B$$ — exact, all four are counts.
  • One turn $$= 60\,000 / R$$ ms — $$R$$ in revolutions per minute.
  • Average rotation $$= \tfrac{1}{2}$$ turn — needs a uniform arrival phase.
  • Access $$= T_{\text{seek}} + T_{\text{rot}} + k\,T_{\text{sector}}$$ — needs the $$k$$ sectors contiguous on one track.
  • Effective bandwidth $$= \text{bytes} / T_{\text{access}}$$ — the request size, not the drive, decides.
  • RAID 5 capacity $$(N-1)S$$; RAID 6 $$(N-2)S$$; RAID 10 $$\tfrac{N}{2}S$$ — identical disks, one parity group.
  • Small write: $$1, 2, 4, 6, 2$$ operations for levels $$0, 1, 5, 6, 10$$.

Decision procedures

To convert an address: divide by sectors-per-cylinder for the cylinder, divide the remainder by sectors-per-track for the surface, add one to the last remainder for the sector.

To compute an access: write the three terms on three lines with units, then add. Never multiply a transfer rate by a size and stop.

To choose a RAID level: fix the quantity the question constrains — capacity, tolerance, write rate or loss probability — compute that one for each candidate, and read off the answer. If two candidates tie, the question is asking about a different quantity.

To test a "survives k failures" claim: count the check blocks. One check block, one equation, one unknown.

Traps, each with the case that defuses it

  • Average rotation is half a turn — but a worst-case question uses a whole one.
  • Transfer is not the dominant term for a small read: 0.14 per cent here.
  • RAID 5 tolerates one failure, not two, however many disks carry parity.
  • RAID 10 guarantees one failure; three are survivable only one per mirror.
  • Parity reconstructs, it does not merely detect.
  • Sectors inside a track count from one; block addresses from zero.
  • More disks is not more reliable without redundancy: 100 disks at 200,000 hours give 2,000 hours.

Official

GATE 2027 CS syllabus, IIT Madras. The wording quoted on the Overview tab — "Memory interfacing and hierarchy: performance, cache memory mapping" and, under Operating System, "CPU and I/O scheduling" and "file systems" — is the 2027 text as reported by the syllabus-comparison pages below. It is what this lesson classifies against.

What it supports: the class-B classification, and the statement that "secondary storage" is no longer named in the 2027 Computer Organization clause.

Textbook and lecture material

UC Riverside, CS 153 lecture on disks. Gives the three-term access time, the average-rotation formula $$T = \tfrac{1}{2}(60/R)$$ and the worked example at 7,200 rpm with 400 sectors per track. Supports: the decomposition, the halving, and the claim that the first bit of a sector is the expensive one.

Cornell CS 4410, "Disks and RAID". Gives the typical ranges — seek 5–15 ms, rotational 4–8 ms, transfer 25–50 µs — and the statement that on average only half a rotation is needed. Supports: the relative sizes of the three terms.

Columbia W4118, disks lecture. Gives the random-versus-sequential contrast with measured drive figures. Supports: the order-of-magnitude gap between a small random read and a sequential stream.

The RAID literature

Patterson, Gibson and Katz, "A Case for Redundant Arrays of Inexpensive Disks (RAID)", SIGMOD 1988. Defines levels 1–5, the read-modify-write rule that a small write uses "2 disks to perform 4 accesses — 2 reads and 2 writes", and the parity-disk bottleneck that level 5 removes by distributing parity. Supports: the four-operation small write, the RAID 4 versus RAID 5 distinction, and the reconstruction arithmetic.

Chen, Lee, Patterson and Katz, "RAID: High-Performance, Reliable Secondary Storage", ACM Computing Surveys 1994. Gives the six-access small write for P+Q redundancy, the left-symmetric parity placement, and the reliability expressions including the 3,000-year figure for a hundred-disk RAID 5 with a parity group of sixteen. Supports: the RAID 6 operation count, the parity layout, and every MTTF number on this page.

SNIA RAID 6 definition, as quoted in the encyclopaedia entry. Supports: the statement that RAID 6 must tolerate any two concurrent failures.

Community material, used as observation only

GATEOverflow, "GATE CSE 2027 Syllabus Update", and a syllabus-change table on prosyllabus.com. Both compare the 2026 and 2027 CS syllabi and both note that "main memory and secondary storage" is no longer named in Computer Organization. These are reports about past papers and about a syllabus diff. They are not official documents, no question from them is reproduced here, and no weightage claim in this lesson rests on them beyond the statement that the questions have appeared.

What the research did not settle

No official GATE paper was read for this lesson, so no previous-year question is quoted and no year or question number is claimed. Every practice question is labelled original.

The 2027 syllabus wording is taken from two independent comparison pages that agree with each other; the official PDF was not fetched directly here, so the exact sentence should be confirmed against it before relying on the classification.

The reliability figures assume independent failures and a repair time of one hour. The RAID paper itself shows that correlated failures and bit errors during a rebuild dominate real loss rates; that analysis is named here and not reproduced, and the numbers on this page should be read as the idealised model they are.
