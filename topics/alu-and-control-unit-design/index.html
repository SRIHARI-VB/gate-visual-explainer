Topic and objective

TOPIC: Design of the arithmetic and logic unit, the datapath it sits in, and the control unit that sequences it — the three things GATE 2027 Section 3 names in one breath: "Design of arithmetic and logic unit (ALU). Design of control unit – hardwired and microprogrammed."

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a set of ALU functions, produce the number of function-select lines. Given an addition, produce the four flags and say whether the carry and the overflow agree. Given a datapath and an instruction, produce the ordered list of micro-operations and the number of control steps. Given a microinstruction format, produce the word width and the control-memory size, in horizontal or in vertical encoding. Given a design description, say whether hardwired or microprogrammed control is the better choice, and justify it by the trade rather than by a slogan.

I read this topic as one mechanism in three parts: the datapath can do a small set of things, the ALU is the part that computes, and the control unit is the part that decides which thing happens in which step. The ALU arithmetic itself (two's complement, Booth, floating point) lives in Number Representation and Arithmetic and is not re-taught here; the gates and multiplexers live in Combinational Circuits; the step counter is a counter, which is Sequential Circuits. This lesson is the wiring between them, which is exactly what the syllabus asks for.

GATE relevance

Verified fact Class A — explicitly in the syllabus, and newly explicit.

Section 3 of the official GATE 2027 CS syllabus (IIT Madras, CS_GATE2027_Syllabus.pdf, read for this run) reads: "Instruction set and addressing modes. Design of arithmetic and logic unit (ALU). Design of control unit – hardwired and microprogrammed. Memory interfacing and hierarchy: performance, cache memory mapping. I/O interface (interrupt and DMA). Instruction pipelining, pipeline hazards."

The word design is doing real work. The 2026 wording was "ALU, data-path and control unit"; the 2027 wording names the design of the ALU and the design of the control unit, and names the two control-unit styles. Community observation Independent syllabus analyses read that as making hardwired and microprogrammed control fair game again after a quiet decade; that is their reading, not an official statement about marks.

Class A means a question here is inside the declared boundary. It does not mean one is guaranteed in GATE 2027, and it says nothing about marks. The previous-year questions quoted in this lesson are observations about old papers, not a forecast.

Prerequisites

Four published lessons carry the machinery this one uses:

  • Combinational Circuits — the adder, the multiplexer and the decoder. A one-bit ALU slice is an adder plus two gates plus a multiplexer, and a hardwired control unit is a decoder plus gates.
  • Number Representation and Arithmetic — two's complement, the meaning of the top bit, and why subtraction is addition of a complement.
  • Sequential Circuits — the step counter, the register with a load line, and the flip-flop behind every latch in a datapath.
  • Boolean Algebra and Minimisation — each control signal in a hardwired unit is a boolean function that has to be minimised.

The only extra mathematics is ceil(log2 k), used three times: for function-select lines, for a condition-select field, and for a vertical encoding. Scene 2 derives it and the page checks it for every k from 2 to 16.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. The ALU is combinational and obedient; the control unit is what decides.
  2. k functions need ceil(log2 k) select lines; n mutually exclusive signals in a vertical field need ceil(log2(n+1)) bits.
  3. Carry and overflow are different questions. V is the exclusive-or of the two carries at the top bit.
  4. One bus is one transfer per step; steps × clock period is the time.
  5. Hardwired is fastest and rigidest; vertical is narrowest and slowest. Width and speed pull in opposite directions.

Intuition: a workshop with one bench and one foreman

A workshop has benches, tools and a trolley that carries one part at a time between them. The benches and the trolley are the datapath: they are what can physically be done. The foreman with the clipboard is the control unit: he does not cut or drill anything, he only says what happens next, and in what order.

Three things follow immediately, and they are the three things GATE asks about. First, the tools do not know what job they are on — a saw cuts whether the foreman meant to cut or not, which is why the ALU needs function-select lines. Second, the trolley carries one part per trip, which is why a single-bus datapath needs more steps. Third, a foreman who has memorised one fixed routine is fast but cannot learn a new product, while a foreman who reads the routine off a card is slower but can be given a new card — which is hardwired control against microprogrammed control, exactly.

Educational interpretation The analogy is a teaching device, not a model of any real processor. It is chosen because it makes the two boundaries visible: what can be done, and what is done next.

Formal definition: the datapath

A datapath is a collection of registers, functional units and buses, together with the control inputs that select what each unit does. It holds the state of the computation between steps and provides the operations.

A micro-operation is one elementary transfer or computation performed in one step, written in register-transfer notation, for example:

TEMP1 ← R1

Read aloud: "in this step, the contents of register R1 are copied into register TEMP1". The arrow is a copy, not an equation — after the step both registers hold the same value.

A control step is one clock period in which a set of micro-operations executes together. A control signal is one wire that enables one such action: a register's load line, a bus driver, an ALU function bit.

Formal definition: the control unit

A control unit is the hardware that produces the control signals. Its inputs are the instruction (or the decoded instruction), the step number, and the condition flags; its outputs are the control signals for the current step.

control signals = f( instruction , step , flags )

Every design in this lesson is an implementation of that one equation. Hardwired control implements f as gates; microprogrammed control implements it as a table stored in a memory and read one word per step.

Two consequences, both examinable. Because the step number is an input, the same instruction produces different signals in different steps — that is what sequencing means. Because the flags are an input, the same instruction and step can produce different signals on different data — that is what conditional execution means.

Assumption this lesson keeps

Unless a question says otherwise, assume a single-cycle-per-step, non-pipelined machine: one control step is one clock, and the next instruction starts only after the previous one finishes. Pipelining is a separate lesson and a separate set of questions; every count in this lesson is for the non-pipelined case, and the counts would change under pipelining.

The ALU, defined by what it must do

An arithmetic and logic unit is a combinational block with two n-bit data inputs A and B, a k-bit function-select input F, and an n-bit result output Y, plus status outputs. For each value of F it computes one named function of A and B.

Y = fF(A, B)    for F = 0, 1, ..., 2k − 1

The word combinational is the load-bearing one: there is no clock and no state inside. The ALU produces an answer continuously, and the answer changes as soon as an input changes. It never remembers which function it performed last step, and it has no notion of an instruction.

Because F has k bits, it can name at most 2k functions. So a design with m functions needs the smallest k with 2k ≥ m, which is ceil(log2 m). Four functions: two lines. Eight: three. Five: three, with three of the eight codes unused.

The one-bit slice, built

Take one bit position i. The slice receives ai, bi and a carry-in ci, and produces yi and a carry-out ci+1.

  • An AND gate computes ai · bi.
  • An OR gate computes ai + bi.
  • A full adder computes the sum bit and the carry-out.
  • A 4-to-1 multiplexer, addressed by two function bits, chooses which of those leaves as yi.

All three computations happen at the same time; only the choice depends on F. That is why adding a function costs a wider multiplexer and not a longer delay.

Subtraction, for the price of one invert line

In two's complement, −B is the bitwise complement of B plus one. So

A − B = A + B̅ + 1

A single control line, call it Binvert, gates an exclusive-or into every bit of B. When Binvert is 1, the adder receives B̅ and the carry-in of the bottom slice is forced to 1. That is the entire subtractor.

Mathematical derivation Verified here on all 65536 pairs of eight-bit values: (a − b) mod 256 equals (a + (~b) + 1) mod 256 for every pair. The identity is exact, not approximate.

The four status flags

These are wires tapped off the adder, so they cost no extra step:

  • C, carry. The carry out of the top slice. It is the unsigned overflow: 1 exactly when the unsigned sum does not fit.
  • Z, zero. 1 when every bit of the result is 0, computed as the NOR of all result bits.
  • S, sign. The top bit of the result. It is the sign only if the operands are being read as signed.
  • V, overflow. For signed numbers, the carry into the top slice exclusive-or the carry out of it.

C and V answer different questions about the same adder. C asks "did the unsigned value escape the width"; V asks "did the signed value escape the width". Neither implies the other.

Where the intuition must stop

"Overflow means the carry came out" is the belief that costs the most marks in this topic, and it is false in both directions: 127 + 1 in eight bits overflows with no carry out, and 255 + 1 carries out with no overflow. Both are checked on the page over all 65536 pairs, and both are worked in the Examples tab.

Hardwired control, defined

A hardwired control unit implements f as a combinational network. Its standard parts are:

  • an instruction decoder: m opcode bits in, 2m one-hot lines out;
  • a step counter: s steps, needing ceil(log2 s) bits, or a ring of s flip-flops;
  • a condition and flag block: the status bits that make a step conditional;
  • a gate matrix: one boolean function per control signal, of the above.

The signal appears after the gate delay of that matrix — one or two levels if it is minimised well. The price is that the matrix is fixed in silicon: a new instruction changes it.

Microprogrammed control, defined

A microprogrammed control unit stores the control signals in a control memory (control store). One word is a microinstruction, and its fields are conventionally:

  • control field — the signals for this step;
  • condition-select field — which status bit decides a branch;
  • next-address field — where the next microinstruction is.
width = control bits + condition bits + address bits

A microprogram counter holds the address being executed. The next address field is what allows branching, and therefore conditional execution and shared routines.

Horizontal and vertical, defined

Horizontal microprogramming gives each control signal its own bit: the control field is S bits for S signals. Nothing is decoded, and any combination of signals can be expressed in one word.

Vertical microprogramming encodes groups of mutually exclusive signals into fields. A group of n signals that are never asserted together needs

ceil( log2(n + 1) ) bits

The +1 is not decoration. The field must be able to select no signal at all, so n signals plus "none" is n+1 meanings. For n = 4 that is 3 bits, not 2.

Mathematical derivation The page checks this rule for n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 15 and 16, and shows where ceil(log2 n) would be one bit short.

Two conventions you must state in an answer

When a question gives "c status bits" for the condition field, two readings exist and both appear in real papers:

  • Direct selection: the multiplexer is addressed by the c status bits themselves, so the field is c bits.
  • Encoded selection: one code is needed per status bit plus one for "unconditional", so the field is ceil(log2(c + 1)) bits.

The 2004 question below uses the first reading with c = 8, giving 3 bits — the same number the second reading gives for c = 7. State which reading you used; the arithmetic follows from it, and examiners have keyed both.

The whole topic in one sentence

The datapath says what can happen, the ALU is the arithmetic part of it, and the control unit is a function of instruction, step and flags that says what does happen — implemented as gates (fast, rigid) or as a memory of microinstructions (slower, changeable), in a wide undecoded format or a narrow encoded one.

Example 1 — Counting select lines for an ALU

Question. A designer wants an ALU with these functions: AND, OR, XOR, NOT A, add, subtract, increment A, and compare-and-set-less-than. How many function-select lines are needed, and how many codes are left over?

  1. Count the functions. Justification: the count is of distinct functions the multiplexer must choose between, so increment and add are two entries even though both use the adder.
  2. There are 8 functions.
  3. Find the smallest k with 2k ≥ 8. Justification: k lines address a 2k-input multiplexer.
  4. 23 = 8, so k = 3.
  5. Codes left over: 8 − 8 = 0.

Now delete one function. Seven functions still need 3 lines, because 22 = 4 < 7. One code is left over. That is the whole content of the rule: k = ceil(log2 m), and the waste is 2^k − m.

Example 2 — The four flags on 127 + 1

Eight-bit operands A = 0111 1111 (127) and B = 0000 0001 (1).

  1. Add bit by bit. The low seven bits are all ones plus 000 0001, which produces carries that propagate to the top.
  2. The carry into the top bit is 1; the top bits are 0 + 0 + 1 = 1, so the carry out of the top bit is 0.
  3. Result = 1000 0000.
  4. Z = 0, because the result is not zero. S = 1, because the top bit is 1.
  5. C = 0 (carry out of the top slice). V = 1 XOR 0 = 1.

Read as signed, 127 + 1 = −128: wrong, and V says so. Read as unsigned, 127 + 1 = 128: correct, and C = 0 agrees. The same adder, two readings, two flags.

Example 3 — The four flags on 255 + 1

A = 1111 1111, B = 0000 0001.

  1. Every bit position carries into the next; the top bit produces a carry out.
  2. Result = 0000 0000. Carry out of the top slice = 1.
  3. The carry into the top slice was also 1.
  4. C = 1, V = 1 XOR 1 = 0.
  5. Z = 1, S = 0.

Read as unsigned, 255 + 1 = 256 does not fit, and C = 1 says so. Read as signed, −1 + 1 = 0, which is perfectly representable, and V = 0 agrees. Examples 2 and 3 together are the counterexample pair for "carry means overflow".

Example 4 — Width and size of a horizontal control memory

Question. A horizontal microprogrammed unit has 40 control signals, 7 status conditions plus an unconditional option, and 512 words of control memory. Find the microinstruction width and the control memory size.

  1. Control field: horizontal, so one bit per signal = 40 bits.
  2. Condition field: 7 conditions plus unconditional = 8 meanings, so ceil(log2 8) = 3 bits.
  3. Address field: 512 words, so log2 512 = 9 bits.
  4. Width = 40 + 3 + 9 = 52 bits.
  5. Size = 512 × 52 = 26 624 bits = 3328 bytes.

The page computes all five of these numbers and checks that the field widths add to the stated total, so the arithmetic here is verified rather than asserted.

Example 5 — The five micro-operations of R0 ← R1 + R2 (GATE CSE 2020, Q4)

The datapath is the single-bus one from the 2020 paper: one bus, registers R0–R7, MAR, MDR, IR, PC, TEMP1 as a latched ALU input, TEMP2 as a latched ALU output, and the ALU fed from TEMP1 and from the bus. The five given steps are numbered 1 to 5 in the paper; here they are named:

  1. a = R2r, TEMP1r, ALUadd, TEMP2w — put R2 on the bus, add TEMP1, latch the sum in TEMP2.
  2. b = R1r, TEMP1w — put R1 on the bus, latch it into TEMP1.
  3. c = PCr, MARw, MEMr — send PC to MAR and start a memory read.
  4. d = TEMP2r, R0w — put the sum on the bus and write it into R0.
  5. e = MDRr, IRw — copy the fetched word from MDR into IR.

Now impose the dependencies, and only the dependencies:

  • c before e: the memory read must complete before MDR holds the instruction.
  • b before a: step a reads TEMP1, so TEMP1 must already hold R1.
  • a before d: step d reads TEMP2, so the sum must already be latched.
  • c, e before a, b, d: the instruction must be decoded before its operands are fetched.

Answer: c, e, b, a, d — that is 3, 5, 2, 1, 4, option (C) of the paper. Option (D) is 3, 5, 1, 2, 4, which uses TEMP1 in step 1 before step 2 has filled it; that is the trap the question is built around. The page checks each option against the four dependencies and confirms that only one survives.

Example 6 — Steps on a single bus against three buses

Question. For R3 ← R1 + R2, how many control steps does a single-bus datapath need, and how many does a three-bus datapath need? Assume the ALU has two latched inputs and its output goes straight to a bus.

Single bus. One transfer per step, so:

t0: A ← R1    t1: B ← R2    t2: R3 ← A + B

Three steps. If the ALU output must first be latched into a temporary, add t3: R3 ← TEMP, giving four.

Three buses. Both operands travel in on two buses while the result leaves on the third, so the whole addition is one step. The price is the wiring and a longer clock: three buses and the ALU must all settle within the period.

Example 7 — Vertical encoding of a 4-signal group

Question. Four control signals, of which at most one is ever asserted in a step. How many bits does the field need under vertical encoding, and what does each code mean?

  1. Meanings required: signal 1, signal 2, signal 3, signal 4, or none = 5.
  2. ceil(log2(4+1)) = ceil(log2 5) = 3 bits.
  3. Codes: 000 = none, 001 = s1, 010 = s2, 011 = s3, 100 = s4. Three codes are unused.

Two bits would give only four codes, and one of them would have to mean both "signal 4" and "none". That is why the answer is 3 and not 2 — and it is why the "+1" in the formula is worth remembering as a sentence, not as a symbol.

Example 8 — Reading the operation off a micro-operation sequence (GATE CSE 2013, Q28)

The sequence given is: MBR ← PC; MAR ← X; PC ← Y; Memory ← MBR.

  1. MBR ← PC copies the return address out of the program counter.
  2. Memory ← MBR writes it to memory at the address in MAR. A return address is being saved.
  3. PC ← Y loads a new address into the program counter.
  4. Nothing in the sequence reads memory, so it is not an instruction fetch or an operand fetch.
  5. A conditional branch changes PC but does not save it. Saving PC and then jumping is the signature of entering a service routine.

Answer: initiation of interrupt service. The reasoning is entirely about which register is written and whether memory is read or written — not about the mnemonic of any instruction.

Counterexample: overflow with no carry out

Eight bits, A = 0111 1111 (127), B = 0000 0001 (1). The sum is 1000 0000. The carry into the top bit is 1 and the carry out is 0, so V = 1; C = 0. The condition violated: the belief that overflow requires a carry out.

The page enumerates all 65536 pairs and reports how many overflow with no carry out. The count is not zero, and that is the refutation.

Counterexample: carry out with no overflow

Eight bits, A = 1111 1111 (−1 signed, 255 unsigned), B = 0000 0001. The result is 0000 0000 with carry out 1, so C = 1 and V = 0. The condition violated: the belief that a carry out signals a signed error.

Read as unsigned the addition genuinely escaped the width, so C is right. Read as signed nothing escaped, so V is right. Both flags are correct; the mistake was answering one question with the other's flag.

Counterexample: ceil(log2 n) for a vertical field

n = 4 mutually exclusive signals. The naive rule gives ceil(log2 4) = 2 bits, which is 4 codes — exactly enough for the four signals and not one code left for "none". The condition violated: the field must be able to assert nothing.

The correct width is ceil(log2(4+1)) = 3. The rule fails for every power of two: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 each need one bit more than the naive formula. The page checks all of them.

Counterexample: a single bus cannot do it in one step

"The ALU computes A + B in one gate delay, so R0 ← R1 + R2 is one step." The first clause is true and the second does not follow: the bus can carry only one value, so R1 and R2 cannot both arrive in the same step. The condition violated: one bus, one transfer per step.

On the 2020 datapath the execute phase alone is three steps, and with the two-step fetch the instruction is five. Any option claiming fewer steps has put two sources on the bus at once.

Boundary: one function, one signal, one word

  • m = 1 function needs k = 0 select lines. An ALU that only adds has no multiplexer and no select input at all.
  • m = 2 functions needs k = 1. The step from 1 to 2 functions costs one wire.
  • n = 1 signal in a vertical group needs 1 bit, not 0, because the field must still be able to say "none".
  • 512 words need 9 address bits, and the address field can express exactly 512 distinct words — 511 and 512 are both representable, so the memory is full.
  • 1 status bit in a condition field with an unconditional option needs 1 bit: branch-on-flag or branch-always.

Boundary: what happens at the widest values

  • Adder width. A ripple-carry adder of width n has delay proportional to n, so the ratio between a 16-bit and a 32-bit version is 2 — but a look-ahead version's ratio is much smaller, because its depth grows like log n.
  • Subtraction of equal values. A − A gives 0, so Z = 1, S = 0, C = 1 (the carry out of adding A + A̅ + 1 is 1), and V = 0. The borrow is not a separate flag; it is the complement of C.
  • Address field of 0 bits. A control memory with exactly one word needs no address field and no microprogram counter increment: the microprogram cannot branch.
  • Instruction needing more steps than the counter. The step counter must cover the longest instruction; a shorter one simply finishes early, which is why the step count in the counter is a maximum, not a per-instruction value.

Closely confused pair 1: carry against overflow

Carry (C) is about unsigned arithmetic: the carry out of the top slice, meaning the unsigned sum did not fit. Overflow (V) is about signed arithmetic: the exclusive-or of the two carries at the top slice, meaning the signed sum did not fit.

They coincide on some inputs and differ on others, which is why neither can be computed from the other. On 127 + 1 they differ; on 255 + 1 they differ the other way. The one thing that is always true: C = 1 and V = 1 together mean the result fits as neither signed nor unsigned.

Closely confused pair 2: control word against machine instruction

A machine instruction is what a programmer writes and what main memory holds. A microinstruction is one word of control memory, holding control signals for one step. A micro-operation is the action such a word requests.

So one machine instruction expands into several microinstructions, each of which may request several micro-operations in parallel. A question that says "the control memory holds the program" is wrong; it holds the control of the program.

"More ALU functions need more select bits, one per function"

Why it tempts: the select lines are described as "choosing a function", and it is natural to imagine one wire per choice, the way a decoder's outputs are one-hot.

Minimal counterexample: 4 functions. One bit per function would be 4 bits; the true answer is 2 bits, because 22 = 4 codes already distinguish four functions.

Correct rule: k = ceil(log2 m) select lines for m functions, and 2k − m codes are unused. The one-hot picture applies to the decoder inside the control unit, not to the ALU's select input.

"Overflow is the carry out"

Why it tempts: for unsigned arithmetic it is exactly right, and most first examples are unsigned. The word "overflow" is used for both.

Minimal counterexample: 127 + 1 in eight bits overflows with C = 0, and 255 + 1 carries out with V = 0.

Correct rule: V is the exclusive-or of the carry into and the carry out of the top slice. C is the carry out alone. State which arithmetic you are reading the bits as before naming a flag.

"Microprogrammed control is faster, because a program is easier than a circuit"

Why it tempts: software feels more flexible and flexibility feels like progress; and the control store is small and fast.

Minimal counterexample: every control signal in a microprogrammed unit waits for a memory read, and every signal in a hardwired unit waits for one gate. The 1999 paper keys the order hardwired > horizontal > vertical.

Correct rule: hardwired is fastest and least flexible. Microprogrammed is slower and more flexible. Flexibility is bought with time, not with speed.

"Vertical microprogramming is faster because the word is narrower"

Why it tempts: a narrower word means less memory and a shorter read, so "less" sounds like "faster".

Minimal counterexample: a narrower word carries encoded fields, and encoded fields must be decoded before they can drive control lines. The decoder sits in the path.

Correct rule: width and speed run in opposite directions. Horizontal is wide and fast; vertical is narrow and slow. Memory size and speed are the two ends of the same trade.

"Control memory size is the number of control signals"

Why it tempts: the control field is one bit per signal in horizontal format, so the two numbers look like the same quantity.

Minimal counterexample: 40 signals in 512 words of 52 bits is 26 624 bits, not 40. Even in words the answer is 512, not 40.

Correct rule: size = words × width, and width = control + condition + address. Read whether the answer is wanted in words, in bits or in bytes.

"The ALU decodes the instruction"

Why it tempts: the ALU is the part that "does the operation", so it feels like the part that knows which operation to do.

Minimal counterexample: the ALU has no input for an opcode, only a k-bit function-select input. Two different instructions that both add present the same select code.

Correct rule: decoding is the control unit's job. The ALU receives a select code and obeys it; it is combinational and stateless.

"A wider datapath is always faster"

Why it tempts: more buses means fewer steps, and fewer steps means less time.

Minimal counterexample: a three-bus datapath does the addition in one step instead of three, but its clock must cover three bus delays and the ALU, so the period is longer. Total time is steps × period, and both terms moved.

Correct rule: compare total time, never step count alone. This is the same trap as comparing algorithms by operation count while ignoring the cost of an operation.

"The step counter is the program counter"

Why it tempts: both are counters, both feed a decoder, both appear in the control unit's input list.

Minimal counterexample: a program counter must address main memory, so it is as wide as an address. A step counter must count the steps of one instruction, typically fewer than ten.

Correct rule: the program counter belongs to the datapath side of the fetch; the step counter belongs to the control unit. Their widths differ by orders of magnitude.

Five question categories, and the reasoning each demands

Every previous-year question I could verify on this topic is one of these five. The category tells you what to compute; the trap tells you what people compute instead.

1. Field arithmetic on a microinstruction

Shape: a total width, some fields given, one field to find, and a question about control-memory size.

Reasoning: write the width equation, fill in the known fields, solve for the unknown. Then convert the address field to a word count with 2bits.

Common wrong approach: treating the condition count as the condition width. Eight status bits is not an 8-bit field; it is a 3-bit select, because 23 = 8.

Seen in: GATE CSE 2004 (the 26-bit microinstruction), and in the same shape in GATE IT 2008.

2. Ordering micro-operations on a given datapath

Shape: a datapath diagram, an instruction, and a shuffled list of steps to put in order.

Reasoning: write down the read-before-write dependencies only, then find the one permutation that respects all of them. Fetch always precedes execute.

Common wrong approach: ordering the steps by their numbering, or grouping "the arithmetic ones" together without checking which register each one reads.

Seen in: GATE CSE 2020 Q4.

3. Naming the operation from a micro-operation sequence

Shape: four or five register transfers, and a choice between instruction fetch, operand fetch, branch, interrupt, call.

Reasoning: ask which register is written and whether memory is read or written. Writing PC to memory is saving a return address; reading memory into IR is a fetch.

Common wrong approach: seeing PC and answering "branch", without noticing that PC is being saved rather than merely replaced.

Seen in: GATE CSE 2013 Q28.

4. Conceptual comparison, often multiple-select

Shape: statements about horizontal, vertical, hardwired; or an ordering question.

Reasoning: anchor on three facts — one bit per signal (horizontal), encoded fields need ceil(log2(n+1)) (vertical), gates are faster than a memory read which is faster than a memory read plus a decoder.

Common wrong approach: assuming "smaller" means "faster", which reverses the vertical answer.

Seen in: GATE CSE 1999 Q2.19 and GATE CSE 2002 Q2.7.

5. Flag arithmetic on an ALU operation

Shape: two operands in binary, and a question about which flags are set, or whether an overflow occurred.

Reasoning: compute the carry into and out of the top slice separately. V is their exclusive-or; C is the carry out alone. Z is "result is all zeros"; S is the top bit.

Common wrong approach: computing only the carry out and calling it overflow.

Status: no previous-year question in this exact shape was verified for this lesson, so the practice items on flags are labelled original. The reasoning they test is the one the syllabus's "design of ALU" wording names.

The decision procedure, in four questions

  1. What is being asked for? Select lines, word width, control-memory words, or control steps. Name it before computing; the four are independent.
  2. Which format? Horizontal means one bit per signal. Vertical means encoded fields with the +1 for "none". Hardwired means no control memory at all, so width questions do not apply.
  3. Which direction is the dependency? For ordering questions, list read-before-write pairs only. For flag questions, list the two carries at the top slice only.
  4. Words, bits or bytes? Read the options' units before answering the size question. 1024 words, 26 624 bits and 3328 bytes are the same memory.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2004, 2 marks

The microinstructions stored in the control memory of a processor have a width of 26 bits. Each microinstruction is divided into three fields: a micro-operation field of 13 bits, a next-address field (X), and a MUX select field (Y). There are 8 status bits in the inputs of the MUX. How many bits are there in the X and Y fields, and what is the size of the control memory in number of words?

  1. 10, 3, 1024
  2. 8, 5, 256
  3. 5, 8, 2048
  4. 10, 3, 512

Answer: A — X = 10, Y = 3, 1024 words

Y selects among 8 status bits, so ceil(log2 8) = 3 bits. The width equation then gives X = 26 − 13 − 3 = 10. A 10-bit address field addresses 210 = 1024 words.

B and C swap the two fields, which would make a 5-bit address reach 2048 words — impossible, since 25 = 32. D has the right fields and the wrong word count: a 10-bit address cannot address only 512 words.

The page recomputes 13 + 3 + 10 = 26 and 210 = 1024, and confirms that only option A satisfies both. Note the reading used: the 8 status bits address the multiplexer directly, so the field is 3 bits rather than ceil(log2 9) = 4.

MCQEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 1999, Q2.19

Arrange the following configurations for a CPU in decreasing order of operating speeds: hardwired control, vertical microprogramming, horizontal microprogramming.

  1. Hardwired control, vertical microprogramming, horizontal microprogramming
  2. Hardwired control, horizontal microprogramming, vertical microprogramming
  3. Horizontal microprogramming, vertical microprogramming, hardwired control
  4. Vertical microprogramming, horizontal microprogramming, hardwired control

Answer: B

Hardwired control produces its signals from gates, so it is fastest. Between the two microprogrammed styles, horizontal stores the signals directly and needs no decoding, while vertical stores them encoded and must decode them first, so horizontal is faster.

A and C both put vertical ahead of horizontal. C and D also put a microprogrammed style ahead of hardwired, which reverses the basic structural fact.

The reasoning is structural, not measured: the number of stages between the microprogram counter and the control lines is one for horizontal (a memory read) and two for vertical (a read and a decode), against zero for hardwired (gates only).

MCQEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2002, Q2.7

Horizontal microprogramming:

  1. does not require use of signal decoders
  2. results in larger sized microinstructions than vertical microprogramming
  3. uses one bit for each control signal
  4. all of the above

Answer: D — all of the above

Each control signal has its own bit, so the control field is one bit per signal — statement 3. Because every bit drives its line directly, no decoder is needed — statement 1. And because no encoding compresses the field, the word is wider than a vertical one — statement 2.

The three statements are not independent: 3 is the cause of 1 and of 2. A question that offers only one of them and asks which is "the" property is testing whether you know the causal direction, not whether you memorised the list.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE CSE 2020, Q4

On the single-bus datapath of the paper, the instruction is R0 ← R1 + R2, and the five given steps are:

1. R2r, TEMP1r, ALUadd, TEMP2w   2. R1r, TEMP1w   3. PCr, MARw, MEMr   4. TEMP2r, R0w   5. MDRr, IRw

Which one of the following is the correct order of execution of the above steps?

  1. 2, 1, 4, 5, 3
  2. 1, 2, 4, 3, 5
  3. 3, 5, 2, 1, 4
  4. 3, 5, 1, 2, 4

Answer: C — 3, 5, 2, 1, 4

The fetch is 3 then 5: the address must be in MAR before memory is read, and the word must be in MDR before it can enter IR. Then step 2 fills TEMP1 with R1, step 1 adds R2 (from the bus) to TEMP1 and latches the sum in TEMP2, and step 4 writes TEMP2 into R0.

A and B execute before fetching. D puts step 1 before step 2, so it reads TEMP1 before anything has written it — the single dependency the question is built on.

The page models the four read-before-write dependencies and checks all 120 permutations: exactly one satisfies every dependency, and it is 3, 5, 2, 1, 4. The official key is (C).

MCQEasyPYQ — GATE CSE 2013, Q28

Consider the following sequence of micro-operations: MBR ← PC; MAR ← X; PC ← Y; Memory ← MBR. Which one of the following is a possible operation performed by this sequence?

  1. Instruction fetch
  2. Operand fetch
  3. Conditional branch
  4. Initiation of interrupt service

Answer: D — initiation of interrupt service

PC is copied out and then written to memory at address X, which is the saving of a return address; PC is then loaded with Y, which is the transfer of control to a routine. That pair — save PC, then jump — is what entering a service routine does.

A would read memory into MBR, not write PC to it. B would also read memory. C changes PC but does not save it.

The tell is the direction of the memory operation. Every fetch reads; this sequence writes.

NATEasyOriginal practice

An ALU must implement 12 distinct functions. The minimum number of function-select lines is ______.

Answer: 4

Three lines give 23 = 8 codes, which is fewer than 12. Four lines give 16 codes, so 4 is the smallest k with 2k ≥ 12. Four of the 16 codes are unused.

The trap is answering 3 by rounding down, or answering 12 by imagining one wire per function. The page checks the rule for every m from 2 to 16.

MSQMediumOriginal practice

An eight-bit ALU adds A = 0110 1010 and B = 0111 0100. Which of the following statements are TRUE about the flags?

  1. The carry flag C is 0.
  2. The overflow flag V is 1.
  3. The zero flag Z is 1.
  4. The sign flag S is 1.

Answer: A, B and D

106 + 116 = 222 = 1101 1110. No carry leaves the top bit, so C = 0 (A is true). Both operands have top bit 0 and the result has top bit 1, so the carry into the top bit differs from the carry out: V = 1 (B is true). The result is not zero, so Z = 0 (C is false). The top bit is 1, so S = 1 (D is true).

Read as signed: 106 + 116 should be 222, which does not fit in a signed eight-bit range of −128 to 127, so an overflow is exactly right. Read as unsigned: 222 fits in eight bits, so no carry is exactly right. Both flags are correct at once, which is the point.

NATMediumOriginal practice

A vertical-format microinstruction encodes a group of 7 mutually exclusive control signals in one field. The minimum width of that field in bits is ______.

Answer: 3

The field must name one of 7 signals or none, so it needs 8 meanings: ceil(log2(7+1)) = 3. Codes 000 to 110 select a signal; 111 means none.

Here the naive rule happens to give the same answer, because 7 is not a power of two. Try 8 signals instead and the two rules part company: ceil(log2 8) = 3 is too few, and 4 is right. That is why the rule is written with the +1.

MCQMediumPYQ — GATE IT 2008, Q7 (the IT paper)

Consider a CPU where all instructions require 7 clock cycles. There are 140 instructions in the instruction set, and 125 control signals are needed. A horizontal microprogrammed control unit is designed using a single address field format for branch control logic. What is the minimum size of the control word and of the control address register?

  1. 125, 7
  2. 125, 10
  3. 135, 9
  4. 135, 10

Answer: D — 135 bits and 10 bits

140 instructions at 7 cycles each is 980 microinstructions, which need an address of ceil(log2 980) = 10 bits, since 29 = 512 < 980 ≤ 1024 = 210. Horizontal format gives 125 control bits, so the word is 125 + 10 = 135 bits. The control address register must hold that address, so it is 10 bits.

A and B forget the address field. C takes 9 address bits, which can reach only 512 of the 980 microinstructions.

This is the same arithmetic as the 2004 question, with the unknown moved: there the width was given and the fields were asked for, here the fields are given and the width is asked for. The page checks ceil(log2 980) = 10 and 125 + 10 = 135.

NATMediumOriginal practice

A single-bus datapath has an ALU with two latched input registers and an output latch, and one bus. How many control steps are needed to execute R3 ← R1 + R2, counting only the execute phase and assuming the result must pass through the output latch before reaching R3?

Answer: 4

One transfer per step: A ← R1, then B ← R2, then TEMP ← A + B, then R3 ← TEMP. Each step has one bus source.

If the ALU output could drive the bus directly to R3 in the same step, it would be 3. The difference is one latch, which is one clock — the point of the trade in scene 6.

MSQHardOriginal practice

Which of the following statements about hardwired and microprogrammed control are TRUE?

  1. A hardwired control unit produces its control signals with no control-memory access.
  2. A vertical microprogrammed unit generally has a smaller control memory than a horizontal one for the same microprogram.
  3. Adding a new instruction to a hardwired control unit requires only a new word in control memory.
  4. A microprogrammed unit can share a common fetch routine among all instructions.

Answer: A, B and D

A is the definition: gates, not a memory. B is what encoding buys: fewer bits per word, so a smaller store. D is what the next-address field makes possible: one fetch sequence reached by a jump from every instruction.

C is false, and it is the sentence that reverses the trade. Adding an instruction to a hardwired unit means changing the gate network; adding one to a microprogrammed unit means writing new microinstructions.

Note that B and D are the two advantages of microprogramming, while A is the one advantage of hardwiring. A question can ask for advantages of either, so read which side it wants.

The revision sheet

Everything below is either a definition, a formula the page computes, or a fact quoted from a named source. Nothing here needs a diagram.

Definitions in one line each

  • Datapath — registers, functional units and buses, plus the control inputs that select what each does.
  • Micro-operation — one elementary transfer or computation in one step.
  • Control step — one clock in which a set of micro-operations runs.
  • Control signal — one wire enabling one action.
  • Hardwired control — control signals from gates, with inputs instruction, step, flags.
  • Microprogrammed control — control signals from a control memory, one word per step.
  • Microinstruction — control field + condition-select field + next-address field.
  • Horizontal — one bit per control signal.
  • Vertical — encoded fields, one code reserved for "none".

Formulas, each with its assumption

k = ceil(log2 m)

Select lines for m ALU functions. Assumes each function is selected by one code; unused codes are allowed.

V = cin(MSB) ⊕ cout(MSB)

Signed overflow. Assumes the operands are being read as two's complement; for unsigned arithmetic use C alone.

width = S + ceil(log2(C+1)) + ceil(log2 W)

Microinstruction width in horizontal format, with S control signals, C conditions plus an unconditional option, and W words of control memory. If the status bits address the multiplexer directly, use C rather than C+1.

size = W × width   bits

Control memory size. Divide by 8 for bytes.

time = steps × clock period

Instruction time on a non-pipelined machine. Under pipelining this is not the latency formula, and the count changes.

The comparison, in a table

Hardwired — signal delay: gate delay. Word width: not applicable. Flexibility: low. Best for: small fixed fast instruction sets.

Horizontal microprogrammed — signal delay: control-memory read. Word width: S bits for control, plus fields. Flexibility: high. Best for: large or changeable instruction sets.

Vertical microprogrammed — signal delay: control-memory read plus decoder. Word width: smallest. Flexibility: high. Best for: the smallest possible control store.

The traps, one line each

  • Carry is not overflow. 127+1 overflows with C=0; 255+1 carries with V=0.
  • Vertical fields need the +1 for "none"; the naive logarithm is short for every power of two.
  • Vertical is narrowest and slowest. Smaller is not faster.
  • Control memory size is words × width, not the signal count.
  • One bus is one transfer per step; two sources in a step is a conflict, not speed.
  • The ALU does not decode; the control unit does.
  • The step counter is not the program counter.
  • More buses shorten the step count and lengthen the clock; compare total time.
  • Read whether a size answer is wanted in words, bits or bytes.

Four numbers to carry into the exam

  • 3 — select lines for 8 functions; condition bits for 8 status lines; field bits for 4 mutually exclusive signals.
  • 10 — address bits for 1024 words, and the 2004 answer for X.
  • 26 — the 2004 microinstruction width, 13 + 3 + 10.
  • 5 — the micro-operations of R0 ← R1 + R2 on a single bus: fetch twice, then three execute steps.

If you remember only one sentence

The datapath can do a few things, the ALU computes, and the control unit is a function of instruction, step and flags — built as gates when speed matters and as a memory of microinstructions when flexibility matters, wide and undecoded or narrow and decoded, with the width of the select fields and the size of the store falling out of two ceilings and one multiplication.
