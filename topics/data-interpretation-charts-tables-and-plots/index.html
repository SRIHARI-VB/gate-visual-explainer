Topic and objective

TOPIC: Data interpretation — reading a data set presented as a table, a bar chart, a pie, a stacked bar or a plot, and then computing the one quantity the question asks for. This is Section 2 of the GATE General Aptitude syllabus, the part it calls "Data interpretation: data graphs (bar graphs, pie charts, and other graphs representing data), 2- and 3-dimensional plots, maps, and tables."

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a small data set and one sentence about it, decide which two numbers the sentence names, write the ratio between them in words before writing it in numbers, compute it, and then check the direction — because a share, a rate of change, a comparison and an average are four different answers built from the same cells. Along the way: tell percentage points from per cent, keep the base straight when a comparison is turned round, refuse to average rates whose bases differ, and say how far an estimate is from the truth.

I read this clause as one skill practised on four displays: locate, ratio, check. Every question the section can ask is a ratio of two cells or of two sums of cells, so the arithmetic is always short and the decision is always the same one. The data set below is used unchanged through all ten scenes, so the differences between the four answers are visible on the same twelve numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Percentages as ratios with a fixed second term. "15 of 150 is 10 per cent" and "15/150 = 10/100" are the same sentence. Built in Quantitative Aptitude.
  • Change as a fraction of the old value. (new − old) / old, and why dividing by the new value gives a different, smaller number. Built in Quantitative Aptitude, and used in Bayes' theorem as a revision rule.
  • Mean, median and mode, and what each one is for. A plain mean of rates is a mean of the wrong thing; the same distinction appears there. Built in Mean, Median, Mode and Standard Deviation.
  • Reading a figure before computing from it. Axes, legends and what a mark means. Built in Spatial Aptitude and Analytical Aptitude.
  • Ratio and proportion, and cancelling. 190/700 is 19/70, and cancelling first is what makes an estimate possible without a calculator.

No statistics beyond a plain average is assumed: no standard deviation, no regression, no probability. Where a question in the section needs those, it belongs to the statistics and probability part of Section 2, which the lessons linked above already cover.

Conventions used throughout

The data set is four departments and three years of intake, in students. It is fixed:

2023: CSE 240, ECE 180, ME 150, CE 90 → 660
2024: CSE 300, ECE 190, ME 140, CE 70 → 700
2025: CSE 360, ECE 200, ME 130, CE 60 → 750Twelve cells and three totals. Every number in every caption is computed from these by the model in lesson.js and re-checked there.

A share is a part over its own year's total. A rate is a change over the value it started from. A comparison puts one value against another, and the base is the one after "than". An average of several values is plain only when their bases are equal.

Educational interpretation I describe a chart as a lossy encoding of its table: a pie keeps the shares and throws away the totals, a truncated bar chart keeps the order and throws away the ratios, a stacked bar keeps the parts and hides the growth of the total. That is a way of saying what each display can and cannot answer; it is not a formal claim from a textbook.

How this differs from the lesson next door

Quantitative Aptitude surveys five computations — ratio, percentage, logarithm, mensuration, statistics — and applies each once, including one example on a chart. It teaches the arithmetic of a percentage change, which this lesson assumes and does not re-teach.

This lesson takes the one clause that survey only opens — data interpretation — and does the part the survey does not: which denominator a sentence names, what each display keeps and discards, percentage points against per cent, the asymmetry of "more than" and "less than", weighted against plain averages of rates, estimation with a stated band, and the axis and stack traps. If you have not read the survey, read it first; the two are designed to be read in that order.

Intuition first: the notice board and the four questions

A college puts a notice board outside the admissions office. It has four rows — CSE, ECE, ME, CE — and three columns, one for each year. Each cell holds one number: how many students joined that department that year.

Everything a question can ask about that board is a ratio between two numbers on it, or between two sums of them. "What fraction of last year's intake was CSE?" divides one cell by a column sum. "How much did ME grow?" divides a difference by one cell. "How much bigger is CSE than ECE?" divides a difference by the other cell. "Did the college grow overall?" divides the difference of two column sums by the first of them. Four sentences, four divisions, one board.

Educational interpretation So the section is not arithmetic practice. It is a reading exercise with a short calculation at the end, and the marks are lost in the reading — the wrong column, the wrong total, the wrong base — far more often than in the division.

Formal definitions

share = part ÷ (the total the part belongs to)The denominator is fixed by the phrase after "of" in the question, not by the table. "Of the 2025 intake" gives 750; "of all three years" gives 2,110.

rate of change = (new − old) ÷ oldThe base is the old value. Dividing by the new value instead gives the reverse change, a smaller number, and it is the standard planted distractor.

comparison = (this − that) ÷ that"A is p per cent more than B" puts B in the denominator. "B is q per cent less than A" puts A there. p and q are not equal and are not meant to be.

weighted mean of rates = Σ(rate × its base) ÷ Σ(bases)With the bases as weights this equals the growth computed from the two totals. Without weights it is the plain mean, which is a different quantity whenever the bases differ.

Mathematical derivation A pie angle is 360 × share, because a full turn is 360° and the share is a fraction of the whole. A share of s per cent is therefore 3.6s degrees. This is a change of units, not a new idea, which is why the pie carries exactly the information the percentages carry.

Percentage points against per cent

A percentage is a number with its denominator fixed at 100. Two of them can be subtracted, and the result is measured in percentage points, because it is the difference between two quantities that were themselves percentages. CSE's share went from 36.36 per cent to 48.00 per cent, which is a rise of 11.64 points.

A per cent change asks a different question: how large is the move compared with where it started? That divides the change by the old percentage: 11.64 / 36.36 = 0.32, which is 32.00 per cent.

Mathematical derivation The two answers differ by the factor (100 / old percentage) = 100 / 36.36 = 2.75. They can never be the same number unless the old value happened to be 100, which is why a question that mixes the words up has two plausible options and no way to choose between them except by reading the wording.

What each display keeps, and what it discards

Table. Keeps every cell and every total, and gives no visual hint of scale. Nothing is lost, so every question is answerable; nothing is emphasised, so you must locate the cells yourself.

Grouped bar. Keeps amounts as lengths from a common origin, so the ratio of two bar heights is the ratio of two amounts — provided the axis starts at zero.

Pie. Keeps shares exactly and discards the total. Two pies with the same shares and different totals are drawn identically.

Stacked bar. Keeps the parts of one total end to end, so a segment's height is its amount but a segment's share also depends on the total underneath it.

Line or 2-D plot. Keeps the order along one axis and the value along the other, so a slope is a rate — again only if both scales are read. Map. Keeps a position and a band of values; a shade is a range, never a point value, and the legend is part of the question.

The sentence this page earns

Every data-interpretation question is one ratio, and the only hard part is deciding which number goes underneath. That decision is made by the words "of" and "than" in the question, not by the shape of the table.

Level 4, worked once: the same twelve numbers, four answers

The board again: 2023 is 240, 180, 150, 90 (total 660); 2024 is 300, 190, 140, 70 (total 700); 2025 is 360, 200, 130, 60 (total 750). Four questions, each answered by one division, and each answer is checked against a property of the data.

share CSE in 2025 = 360 / 750 = 0.4800 = 48.00%
rate CSE 2023→2025 = 120 / 240 = 0.5000 = 50.00%
compare CSE vs ECE = 160 / 200 = 0.8000 = 80.00% more
average whole 2023→2025 = 90 / 660 = 0.1364 = 13.64%Four divisions of the same twelve numbers. Three of them use 360 or 240; only the last one uses the totals, which is why it is the one people skip.

Each answer can be checked without redoing the division. The shares of 2025 must sum to 100 per cent. The changes in students must sum to the change in the total: 120 + 20 − 20 − 30 = 90, and 660 + 90 = 750. The comparison must multiply back: 200 × 1.8 = 360. The rate must reconstruct its own value: 240 × 1.5 = 360. A wrong answer that survives all four checks is rare, and each check costs one addition or one multiplication.

The checks, stated as properties

Shares of one total sum to 1. This catches a wrong denominator immediately: 48.00 + 26.67 + 17.33 + 8.00 = 100.00 works only when all four were divided by the same 750.

Pie angles sum to 360°. The same check in different units: 172.8 + 96.0 + 62.4 + 28.8 = 360.0. If a question prints three angles, the fourth is the remainder.

Changes sum to the change of the total. Additive in the original units, never in percentages.

A weighted mean of rates equals the growth of the totals. Σ(rate × base) / Σbase = 9,000 / 660 = 13.64 per cent, which is the fourth division above.

Mathematical derivation The last property is an identity, not a coincidence: Σ(base_i × (new_i − old_i)/old_i) = Σ(new_i − old_i) = Σnew − Σold, and dividing by Σold gives exactly the growth of the totals. That is why weighting restores the whole, and why a plain mean cannot.

Estimation, with a band

Aptitude options are often spaced by one or two units, and the exact value falls between them, so the question is asking for the nearest. The safe procedure is to estimate the quotient from a simplified fraction and to state how far the estimate can be from the truth.

190 / 700 = 19 / 70. A quarter of 70 is 17.5, so 19 is a quarter plus 1.5/70, which is 0.2714. The options 26, 27, 28, 30: 27 per cent of 700 is 189, one student short of 190, so the estimate is 0.14 points low.

Mathematical derivation Rounding the inputs is a different operation from estimating the quotient, and it can change which option is nearest: 190/700 rounded to 200/700 is 28.57 per cent, which is closer to 28 than the truth is to 27. Estimate from a cancelled fraction, not from rounded inputs.

Where the section stops being safe

A rate needs a non-zero base. A department that had 0 students in 2023 has no growth rate from 2023; the question must then be answered from the amount, not the percentage.

A share needs a non-zero total. If a year's total is zero, no share exists for it.

Percentages of different totals cannot be added. 8 per cent of 750 and 8 per cent of 50 are 60 and 4; their "sum" of 16 per cent means nothing until both are converted to students.

Rounded shares may not sum to 100. Three shares printed to one decimal place can total 99.9 or 100.1. That is rounding, not an error, and the residual belongs to the cell that was rounded hardest.

The sentence this page earns

A data-interpretation answer is finished only when a property of the data has confirmed it: shares summing to 100, angles to 360, changes to the total change, or a rate multiplying back to its own value.

Six worked examples, each one decision then one division

Every example uses the same board. The point of each is the step before the arithmetic: which number goes underneath.

Example 1 — a share

What share of the 2025 intake was CSE?

  1. Read: the phrase after "of" is "the 2025 intake", so the total is 750.
  2. Locate: CSE in 2025 is 360.
  3. Divide: 360 / 750 = 0.48.
  4. Check: the four 2025 shares are 48.00, 26.67, 17.33, 8.00, and they sum to 100.

Answer: 48.00 per cent. The distractor is 360 / 2,110 = 17.06 per cent, which answers "of all three years together".

Example 2 — a pie angle, and a missing one

A pie shows the 2025 shares. What angle does CSE occupy, and what angle is left for CE if the other three are printed?

  1. Convert: angle = 3.6 × share. CSE: 3.6 × 48.00 = 172.8°.
  2. The other printed angles: ECE 96.0°, ME 62.4°.
  3. Remainder: 360 − 172.8 − 96.0 − 62.4 = 28.8°.
  4. Check: 28.8 / 3.6 = 8.00 per cent, which is 60 of 750. Both routes agree.

Answer: 172.8° and 28.8°. The remainder route is faster than converting the fourth share, and it is the same number.

Example 3 — a rate with a minus sign

By what per cent did ME change between 2023 and 2025?

  1. Base: ME in 2023, which is 150.
  2. Change: 130 − 150 = −20.
  3. Divide: −20 / 150 = −0.13333.
  4. Check: 150 × (1 − 0.13333) = 130, which is the 2025 value.

Answer: a fall of 13.33 per cent. The distractor is 13.33 per cent stated as a rise, or 20 / 130 = 15.38 per cent, which divides by the new value instead of the old.

Example 4 — points against per cent

CSE's share of the intake rose between 2023 and 2025. By how much?

  1. Share in 2023: 240 / 660 = 0.363636, so 36.36 per cent.
  2. Share in 2025: 360 / 750 = 0.48, so 48.00 per cent.
  3. Points: 48.00 − 36.36 = 11.64 points.
  4. Per cent of the old share: 11.64 / 36.36 = 0.3200, so 32.00 per cent.

Answer: 11.64 percentage points, or 32.00 per cent of the old share — the question's wording decides which. "By how many percentage points" asks the first; "by what per cent" asks the second.

Two more, where the sentence is the whole difficulty

Both of these are asked with the same two numbers, 360 and 200, and they have four different correct answers between them.

Example 5 — the comparison, turned round

In 2025, by what per cent is CSE's intake more than ECE's? And by what per cent is ECE's less than CSE's?

  1. "More than ECE" puts ECE underneath: (360 − 200) / 200 = 0.80.
  2. "Less than CSE" puts CSE underneath: (360 − 200) / 360 = 0.4444.
  3. Check by going back: 200 × 1.8 = 360, and 360 × (1 − 0.4444) = 200.
  4. The ratio 360 / 200 = 1.8 is the same number in both directions; the percentages are not.

Answer: 80.00 per cent more, and 44.44 per cent less. A third question on the same pair — "what per cent of ECE is CSE" — asks for the ratio as a percentage, 180 per cent, with no subtraction at all.

Example 6 — the average that is not the growth

What was the average growth rate of the four departments, and what was the growth of the whole intake?

  1. Rates: CSE +50.00, ECE +11.11, ME −13.33, CE −33.33 per cent.
  2. Plain mean: (50.00 + 11.11 − 13.33 − 33.33) / 4 = 14.44 / 4 = 3.61 per cent.
  3. Growth of the whole: (750 − 660) / 660 = 13.64 per cent.
  4. Weighted mean: 9,000 / 660 = 13.64 per cent, which matches the whole.

Answer: 3.61 per cent plainly, 13.64 per cent when the bases are respected. The two differ by a factor of 3.78, so the wording "the average growth" and "the growth of the intake" cannot be treated as the same question.

One counterexample, stated as data

A total can rise while three of its four parts fall. Here the total rises 13.64 per cent while ME falls 13.33 per cent and CE falls 33.33 per cent, because CSE alone gained 120 students against the other three losing 30 between them.

So "the intake grew" and "every department grew" are different statements, and neither implies the other. A question that says "the intake grew" tells you nothing about any individual row.

Where each rule stops applying

Each of these is a condition on a definition, not a curiosity. When the condition fails, the quantity does not exist, and the correct answer to the question is "not defined" or "cannot be determined".

A rate needs a non-zero base

Growth is (new − old) / old. If old is 0, the division is undefined: a department that enrolled nobody in 2023 has no percentage growth into 2024. Questions in that shape are answered in students, not in per cent.

Mathematical derivation The same failure appears in reverse when the new value is zero: the fall to zero is a 100 per cent fall, which is defined, but the recovery from zero is not. A rate is not symmetric about zero.

A share needs a non-zero total

A share is a part over its total. If a year's total is zero then every part is zero and the ratio 0/0 is undefined — not 0 per cent and not 100 per cent.

A related trap: a share can be 100 per cent (one department is the whole intake) and that is perfectly defined, but no share can exceed 100 per cent if the parts are disjoint. A computed share above 100 means the part and the total came from different places.

Percentages of different wholes cannot be combined

8 per cent of 750 is 60 and 8 per cent of 50 is 4. Adding them as "16 per cent" is meaningless; adding them as 64 students is correct and needs both totals.

This is the edge case behind the weighted-average rule: two rates can be added only after both are converted to the same unit, which is the unit of the underlying quantity.

Rounding, and a total that misses 100

Shares printed to one decimal place can sum to 99.9 or 100.1. The residual is rounding, not an error, and it is largest for the smallest cell. An option that says "the shares do not add to 100, so the data is wrong" is testing whether you know this.

The same applies to pie angles given to the nearest degree: 172 + 96 + 62 + 29 = 359, which is one degree short of a full turn and entirely consistent with the underlying data.

A truncated axis, and a stacked bar with a growing total

A bar chart whose axis starts at 50 rather than 0 draws 360 and 60 as 310 and 10, a visual ratio of 31 to 1 for amounts whose ratio is 6 to 1. The numbers on the bars are still correct; only the comparison by eye is broken.

In a stacked bar, a segment's height is its amount but its share also depends on the total underneath. ME's segment falls 13.33 per cent while its share falls 23.73 per cent, because the total grew from 660 to 750 in the same period.

A counterexample to "the average rate is the rate"

Four departments, four rates, one total. The plain mean of the rates is 3.61 per cent; the total grew 13.64 per cent. If a question asked "at what rate did the intake grow" and a candidate answered 3.61, the arithmetic would be right and the answer wrong.

The smallest case that shows it is two departments with bases 1 and 99: a 100 per cent rise in the first adds one student, and the whole has grown about 1 per cent, while the plain mean of the rates says 50 per cent.

The two pairs most often confused

Percentage points against per cent. Points are a subtraction of two percentages; per cent is a division by the old percentage. 11.64 points, 32.00 per cent, same move.

Share against amount. A share is scale-free and cannot answer "how many"; an amount needs the total. A pie shows the first and never the second, and a table shows both.

Eight beliefs that cost marks, and the smallest thing that corrects each

Each belief is stated as a student would hold it, then killed with the smallest case that shows it is false on this board.

1. "The denominator is the table's total."

It tempts because the grand total is the biggest number on the page and the phrase "the total" appears in the question. It gives 360 / 2,110 = 17.06 per cent where 48.00 per cent was wanted.

The correction: the denominator is the words after "of". "Of the 2025 intake" is 750.

2. "A rise of 11.64 is a rise of 11.64 per cent."

It tempts because both numbers are printed as percentages, so subtracting them feels like a percentage change. On this board the same movement is 11.64 points and 32.00 per cent, a factor of 2.75 apart.

The correction: points subtract, per cent divides by the old value.

3. "If A is 80 per cent more than B, then B is 80 per cent less than A."

It tempts because the two sentences look like the same sentence reversed. 80 per cent more and 44.44 per cent less describe the same pair, 360 and 200.

The correction: the base is the word after "than", and it changes when the sentence is turned round.

4. "The average of the growth rates is the growth rate."

It tempts because averaging is the obvious way to combine four numbers into one. The plain mean here is 3.61 per cent; the intake grew 13.64 per cent.

The correction: weight each rate by its base, or divide the two totals once.

5. "A pie shows the amounts."

It tempts because the slices have visibly different sizes, and size feels like quantity. Two pies with the same shares and totals 750 and 1,500 are drawn identically.

The correction: multiply the share by the total printed beside the pie.

6. "The taller bar is the bigger number."

It tempts because that is exactly what a bar chart is for. It is false only when the axis does not start at zero: 360 and 60 are drawn as 310 and 10, a ratio of 31 to 1.

The correction: read where the axis starts, and subtract that start from both heights before comparing.

7. "Two percentages can be added."

It tempts because percentages look like ordinary numbers. 8 per cent of 750 and 8 per cent of 50 are 60 and 4 students; their "sum" of 16 per cent corresponds to no quantity at all.

The correction: convert both to the underlying unit, add there, and re-divide if a percentage is wanted.

8. "Rounding the inputs early is safe."

It tempts because the options are round numbers. Rounding 190/700 to 200/700 gives 28.57 per cent, which pulls the answer to 28 when the truth, 27.14, is nearest 27.

The correction: cancel the fraction, estimate the quotient, then state the band.

The one sentence that covers all eight

Every one of these beliefs is a ratio written in numbers before it was written in words. Write "part over that year's total", "change over the old value", "this minus that over that", and the eight collapse to none.

How this section is actually asked

The General Aptitude section of the CS paper is 10 questions for 15 marks — five of 1 mark and five of 2 marks — and a data-interpretation item is usually one question, occasionally a set of two sharing one table. That is the shape to expect; it is not a forecast of how many appear.

Verified fact The marking rules matter here: a wrong 1-mark MCQ costs 1/3 and a wrong 2-mark MCQ costs 2/3, while MSQ and NAT carry no negative marking. A data-interpretation item with four close options is exactly the shape where a guess is expensive, which is why the checks in the Revision tab are worth the ten seconds they cost.

Shape 1 — the share in a table

A table of counts, one sentence naming a part and a year. The work is the denominator; the division is trivial.

The distractor is always a division by the wrong total. Check by summing the shares to 100.

Shape 2 — the rate with a sign

Two years, one row, "by what per cent did it change". The work is choosing the old value as the base and reporting the sign.

Distractors: the rate computed on the new value, and the same number with the sign dropped.

Shape 3 — the pie with a remainder

A pie with a total and three of four angles or shares printed. The work is the subtraction from 360 or from 100.

Distractors: converting the remainder wrongly (using 100 instead of 360, or the reverse), and forgetting to multiply by the total when an amount is asked for.

Shape 4 — the comparison in words

"A is what per cent more than B", or "B is what per cent of A", or "how many times A is B". Three different divisions of the same pair.

The habit: underline the word after "than", or the word "of", before writing anything.

Shape 5 — the approximate answer

A NAT or MCQ where the exact value falls between options and the word "approximately" appears. The work is a cancelled fraction and a stated band.

For a NAT, key the exact value unless the question states a rounding; for an MCQ, choose the nearest and keep the band in mind.

Recently seen variants, described but not reproduced

Two shapes recur in recent aptitude sections and are worth naming without quoting: a table whose entries are cumulative rather than per-year, so a year's value is a difference of two printed cells; and a figure that is really a network or a map, where the "data" is a path or a region rather than a cell, and the arithmetic is a count or a shortest distance rather than a ratio.

Both are handled by the same routine. A cumulative table adds one step — subtract adjacent cells before using them — and a network or map adds one question — what does a mark, an edge or a shade stand for?

The habit that covers all five shapes

Before any arithmetic: say the rows, say the columns, say the unit, then finish the sentence "as a share of ___" or "as a change from ___" with words from the question. Then divide once, then run one check. That is the whole method, and it is the same method in all five shapes.

Practice set — answers are withheld until you reveal them

All eight questions are original practice questions written for this lesson, using the board above. None is an official GATE question: no CS data-interpretation previous-year item was verified for this page, and the Sources tab says so. Attempt each one before revealing the answer.

Q1 MCQ Original

What share of the 2024 intake was ECE?

(A) 17.06%   (B) 27.14%   (C) 36.36%   (D) 48.00%

Answer: B. 190 / 700 = 0.271428…, so 27.14 per cent.

A is 360/2,110, the whole-table denominator applied to the wrong year. C is CSE's 2023 share, 240/660. D is CSE's 2025 share, 360/750 — a share of a different year.

Check: the four 2024 shares are 42.86, 27.14, 20.00 and 10.00, summing to 100.

Q2 NAT Original

By what per cent did the total intake change between 2023 and 2025? Give the answer to two decimal places, and enter the magnitude (a positive number).

Answer: 13.64. (750 − 660) / 660 = 90 / 660 = 0.136363…, so 13.64 per cent.

The two traps: dividing by 750 gives 12.00, and averaging the four departmental rates gives 3.61. Neither is the growth of the total. Check: 660 × 1.1364 = 750.

Q3 MCQ Original

CSE's share of the intake rose from 2023 to 2025. Which statement is correct?

(A) It rose by 11.64 per cent.   (B) It rose by 11.64 percentage points.   (C) It rose by 32.00 percentage points.   (D) It rose by 36.36 per cent.

Answer: B. 48.00 − 36.36 = 11.64, and a difference of two percentages is measured in percentage points.

A is the right number with the wrong unit — the relative change is 32.00 per cent, not 11.64. C mixes the two: 32.00 is a per cent, not points. D is the 2023 share itself, offered as a change.

The distinction to keep: points subtract, per cent divides by the old share.

Q4 MSQ Original

Which of the following are true of the board as given?

(A) Every department's intake fell from 2023 to 2025.
(B) The total intake rose from 2023 to 2025.
(C) ME's share of the intake fell by more, in per cent, than ME's intake fell in students.
(D) CSE's intake in 2025 is 80 per cent more than ECE's in 2025.

Answer: B and D.

A is false: CSE rose from 240 to 360 and ECE from 180 to 200, so two of four rose. C is a comparison in different units and is false as worded: ME fell 20 students and 13.33 per cent of its own intake, while its share fell 23.73 per cent — the statement as written compares a per cent with a count.

B is true: 660 to 750. D is true: (360 − 200) / 200 = 0.80. Note that B and A together are the counterexample — a total can rise while parts fall.

Q5 MCQ Original

A pie chart shows the 2025 intake. The angle for CSE is printed as 172.8°. What angle is printed for CE?

(A) 8.0°   (B) 28.8°   (C) 60.0°   (D) 288.0°

Answer: B. CE is 60 of 750, so 0.08, and 3.6 × 8.00 = 28.8°.

A is the share as a percentage, not an angle. C is the number of students, not an angle. D is the angle multiplied by ten — the share 0.08 read as 80 per cent.

Check: 172.8 + 96.0 + 62.4 + 28.8 = 360.0.

Q6 MCQ Original

A bar chart of the 2025 intake draws CSE as 310 pixels and CE as 10 pixels. What does this tell you?

(A) CSE has 31 times the intake of CE.   (B) The axis does not start at zero, and the true ratio is 6 to 1.   (C) The chart is wrong.   (D) CE had 10 students.

Answer: B. 310 and 10 are 360 − 50 and 60 − 50, so the axis starts at 50. The amounts are 360 and 60, a ratio of 6 to 1.

A is the visual ratio, which is not the data ratio once the axis is truncated. C overstates: the bar heights are consistent with an axis starting at 50, which is a presentation choice, not an arithmetic error. D confuses a pixel height with a count.

The habit: find where the axis starts, subtract it from both heights, then compare.

Q7 NAT Original

The four departmental growth rates are averaged without weights. What is that average, to two decimal places, in per cent?

Answer: 3.61. (50.00 + 11.11 − 13.33 − 33.33) / 4 = 14.44 / 4 = 3.61 per cent.

This is the wrong answer to the question "at what rate did the intake grow" — that question wants 13.64. It is the right answer to the question actually asked here, which is what the unweighted average is. Reading the wording is the whole skill.

Q8 MSQ Original

A candidate computes ECE's 2024 share as 190 / 2,110 and keys 9.00 per cent. Which statements are correct?

(A) The computation is arithmetic-correct.   (B) The denominator is wrong for the question.   (C) The correct share is 27.14 per cent.   (D) 9.00 per cent is the correct answer to some other question.

Answer: A, B, C and D — all four.

190 / 2,110 = 0.09005…, so A holds. The question asks for a share of the 2024 intake, whose total is 700, so B holds and C is the answer (190/700 = 27.14 per cent). D holds too: 9.00 per cent is ECE's share of all three years together, which is a real question with a real answer.

This is the lesson's central point in one item: the same division can be right arithmetic and wrong answer, and the phrase after "of" is what decides.

Revision sheet — the whole section on one screen

Definitions, four formulas, a decision procedure, the traps in one line each, and three sentences worth keeping.

Definitions

Share — a part divided by the total it belongs to. Scale-free: it says nothing about size.

Rate of change — the change divided by the value it started from. Carries a sign.

Percentage point — the unit of a difference between two percentages.

Comparison — one value against another, with the one after "than" in the denominator.

Weighted mean of rates — each rate multiplied by its own base, divided by the sum of the bases. Equal to the growth of the totals.

Formulas

share = part / total
rate = (new − old) / old
points = p₂ − p₁
per cent change of a percentage = (p₂ − p₁) / p₁
angle = 3.6 × share (per cent)
weighted mean = Σ(rate × base) / ΣbaseSix lines, one per question shape. Every one of them is a division by something the question named.

Mathematical derivation The last line is an identity: Σ(base × change/base) = Σchange = Σnew − Σold, so dividing by Σold returns the growth of the totals exactly.

Decision procedure

  1. Say the rows, the columns and the unit of a cell.
  2. Underline the phrase after "of" (denominator) or after "than" (base).
  3. Name the shape: share, rate, comparison or average.
  4. Build any total the question needs and the table does not print.
  5. Divide once.
  6. Run one check: shares to 100, angles to 360, changes to the total change, or multiply back.

Steps 2 and 3 are where the marks are. Steps 5 and 6 take fifteen seconds together.

Traps, in one line each

  • Grand total used where the year total was asked: 17.06 instead of 48.00.
  • Points reported as per cent: 11.64 instead of 32.00.
  • "More than" and "less than" assumed symmetric: 80.00 against 44.44.
  • Rates averaged plainly: 3.61 instead of 13.64.
  • A pie read as amounts: the total beside it is part of the question.
  • Bars compared on a truncated axis: 31 to 1 drawn for 6 to 1.
  • Percentages of different wholes added: convert to the underlying unit first.
  • Inputs rounded before the quotient: 28.57 instead of 27.14.

Each of these is one of the eight misconceptions, in the form it appears in an option list.

Three sentences worth memorising

A share is a part over its own total, and the denominator is the phrase after "of".

A change is measured against where it started, so the base is the old value, or the word after "than".

Points subtract, per cent divides, and rates are averaged only with the bases as weights.
