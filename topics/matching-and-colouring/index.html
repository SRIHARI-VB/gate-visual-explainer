Topic and objective

TOPIC: Graphs: matching and colouring — a matching as a set of disjoint edges, maximal against maximum, augmenting paths and Berge's theorem, Hall's condition and Hall's theorem, König's theorem and vertex covers, vertex colouring and the chromatic number, greedy colouring with the Δ+1 bound, Brooks' theorem, edge colouring with König's bipartite theorem and Vizing's window, and the planarity bounds that follow from Euler's formula.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given an unfamiliar question about matchings or colourings, name which of the two objects is being asked about, check whether the graph is bipartite before quoting a theorem that needs it, bound the answer from below with a clique or a degree argument and from above with greedy or Brooks, and produce a certificate — a clique, a colouring, a cover or an augmenting path — rather than a guess.

I read this as the second half of the syllabus phrase "Graphs: connectivity, matching, colouring", which the previous lesson cut after connectivity. The two halves are separate lessons because they rest on different machinery: connectivity is about cuts and traversals, matching and colouring are about disjointness and partitions. Bipartiteness, taught there as the two-colour case, is the bridge and is used constantly here.

Prerequisites

  • Graphs: connectivity and structure: a graph as two sets, degree, walk and path, component, tree, bipartite graphs and the odd-cycle test. This lesson assumes all of it, and uses bipartite as the hypothesis of three separate theorems.
  • Counting and combinatorics: the product rule for counting matchings of a path, and the pigeonhole principle for the independent-set bound. Nothing beyond those two rules is used.
  • Sets and set operations: a subset, a partition, and the neighbourhood N(S) as a set built from a set. A colouring is literally a partition of the vertex set.
  • No algorithms background is assumed. The augmenting-path search is described as a walk with an alternating rule, not as an implementation.

The five ideas

  1. A matching is a set of edges; a colouring is a partition of vertices. One colour class is an independent set, and one colour of an edge colouring is a matching — the two questions are built from the same idea of disjointness.
  2. "Maximal" and "maximum" are different words. A maximal matching cannot be extended; a maximum matching cannot be beaten. Berge's theorem turns "maximum" into a search, which is why it is the one definition here with an algorithm attached.
  3. Hall's condition is exact for saturating one side of a bipartite graph, and König's theorem makes the largest matching and the smallest cover the same number. Both theorems need bipartite, and both fail by one on an odd cycle.
  4. The chromatic number is squeezed from both sides: the clique number below, greedy's Δ+1 above, and Δ above once Brooks applies. When the bounds meet, the answer is settled without any search.
  5. The edge chromatic number is squeezed even tighter: Vizing leaves only Δ and Δ+1, and on a bipartite graph it is exactly Δ. So an edge-colouring question has two candidate answers, and a construction decides between them.

Conventions used throughout

Every graph here is simple (no loop, at most one edge between a pair) and undirected. n is the number of vertices, m the number of edges. Delta (Δ) is the largest degree and delta (δ) the smallest. omega (ω) is the clique number, the size of the largest complete subgraph. nu (ν) is the matching number, the size of a largest matching. alpha (α) is the independence number, the size of the largest set of pairwise non-adjacent vertices. chi (χ) is the vertex chromatic number and chi-prime (χ′) the edge chromatic number, also called the chromatic index. N(S) is the set of neighbours of a subset S. Where a definition needs a convention at its edge — χ of the empty graph, whether a single vertex needs one colour — the convention is stated at the point of use.

Intuition first: a dance, not a mathematics

Twenty people are at a dance. Ask two questions about them. First: how many couples can dance at once, if each person dances with at most one partner? That is a matching — a set of pairs with nobody in two pairs. Second: how many separate rooms do you need, if nobody may share a room with someone they have come with? That is a colouring — a split of the people into groups with no friendship inside a group.

The first question counts edges. The second counts groups. They are asked about the same friendships, and they have different answers, and neither answer can be read off the other. Everything in this lesson is one of those two questions asked precisely.

The dance analogy is an educational interpretation: it is there to fix which object is being counted, not to prove anything. The proofs in this lesson are the ones in the Build-up and Examples tabs, and they use only the definitions.

Level 2 — the formal definitions

Matching. A set M of edges such that no two edges of M share a vertex. The size of M is the number of edges in it.

Saturated and free. A vertex v is saturated by M if some edge of M is incident with v, and free otherwise.

Maximal. M is maximal if no edge can be added to it and still leave a matching.

Maximum. M is maximum if no matching of G is larger. Its size is the matching number ν(G).

Perfect. M is perfect if it saturates every vertex. Then |M| = n/2, so n is even.

Vertex cover. A set C of vertices such that every edge has at least one endpoint in C.

Level 2 — colouring definitions

Proper colouring. A function c from V to a set of labels such that c(u) ≠ c(v) for every edge uv.

Colour class. The set of vertices carrying one label. A proper colouring is exactly a partition of V into independent sets.

Chromatic number χ(G). The fewest labels in a proper colouring.

Clique number ω(G). The size of the largest set of vertices that are pairwise adjacent. A clique needs one colour per vertex, so ω ≤ χ.

Independence number α(G). The size of the largest independent set. Each of the χ colour classes is independent, so α ≥ n/χ by the pigeonhole principle.

Chromatic index χ′(G). The fewest labels in a proper colouring of the edges, where two edges conflict when they share a vertex. Each colour class is a matching.

Level 3 — why the two questions have different answers

A matching is a set of edges, so its size is at most n/2 and at most m. A colouring is a partition of vertices, so its value is at least 1 and at most n. The bounds are on different quantities, which is why the cube graph answers 2 to one question and 3 to the other.

The one sentence that connects them: a set of edges sharing no vertex is the same thing as a set of edges of one colour. Every edge-colouring theorem is therefore a statement about decomposing the edge set into matchings, and König's bipartite theorem says a bipartite graph's edges split into exactly Δ matchings.

The four theorems, in the order to use them

Berge. A matching is maximum exactly when no augmenting path exists. Use it to justify a search, and to prove that a matching you exhibit is maximum.

Hall. A matching saturating the left side exists exactly when every subset S of the left side has |N(S)| ≥ |S|. Use it when the question asks whether every item can be assigned.

König. In a bipartite graph the maximum matching size equals the minimum vertex cover size. Use it to convert a matching question into a cover question and back.

Brooks and Vizing. Brooks bounds vertex colouring by Δ outside two families; Vizing bounds edge colouring to Δ or Δ+1. Use them to cut the candidate set down before you compute.

What the definitions do not say

A matching does not have to be as large as possible. A colouring does not have to use the fewest colours. Both words describe a property of one object; "maximum" and "chromatic number" describe the best over all such objects. Confusing the property with the optimum is the single commonest error in this unit, and the Misconceptions tab collects the rest.

Nor does a matching have to be perfect, or a colouring have to use every colour it is allowed. A four-colouring of a bipartite graph is a proper colouring that is not a chromatic one, and it still proves χ ≤ 4.

Example 1 — running Hall's test by hand

Left side L = {0,1,2}, right side R = {3,4,5}. Edges: 0–3, 0–4, 1–3, 1–4, 2–3, 2–4, 2–5. Does a matching saturate L?

  1. Subsets of size 1: N({0}) = {3,4}, size 2 ≥ 1. Same for 1 and 2 every single vertex has a neighbour
  2. Subsets of size 2: N({0,1}) = {3,4}, size 2 ≥ 2. N({0,2}) = N({1,2}) = {3,4,5}, size 3 exactly enough for {0,1}, and spare for the others
  3. Subset of size 3: N({0,1,2}) = {3,4,5}, size 3 ≥ 3 the whole side passes
  4. Hall holds on every subset, so a saturating matching exists: 0–4, 1–3, 2–5 and it is perfect, since both sides have three vertices

Delete the edge 2–5 and the answer flips: N({0,1,2}) = {3,4} has size 2 < 3, so no saturating matching exists. One edge decides it.

Example 2 — one augmenting path, start to finish

L = {0,1,2}, R = {3,4,5}. Edges: 0–3, 1–3, 1–4, 2–4, 0–5. Start from the matching {0–3, 1–4}, of size 2. Free vertices: 2 and 5.

  1. From free vertex 2, take a non-matching edge to 4 alternating: outside the matching
  2. 4 is matched to 1, so take the matching edge 4–1 inside the matching
  3. From 1 take the non-matching edge 1–3 outside again
  4. 3 is matched to 0, so take the matching edge 3–0 inside again
  5. From 0 take the non-matching edge 0–5, and 5 is free the path closes at a free vertex: it is augmenting
  6. Swap: drop {0–3, 1–4}, take {2–4, 1–3, 0–5} size 2 becomes size 3, which is perfect

The path has five edges, three outside the matching and two inside, so the swap gains exactly one. That arithmetic is the whole of the proof that an augmenting path raises the size.

Example 3 — König on a bipartite graph, and off one

Edges 0–3, 0–4, 1–3, 1–4, 2–5, 0–5 with L = {0,1,2}, R = {3,4,5}.

  1. Maximum matching: 0–4, 1–3, 2–5, size 3 found by the augmenting-path algorithm, confirmed by exhaustive search
  2. Minimum cover: {0,1,2}, size 3 every edge touches the left side, and no two vertices cover all six edges
  3. König: the two numbers agree at 3 as the theorem promises for every bipartite graph
  4. Now the triangle: matching {0–1} of size 1, minimum cover {0,1} of size 2 the equality fails, because the triangle is not bipartite

The cover from the alternating construction is the one to remember: unreached left vertices together with reached right vertices, starting from the unmatched left vertices. It always has the matching's size.

Example 4 — squeezing a chromatic number

The Petersen graph: 10 vertices, 15 edges, every degree 3.

  1. Lower bound: it has a five-cycle and no triangle, so ω = 2 and χ ≥ 2 the clique number is a lower bound and here it is weak
  2. Upper bound: greedy gives χ ≤ Δ+1 = 4 three colours are forbidden at most
  3. Brooks: connected, not complete, not an odd cycle, so χ ≤ Δ = 3 this is the step that removes 4
  4. Is 2 possible? That would mean bipartite, and the five-cycle is odd, so no bipartite is the same as no odd cycle
  5. Therefore χ = 3 and a 3-colouring exists, so the bound is attained

Notice the order: clique below, greedy above, Brooks to tighten, odd cycle to rule out the bottom. Four steps, no search.

Example 5 — the same graph, two greedy orders

Six vertices, edges 0–1, 0–2, 1–3, 2–3, 2–4, 3–5. This graph is bipartite, so χ = 2.

  1. Order 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0: 5 gets 1; 4 gets 1; 3 sees 5 and takes 2; 2 sees 3 (colour 2) and 4 (colour 1) and must take 3 three colours, on a graph that needs two
  2. Order 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: 0 gets 1; 1 gets 2; 2 gets 2; 3 gets 1; 4 gets 1; 5 gets 2 two colours, the optimum
  3. Same graph, same algorithm, same number of steps only the order changed

So a greedy count is an upper bound on χ and never a value. In an exam, say "greedy gives at most k", and then look for a lower bound to close the gap.

Example 6 — edge colouring K3,4 plus a universal vertex

This is the shape of a previous-year question. Take K3,4 — three vertices joined to four, twelve edges — and add one vertex s joined to all seven. How many colours edge-colour it?

  1. s has degree 7, so its seven edges need seven different colours: χ′ ≥ 7 edges at one vertex are pairwise adjacent
  2. Vizing: χ′ is 7 or 8, so the answer is one of two numbers the window is what makes this question finite
  3. The graph is not bipartite — s with any edge of K3,4 makes a triangle — so König does not settle it this is the trap in the question
  4. Construction: split the twelve edges of K3,4 into four matchings of three edges each and colour them 1 to 4. Each of the four right-side vertices is missed by exactly one colour, and the four misses are distinct.
  5. Give s colour 5, 6, 7 on its three left-side edges, and the missing colour on each of its four right-side edges seven distinct colours at s, and every vertex still sees distinct colours

Seven colours, so the answer is 7. The exhaustive search in this page's model agrees, and so does the construction — which is why "at most 8 from Vizing" is not an answer.

Example 7 — Euler's formula and the two forbidden graphs

A connected planar drawing satisfies n − m + f = 2, where f counts the outer face.

  1. Every face has at least 3 edge-sides, and each edge borders 2 faces, so 2m ≥ 3f counting edge-sides two ways
  2. Substituting f = 2 − n + m gives m ≤ 3n − 6 the general planarity bound
  3. K5: n = 5, m = 10, and 3n − 6 = 9 10 > 9, so K5 is not planar
  4. Bipartite graphs have no triangle, so every face has at least 4 sides and m ≤ 2n − 4. K3,3: n = 6, m = 9, and 2n − 4 = 8 9 > 8, so K3,3 is not planar
  5. K4: n = 4, m = 6, and 3n − 6 = 6, with f = 4 faces it satisfies the bound and is planar, which the bound alone could not have told you

The bound is necessary and not sufficient. K3,3 satisfies m ≤ 3n − 6 with room to spare and is still non-planar, which is exactly why the bipartite form and Kuratowski's theorem exist.

Example 8 — counting matchings, not finding one

A previous-year question asked for the number of matchings of a path on 8 vertices. This is a counting question, not a maximum question, and the two use different tools.

  1. Write M(n) for the number of matchings of a path on n vertices, counting the empty matching a matching may be empty; the definition does not forbid it
  2. M(1) = 1 and M(2) = 2 nothing, or the single edge
  3. The last vertex is either unmatched, giving M(n−1), or matched to its neighbour, giving M(n−2) conditioning on the last vertex, the recurrence-building move
  4. So M(n) = M(n−1) + M(n−2): 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34 Fibonacci, with the empty matching counted
  5. M(8) = 34 confirmed here by enumerating every subset of the 7 edges and keeping the disjoint ones

The link to recurrence relations is not decoration: counting matchings of a path is the standard worked example of a first-order-plus-second-order recurrence.

Where the definitions run out

Every theorem on this page has a hypothesis, and each of the cases below is a graph that fails one. The habit worth building: read the hypothesis out loud before you use the conclusion, because in this unit the hypotheses are short — bipartite, connected, simple — and easy to skip.

The empty graph and the single vertex

The empty graph has no vertices, so it has no edges and exactly one matching, the empty one. Its chromatic number is 0 by the convention used here, because there is nothing to colour.

A single vertex has one matching — the empty one — and needs exactly one colour. A single edge has two matchings and needs two colours. These are not trick questions; they are the base cases of the recurrences, and a question that counts matchings will include them.

Maximal, but not maximum

Two triangles joined by one edge between them. The matching consisting of that joining edge is maximal: every other edge touches one of its two endpoints. It has size 1.

A maximum matching has size 2, one edge from each triangle. So "no edge can be added" is true of a matching that is not the largest. Any argument that stops at "cannot extend" has proved maximality and nothing more.

A perfect matching needs an even number of vertices

A perfect matching saturates every vertex, and each of its edges saturates two, so |M| = n/2. If n is odd, no perfect matching exists, whatever the edges are.

The odd case has a name: a near-perfect matching leaves exactly one vertex free. On an odd cycle the maximum matching is near-perfect, of size (n−1)/2, and it is also the minimum vertex cover minus one — which is precisely how König fails there.

Hall is about every subset, not the whole side

Take L = {0,1,2} and R = {3,4,5}, with edges 1–3, 1–4, 2–3, 2–4. Vertex 0 has no edges at all.

The whole side has three neighbours? No — N({0,1,2}) = {3,4}, size 2 < 3, so even the whole-side check fails here. To see why the whole-side check is not enough in general, take four left vertices and four right vertices where one left vertex has four neighbours and the other three share only three: the whole side has four neighbours, but the three share only three. Hall fails on that subset, and no saturating matching exists.

König needs bipartite, and the odd cycle is the witness

On a triangle, the maximum matching has size 1 and the minimum vertex cover has size 2. On a five-cycle the same gap appears: matching 2, cover 3.

The gap is exactly one on every odd cycle, and it can be larger elsewhere. So a question that asks for a minimum vertex cover is either bipartite — in which case König gives it from a maximum matching — or it is not, and then the problem is NP-hard and the question will be small enough to reason about by hand.

The clique number is not the chromatic number

The Mycielskian of a five-cycle has 11 vertices, 20 edges, no triangle at all, and chromatic number 4. Its clique number is 2.

So ω = 2 and χ = 4 on the same graph. On bipartite graphs, on interval graphs and on every perfect graph the two agree, which is why the confusion is so common. The Mycielskian construction can widen the gap without limit: apply it repeatedly and χ rises by one each time while ω stays at 2.

Brooks' two exceptions are load-bearing

A complete graph Kn has Δ = n−1 and χ = n, so χ = Δ+1. An odd cycle has Δ = 2 and χ = 3, again one more.

Both are connected, so the theorem's connectivity hypothesis holds and only its exception clause saves the bound from being false. Brooks also needs the graph to be connected: two disjoint triangles have Δ = 2 and χ = 3, and each component is an odd cycle, so the exception covers that case too.

Vizing needs a simple graph

Take a triangle and double every edge. The maximum degree is 4, and the edge chromatic number is 6: the three doubled pairs must each get two different colours, and the six edges are pairwise adjacent at their endpoints.

So χ′ = 6 while Δ+1 = 5, and Vizing's window fails. The theorem is stated for simple graphs, and a question that allows parallel edges is asking something else — the answer is then at most ⌊3Δ/2⌋ by Shannon's theorem, which is outside this syllabus but worth knowing exists.

The planarity bound is necessary, not sufficient

K3,3 has n = 6, m = 9, and 3n − 6 = 12. It satisfies the general bound comfortably and is still non-planar.

The bound rules graphs out and never rules them in. The bipartite form 2n − 4 catches K3,3, and Kuratowski's theorem — planar exactly when no subdivision of K5 or K3,3 appears — is the actual characterisation.

Euler's formula needs connected, or a correction

For a planar graph with c components, n − m + f = 1 + c, not 2. Two disjoint triangles: n = 6, m = 6, f = 2, and 6 − 6 + 2 = 2 = 1 + 1.

Every GATE question on faces states that the graph is connected, and the correction term is what makes that sentence necessary rather than decorative.

Four colours are needed, and three are not enough

Every planar graph is 4-colourable — the four-colour theorem — and some planar graphs need all four. The wheel over an odd cycle is the smallest familiar example: an odd rim needs three colours, and the hub touches every rim vertex, so it needs a fourth.

So "planar" gives χ ≤ 4, never χ ≤ 3. A previous-year question asked for the number of colours sufficient for every planar graph, and the answer is 4.

Greedy's order is part of the algorithm

On a bipartite graph, one order can make first-fit use three colours while another uses two. The bound Δ+1 holds for every order, and the value does not.

So a question that says "a greedy algorithm colours the vertices in the order v1, …, vn" is giving you the order as data. Change the order and the answer changes, and that is not a paradox — it is the algorithm being defined completely.

χ and χ′ answer different questions

The cube graph Q3 is bipartite: χ = 2. Its maximum degree is 3 and it is 3-regular, so χ′ = 3 by König's bipartite theorem.

Two against three on the same graph. Neither number determines the other, and a question that switches between them mid-sentence is the commonest source of a lost mark in this unit.

Eight beliefs that cost marks

Each entry states the belief as it is usually held, then the smallest counterexample that kills it, then the corrected sentence to carry into the exam. The counterexamples are the ones computed in this page's model, so each is a fact about a named graph rather than a remembered anecdote.

1. "Maximal means maximum"

Wrong because: maximal is a property of one set, maximum is a comparison across all sets. Two triangles joined by one edge have a maximal matching of size 1 and a maximum matching of size 2.

Say instead: "This matching cannot be extended, so it is maximal. To show it is maximum, I must show that no augmenting path exists."

2. "Hall's condition only needs checking on the whole left side"

Wrong because: the condition is quantified over every subset. A graph can have |N(L)| ≥ |L| and still fail on a small S, and then no saturating matching exists.

Say instead: "I need |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every S ⊆ L. The fastest refutation is to find one S that is short, and the fastest confirmation on a small graph is to check the subsets by size."

3. "König's theorem holds for every graph"

Wrong because: the triangle has matching 1 and cover 2. So does every odd cycle, and so does the Petersen graph, where the matching number is 5 and the minimum cover is 6.

Say instead: "The graph is bipartite — I checked by two-colouring — so by König the maximum matching equals the minimum vertex cover."

4. "The chromatic number is the clique number"

Wrong because: ω ≤ χ always, and the gap can be large. The Mycielskian of C5 has ω = 2 and χ = 4.

Say instead: "The clique gives a lower bound of ω. Greedy or Brooks gives an upper bound. If they meet, χ is settled; if not, I need a construction for the upper bound and an argument for the lower."

5. "Greedy used 3 colours, so the graph needs 3"

Wrong because: greedy depends on the order. On the six-vertex bipartite graph in this lesson, one order uses 3 and another uses 2.

Say instead: "Greedy in this order used 3, so χ ≤ 3. To show χ = 3 I still need a lower bound, such as an odd cycle or a triangle."

6. "χ′ equals χ, or at least χ′ ≤ χ"

Wrong because: there is no such relation in either direction. The cube graph has χ = 2 and χ′ = 3; the star has χ = 2 and χ′ = n−1.

Say instead: "χ′ is bounded by Δ from below and by Δ+1 above, and equals Δ on bipartite graphs. That is a complete toolkit for simple graphs."

7. "Planar graphs are 3-colourable"

Wrong because: the four-colour theorem gives 4 and the bound is attained. The wheel over a five-cycle is planar and needs 4.

Say instead: "Planar gives χ ≤ 4. The lower bound depends on the graph: a triangle gives 3, and an odd wheel gives 4."

8. "Vizing's theorem gives the answer"

Wrong because: Vizing gives a window of two values. On K3,4 plus a universal vertex the window is 7 or 8, and the answer is 7 — which needs the construction, not the theorem.

Say instead: "Δ is 7, so χ′ is 7 or 8. A vertex of degree Δ already forces 7 distinct colours on its edges, and here I can build a 7-colouring, so the answer is 7."

What the paper actually asks

Six patterns cover everything this unit has produced in recent papers: a chromatic number to compute from a drawing, a set of statements to judge, an edge colouring to compute, a planarity count, a matching count, and a face or edge count through Euler's formula. Each is a one- or two-mark question, and each is settled by a bound plus a construction rather than by search.

Community observation The previous-year questions below are named with their year, set and question number, and their statements are the ones public question archives record. Their answers here are re-derived and, where the graph is small enough, confirmed by the exhaustive search in this page's model — which is why the answer is given with a reason and not as a remembered key.

Pattern 1 — compute χ from a drawing

Shape: a graph is drawn and the question asks for its chromatic number as a number (NAT) or from options.

Method: find a clique for the lower bound; colour greedily for the upper bound; if they differ, look for an odd cycle or use Brooks. Never search blindly — the graph is always small enough for the bounds to meet.

Appeared as: GATE CSE 2018 (chromatic number of a drawn graph), GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q60 (the same, as a two-mark NAT).

Pattern 2 — judge statements about χ

Shape: an MSQ offering four claims about a graph with chromatic number k, each to be marked always true or not.

Method: test each claim on the smallest awkward graph you know — an odd cycle for "contains a clique of size k", a complete graph for "a vertex of degree at least k".

Appeared as: GATE CSE 2024 Set 1 Q41, reproduced below with its answer.

Previous-year question 1 — GATE CSE 2024, Set 1, Q41 (MSQ, 2 marks)

"The chromatic number of a graph is the minimum number of colours used in a proper colouring of the graph. Let G be any graph with n vertices and chromatic number k. Which of the following statements are always TRUE?"

(A) G contains a complete subgraph with k vertices   (B) G contains an independent set of size at least n/k   (C) G contains at least k(k−1)/2 edges   (D) G contains a vertex of degree at least k

Answer: (B) and (C).

(B) is the pigeonhole principle: the k colour classes partition n vertices, so one class has at least n/k, and every class is independent. (C) holds because two colour classes with no edge between them could be merged into one colour, so each of the k(k−1)/2 pairs of classes needs at least one edge. Derivation Both are verified here on all 1,099 graphs with at most five vertices, by computing the chromatic number and then checking the largest independent set and the edge count.

(A) fails on the five-cycle: χ = 3 and there is no triangle. (D) fails on the five-cycle too, where χ = 3 and every degree is 2 — and on every complete graph, where χ = n and every degree is n−1.

Pattern 3 — edge colouring, answered by Δ and one construction

Shape: a NAT asking for the minimum number of colours to edge-colour a described graph.

Method: compute Δ. If the graph is bipartite, the answer is Δ. If not, the answer is Δ or Δ+1, and a vertex of degree Δ together with a construction decides.

Appeared as: GATE CSE 2020 Q52, reproduced below.

Previous-year question 2 — GATE CSE 2020, Q52 (NAT, 2 marks)

"Graph G is obtained by adding vertex s to K3,4 and making s adjacent to every vertex of K3,4. The minimum number of colours required to edge-colour G is ______."

Answer: 7. Vertex s has degree 7, so its seven edges need seven distinct colours: χ′ ≥ 7. Vizing gives χ′ ≤ 8, so the only question is whether 8 can be avoided. Derivation The graph is not bipartite — s with any edge of K3,4 forms a triangle — so König does not apply. The 7-colouring is built in Example 6: split K3,4 into four matchings and colour them 1–4, then give s colours 5, 6, 7 on its three left-side edges and the missing colour on each of its four right-side edges. Every vertex then sees distinct colours on its edges, with seven colours in total. This page's model confirms χ′ = 7 by exhaustive colouring, and separately verifies that the constructed colouring is proper.

The trap: answering 8 from Vizing, or answering 4 because "K3,4 is bipartite". The first forgets that the window has two ends and one of them must be attained; the second forgets that adding s destroys bipartiteness.

Previous-year question 3 — GATE CSE 2016, Set 2, Q3 (NAT, 1 mark)

"The minimum number of colours that is sufficient to vertex-colour any planar graph is ______."

Answer: 4. This is the four-colour theorem, and the word any is what makes it a theorem rather than a computation: no planar graph needs five. The bound is attained, so 4 cannot be lowered — an odd wheel is planar and needs 4.

The trap: answering 3 by analogy with "planar graphs have no K5". Planarity forbids K5 and K3,3, not odd cycles, and an odd cycle alone already forces 3 while an odd wheel forces 4.

Pattern 4 — planarity counts through Euler

Shape: vertices and faces given, edges asked for — or the reverse.

Method: n − m + f = 2 for a connected planar graph. Rearrange before substituting, and check that the graph is connected as stated.

Appeared as: GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 Q16 (8 vertices, 5 faces, edges asked) and GATE CSE 2015 Set 1 Q54 (10 vertices, every face a triangle, edges asked).

Previous-year question 4 — GATE CSE 2021, Set 1, Q16 (NAT, 1 mark)

"In an undirected connected planar graph G, there are eight vertices and five faces. The number of edges in G is ______."

Answer: 11. n − m + f = 2 with n = 8 and f = 5 gives m = 8 + 5 − 2 = 11. Derivation Rearranged and substituted; the model's check recomputes the identity from the rearranged form so the arithmetic is not taken on trust.

Note the same answer follows from the face bound: 2m ≥ 3f would give m ≥ 7.5, which is consistent but not decisive — Euler's formula is the identity that gives the exact value.

Pattern 5 — count the matchings

Shape: "the number of matchings in G is ___", usually for a path or a small tree.

Method: condition on the last vertex: unmatched gives the count for n−1, matched gives the count for n−2. The recurrence is M(n) = M(n−1) + M(n−2) with M(1) = 1, M(2) = 2, and the empty matching is included.

Appeared as: GATE CSE 2026 Set 1 Q47, reproduced below.

Previous-year question 5 — GATE CSE 2026, Set 1, Q47 (NAT)

"Let G be an undirected graph, which is a path on 8 vertices. The number of matchings in G is ______."

Answer: 34. The counts for paths of length 1 to 8 are 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34. Derivation Computed here by enumerating all 27 subsets of the seven edges and keeping those with no shared endpoint; the enumeration returns 34, and the recurrence returns the same number.

The trap: excluding the empty matching, which would give 33. A matching is a set of edges, and the empty set is one. If the official key differs by exactly one, that is the reason.

Pattern 6 — a statement question on bipartite matching

Shape: an MSQ about a bipartite graph, offering claims about Hall's condition, König's equality, or the effect of deleting an edge.

Method: translate each claim into a subset condition or a cover size, then test it on the smallest bipartite graph that could break it — a 2 + 2 graph with three edges, or a 3 + 3 graph with one short subset.

Warning: a claim that is true for every bipartite graph is a theorem; a claim that is true for the drawn one is not. The question will say "always".

Ten questions

All ten are original GATE-style questions written for this lesson, and each is labelled as such. They carry no official marks and none of them is a previous-year question. Learning mode reveals each answer on request; mock-test mode withholds every answer until you submit. Every answer is justified, every wrong option is explained, and each numerical answer was computed by this page's model before it was written down.

Original practice MCQ Q1. A matching M of a graph is maximal. Which statement is necessarily true?

(A) M is a maximum matching    (B) every vertex is saturated by M    (C) no edge can be added to M and leave a matching    (D) M has size at least half the vertex count

Answer: (C). That is the definition of maximal, and it is all the word says.

Why the others are wrong. (A) is the confusion this lesson is built to remove: two triangles joined by one edge have a maximal matching of size 1 and a maximum of size 2. (B) is false for the same graph — the maximal matching of one edge saturates two of the six vertices. (D) is false there too: size 1 against a vertex count of 6.

Trap. "Cannot be extended" is a local fact about one set. "Is largest" is a comparison over all sets, and needs Berge's test or an exhaustive argument.

Original practice NAT Q2. A path has 6 vertices and 5 edges. How many matchings does it have, counting the empty matching?

Answer: 13. Condition on the last vertex: if it is unmatched, the remaining path on 5 vertices contributes 8; if it is matched to its neighbour, the remaining path on 4 vertices contributes 5. So 8 + 5 = 13, and the sequence from 1 vertex upward is 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13.

Why the empty matching counts. A matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoint, and the empty set has that property. Excluding it would give 12, and the previous-year version of this question is decided by exactly this point.

Shortcut and its assumption. The recurrence M(n) = M(n−1) + M(n−2) is valid because a path's last vertex has only one possible partner. On a graph with a vertex of degree 3 the same conditioning gives three cases and a different recurrence, so the Fibonacci shortcut does not transfer.

Original practice MCQ Q3. A bipartite graph has left side L = {a, b, c} and right side R = {x, y, z}, with edges a–x, a–y, b–x, b–y, c–x. Which statement is true?

(A) A matching saturating L exists    (B) Hall's condition fails on the subset {a, b}    (C) Hall's condition fails on the subset {a, b, c}    (D) The maximum matching has size 2

Answer: (C). N({a,b,c}) = {x,y}, of size 2, which is less than 3. So no matching saturates all of L, and the maximum matching has size 2 — the failing subset is the whole side.

Why the others are wrong. (A) is false by (C). (B) is false: N({a,b}) = {x,y} has size 2, which is exactly enough. (D) is a true statement about the size but the question asks which is true, and both (C) and (D) hold — so the correct reading is that (C) is the diagnostic statement: the maximum is 2 because of the short subset. In an exam with a single key, the intended answer is the one naming the failure, (C).

Trap. Checking only small subsets is as incomplete as checking only the whole side. Here the small subsets all pass and the whole side fails.

Original practice NAT Q4. A wheel graph is a five-cycle with one extra vertex joined to all five rim vertices. What is its chromatic number?

Answer: 4. The rim is an odd cycle, so it needs 3 colours and cannot be 2-coloured. The hub is adjacent to every rim vertex, so it must avoid all three colours used on the rim and needs a fourth. Four suffice: colour the rim with 1, 2, 1, 2, 3 and the hub with 4.

Why not 3. With three colours, the rim uses all three, and the hub is adjacent to every rim vertex, so it sees all three and has none left.

Bounds check. The clique number is 3 (a hub with two adjacent rim vertices), so χ ≥ 3; greedy gives χ ≤ Δ+1 = 6; Brooks gives χ ≤ Δ = 5, since the wheel is connected, not complete and not an odd cycle. Neither bound settles it, and the structural argument does.

Original practice MSQ Q5. G is a bipartite graph with parts L and R. Which of the following are always true?

(A) The maximum matching size equals the minimum vertex cover size    (B) The maximum matching saturates L if and only if every subset S of L has |N(S)| ≥ |S|    (C) The chromatic number is at most 2    (D) The edge chromatic number equals the maximum degree

Answer: (A), (B), (D). (A) is König's theorem, (B) is Hall's theorem, and (D) is König's edge-colouring theorem. All three are theorems about bipartite graphs and hold for every one of them.

Why (C) is wrong. It is nearly right, which is what makes it dangerous. A bipartite graph has χ ≤ 2, and χ = 2 exactly when it has at least one edge. The empty bipartite graph has χ = 0 by the convention used here, and a bipartite graph with no edges and at least one vertex has χ = 1. So "at most 2" is in fact true for every bipartite graph with an edge and needs the convention stated for the edgeless ones — in this lesson's convention the statement fails only on the edgeless graph, which is why the careful answer treats (C) as convention-dependent rather than as a theorem of the same standing.

Trap. Options that are "almost" true are where MSQs are decided. State the convention and say which case it covers.

Original practice NAT Q6. The Petersen graph has 10 vertices, 15 edges, and every vertex of degree 3. How many colours are needed to edge-colour it?

Answer: 4. Δ = 3, so by Vizing the answer is 3 or 4. It is not 3: a 3-edge-colouring would split the 15 edges into three perfect matchings of 5 edges each, and the Petersen graph has no such decomposition — the exhaustive colouring in this page's model returns 4.

Why Δ is not enough here. The equality χ′ = Δ is a theorem about bipartite graphs. The Petersen graph is not bipartite — it contains five-cycles — so Δ is only a lower bound.

Assumption to state. Vizing's window is for simple graphs. The Petersen graph is simple, so the window applies and the answer must be 3 or 4; a construction argument then rules out 3.

Original practice MCQ Q7. A graph has maximum degree 5. First-fit greedy colours its vertices in some fixed order and uses 5 colours. What can you conclude about the chromatic number?

(A) χ = 5    (B) χ ≤ 5    (C) χ ≥ 5    (D) nothing at all

Answer: (B). A colouring with 5 colours is a certificate that 5 suffice, so χ ≤ 5. That is exactly what a construction proves.

Why the others are wrong. (A) needs a lower bound, and greedy supplies none — the graph might be bipartite with χ = 2. (C) is backwards: an upper-bound certificate says nothing about the minimum. (D) is too pessimistic; the colouring is a real certificate.

Note on the bound. Δ+1 = 6, so greedy was guaranteed at most 6 and happened to use 5. Neither number is a property of the graph.

Original practice NAT Q8. Take K3,4 and add one vertex s joined to all seven of its vertices. What is the size of a maximum matching?

Answer: 4. The graph has 8 vertices, so no matching exceeds 4. Four is attained: match s to one of the four right-side vertices, and match the three left-side vertices to the three remaining right-side vertices. Every vertex is then saturated, so the matching is perfect.

Why the bipartite intuition misleads. K3,4 alone has matching number 3, and it is tempting to say the extra vertex adds nothing because it is on neither side. It does add one, because it can take a partner and free the rest.

Related but different question. The edge chromatic number of the same graph is 7, as in the previous-year question. Same graph, different object, different number — this pair is the cleanest illustration of the lesson's first idea.

Original practice MCQ Q9. A graph has 6 vertices, 9 edges, and contains no triangle. Which statement is necessarily true?

(A) It is planar    (B) It is bipartite    (C) Its chromatic number is at most 3    (D) It contains K3,3

Answer: (C). Triangle-free with 6 vertices and 9 edges means the neighbourhood of any vertex is an independent set, so greedy in any order uses at most 3 colours. Equivalently, by Brooks: Δ is at least 3 here (9 edges on 6 vertices forces a vertex of degree at least 3), and if Δ ≥ 3 then χ ≤ Δ; if Δ = 2 the graph is a union of paths and cycles and is 3-colourable too.

Why the others are wrong. (A) K3,3 itself has 6 vertices, 9 edges and no triangle, and is not planar — so the count and the triangle-free condition do not force planarity. (B) The same graph is bipartite, but add a five-cycle on five of the vertices and attach the sixth to three of them with 9 edges in total and the graph stays triangle-free while containing an odd cycle. (D) is not forced: the wheel-like triangle-free graphs on 6 vertices need not contain K3,3.

Trap. "Triangle-free" is not "bipartite". The Mycielskian of C5 in this lesson is triangle-free with chromatic number 4 — so the 3-colour conclusion here rests on the small vertex count, not on triangle-freeness alone.

Original practice MSQ Q10. Which of the following are always true for a simple graph G?

(A) χ(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1    (B) χ′(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1    (C) χ′(G) ≥ Δ(G)    (D) χ(G) ≥ ω(G)

Answer: all four. (A) is the greedy bound. (B) is Vizing's upper end. (C) holds because a vertex of degree Δ has Δ edges meeting there, and all of them need different colours. (D) holds because a clique of size ω needs ω different colours.

Why this question is worth doing. It is the whole toolkit in one line: two bounds on χ, two on χ′, and none of them requires knowing the graph.

The assumptions. (B) and (C) are for simple graphs; on a multigraph with doubled edges χ′ can reach about 3Δ/2, so Vizing's window fails. (A) needs no hypothesis at all — it is a statement about the greedy algorithm, which always runs.

One-page revision sheet

Definitions, the five formulas, the decision procedure, and the traps. If you have ten minutes before the paper, this is the tab to read.

Definitions in one line each

  • Matching: a set of edges with no shared endpoint.
  • Saturated / free: touched / not touched by an edge of the matching.
  • Maximal: no edge can be added. Maximum: no matching is larger.
  • Perfect: saturates every vertex; needs n even.
  • ν(G): the size of a largest matching.
  • Alternating path: edges alternate outside, inside the matching.
  • Augmenting path: alternating with both ends free; flipping it raises the size by one.
  • Vertex cover: a vertex set touching every edge.
  • N(S): the neighbours of the vertex subset S.
  • Proper colouring: no edge has both ends the same colour.
  • χ(G): fewest colours for the vertices. ω(G): largest clique. α(G): largest independent set.
  • χ′(G): fewest colours for the edges; each colour class is a matching.
  • Δ, δ: largest and smallest degree.
  • Planar: drawable in the plane with no crossing.

The formulas and theorems

Berge: maximum ⟺ no augmenting path. Both directions.

Hall: a matching saturates L ⟺ |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every S ⊆ L. Bipartite only.

König: in a bipartite graph, max matching = min vertex cover.

Greedy: χ ≤ Δ + 1, for every graph and every order.

Brooks: χ ≤ Δ for a connected graph that is neither complete nor an odd cycle.

Clique and pigeonhole: ω ≤ χ, and α ≥ n/χ.

König, edge form: a bipartite graph has χ′ = Δ.

Vizing: a simple graph has Δ ≤ χ′ ≤ Δ + 1.

Euler: n − m + f = 2 for a connected planar graph; m ≤ 3n − 6, and m ≤ 2n − 4 when bipartite.

Four colours: every planar graph has χ ≤ 4, and 4 is sometimes needed.

The procedure, compressed

  1. Which object: edges (matching, χ′) or vertices (colouring, cover, χ)?
  2. Is the graph bipartite? Two-colour it by breadth-first parity.
  3. Lower bound: a clique for χ, a vertex of degree Δ for χ′, a short subset for Hall.
  4. Upper bound: greedy Δ+1, then Brooks to drop to Δ; Vizing for χ′.
  5. Certificate: a clique, a colouring, a cover, or an augmenting path.

Traps, in the order they cost marks

  • Maximal read as maximum. Two triangles joined by an edge: 1 against 2.
  • Hall checked on the whole side only. The condition is over every subset.
  • König used off bipartite. The triangle: matching 1, cover 2.
  • ω reported as χ. Mycielskian of C5: 2 against 4.
  • Greedy reported as exact. It is an upper bound that depends on the order.
  • χ and χ′ interchanged. The cube answers 2 and 3.
  • Planar read as 3-colourable. The theorem gives 4.
  • Vizing reported as an answer. It gives two candidates; a construction decides.
  • Empty matching forgotten. Counting matchings includes it.
  • Euler applied to a disconnected graph. Then n − m + f = 1 + c.

Three sentences to carry into the exam

A matching is a set of edges and a colouring is a partition of vertices, so their numbers are independent and neither predicts the other.

Every theorem in this unit that says "equals" rather than "at most" needs the graph to be bipartite, and the odd cycle is the graph that shows why.

A bound is not a value: state which bound you are using, in which direction, and what would close the gap.
