Topic and objective

TOPIC: Post’s Correspondence Problem — an instance is a finite set of dominoes, each carrying a string on top and a string on the bottom; a match is an ordering whose two rows read the same string; deciding whether a match exists is undecidable over any alphabet of two letters or more, and decidable over one letter.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: State the problem and check a proposed match. Explain and use the prefix invariant, and say why it is the only pruning a search has. Explain why a bounded search can prove a yes and can never prove a no. Convert MPCP to PCP by padding, and say what the padding forces. Explain how a match becomes an accepting computation history, and state Post’s theorem with its alphabet hypothesis. Use the same reduction to answer whether grammar ambiguity, CFL intersection emptiness and CFG equivalence are decidable. Run the three-case decider for a one-letter alphabet, and say why that case is a counterexample to the missing hypothesis rather than an exception to the theorem.

I read this topic as one object and four consequences: the object is a search tree whose pruning rule is forced, and the consequences are the three CFL results plus the unary counterexample. Every number on this page — the match 1, 2, 3, 3, the 84 sequences against 12 nodes, the frontier that stays at 2, the padded match 1, 4, 5, the unary construction’s 5 letters — is computed at load by the lesson’s own model and re-checked by a second route before it is drawn.

Prerequisites

Four ideas, and the first three are already published in this library.

  • Turing machines and undecidability — decidability, the halting problem, and what a reduction carries. This page uses that lesson’s vocabulary and adds nothing to it.
  • Context-free grammars — a grammar, a derivation, and ambiguity.
  • Closure and decision properties — which questions about regular and context-free languages are decidable. That lesson states the answers; this one proves three of them.
  • Sets, functions and strings — concatenation, prefix, and a finite sequence of indices. Nothing more is assumed.

Approximate learning time: 65 minutes, of which about 20 is the animation.

Five ideas, and the rest follows

  1. A match is an ordering, and a domino may be reused. The input is finite; the search space is not.
  2. At every step of a match one row is a prefix of the other. That is forced, and it is the only pruning there is.
  3. A bounded search can prove a yes. It can never prove a no. That asymmetry is the undecidability, seen from the algorithm’s side.
  4. Over two letters or more it is undecidable, and ambiguity, intersection emptiness and inequivalence of context-free grammars all fall to one reduction.
  5. Over one letter it is decidable in three cases. The alphabet size is part of the theorem, not a detail of the proof.

What this page does not do

It does not prove Post’s theorem from first principles. The reduction in scene 7 is described at the level of what each kind of domino carries and what the resulting match means; the full seven-part construction, with its left-move and right-move cases and its end-game dominoes, is in Sipser and is not reproduced here.

Educational interpretation The description is enough to answer an exam question that asks why the problem is undecidable or which problems follow from it. It is not enough to reconstruct the reduction, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

1. Intuition: two rows of tiles that must read the same

Imagine a set of rectangular tiles. Each tile has a word printed on its top half and a word printed on its bottom half, and the two words need not be the same length. You lay tiles side by side, in any order, reusing any tile as often as you like. The two rows of print then run together into two long strings.

The question is whether some finite row of tiles makes those two long strings identical. Not similar. Identical, letter for letter.

Educational interpretation The tiles are the standard way this problem is drawn, and the analogy is exact: nothing about it is a simplification. It stops being helpful only at the point where the search is described, which is scene 4.

2. Formal definition, every symbol in words

Fix an alphabet Σ with at least two letters. An instance is a finite list of pairs of strings over Σ:

$$(u_1, v_1),\ (u_2, v_2),\ \dots,\ (u_n, v_n)$$ Each pair is one domino. ui is the top string and vi is the bottom string of domino i.

A match is a non-empty sequence of indices i1, i2, …, ik, each between 1 and n, with

$$u_{i_1}u_{i_2}\cdots u_{i_k} \;=\; v_{i_1}v_{i_2}\cdots v_{i_k}$$ Read the top strings in the order chosen, and the bottom strings in the same order. A match is an ordering where those two concatenations are the same string.

The problem is: given an arbitrary instance, decide whether a match exists. It is a yes/no question about the instance, not a request for the match.

3. Visual understanding: what a search actually looks like

A search builds sequences one domino at a time, and at every partial sequence it holds two strings. Two facts make that tractable:

  • The prefix invariant. If the finished rows are equal, then at every intermediate step the shorter row is a prefix of the longer one. The reason is that appending never changes letters already written, so the shorter row can only ever be the beginning of the longer one.
  • The overhang. The unmatched tail of the longer row is called the overhang. A match is exactly a branch where the overhang becomes empty.

These give the only pruning available: if appending a domino breaks the prefix condition, that branch is dead and no continuation can revive it. Nothing else can be ruled out, which is why the search is a tree of unbounded depth rather than a graph of finitely many states.

Mathematical derivation Both statements follow from the definition in one line each, and the model on this page checks the invariant at every step of the worked match rather than asserting it.

4. Worked example, with the reason for each step

Instance: (a, ab), (b, aa), (aa, a), (ab, b).

  1. No domino has equal strings, so a match of length one is impossible. Reason: the four pairs differ.
  2. Take domino 1: rows are a and ab. Overhang b on the bottom. Compatible, so the branch survives.
  3. Take domino 2: rows are ab and abaa. Overhang aa. Still compatible.
  4. Take domino 3: rows are abaa and abaaa. Overhang a.
  5. Take domino 3 again: rows are abaaaa and abaaaa. Overhang empty. Match.

The sequence is 1, 2, 3, 3. The model confirms by exhaustive enumeration that no sequence of length 1, 2 or 3 is a match, so this is a shortest one.

5. Boundaries and constraints

Four conditions are load-bearing, and each one changes the answer if it fails.

  • The alphabet has at least two letters. With one letter the problem is decidable, and scene 10 gives the decider. This is a hypothesis of the theorem, not a technicality.
  • Repetition is allowed. Forbidding reuse gives a different, easier problem.
  • The question is existence, not construction. Answering “here is a match” settles a yes; nothing settles a no except a proof.
  • The instance is finite. An infinite instance is a different problem; every reduction in this lesson produces a finite one.

Verified fact Post’s 1946 paper states the problem over the two-letter alphabet {a, b} and proves it recursively unsolvable.

6. Edge cases and a counterexample to the obvious search

No solution, decided cheaply. If every top string begins with a different letter from its own bottom string, and no domino can ever be placed first, the instance has no match — the frontier dies at depth one. Post notes this case in the 1946 paper.

No solution, undecided cheaply. The instance (a, ab), (b, bb), (ba, b) has no match, but every surviving branch has the bottom longer by one letter, then two, then three. The frontier never empties and the search never finishes. The model runs it to depth twelve and reports the frontier at each depth rather than a verdict.

The counterexample. These two instances are both “no match”, and only the first is settled by the search. That pair is the whole reason the problem is undecidable rather than merely hard.

7. GATE application, and 8. practice

GATE asks this material in three shapes: a one-line “decidable or undecidable” classification for a named problem; a question that hands you a sequence and asks whether it is a match; and a question about which of several statements hold, where the wrong options are the four slips on the Misconceptions tab.

The exam does not ask you to run a search. It asks you to know which side of the line a problem falls on, and to state the theorem with its hypothesis intact.

9. Consolidation

One object: a finite set of pairs, an ordering, and two rows that must agree. One invariant: one row is always a prefix of the other, which is the only pruning and never a decider. One theorem: undecidable over two letters or more, by a reduction from halting, with the match playing the part of an accepting computation history. Three consequences: ambiguity, intersection emptiness, inequivalence — all one reduction. One boundary: a single letter makes it decidable, in three cases, and that is the counterexample the hypothesis exists to exclude.

If you can say those five sentences without the page in front of you, the topic is covered at the depth the syllabus asks for.

Example 1 — check a proposed match

Instance: (a, ab), (b, aa), (aa, a), (ab, b). Proposed sequence: 1, 2, 3, 3.

Top: a · b · aa · aa = abaaaa (6 letters).

Bottom: ab · aa · a · a = abaaaa (6 letters).

Equal, so this sequence is a match. Checking cost four concatenations and one comparison: the work is proportional to the total length, which is why checking is easy.

Now try 1, 2, 3: top abaa, bottom abaaa. Not equal, and not a match. A near miss is still a miss.

Example 2 — the invariant, step by step

After each prefix of 1, 2, 3, 3, record both lengths and the overhang:

after 11 vs 2bottom overhang b
after 1,22 vs 4bottom overhang aa
after 1,2,34 vs 5bottom overhang a
after 1,2,3,36 vs 6empty: a match

At every step the shorter row is a prefix of the longer one, which the model checks on the strings rather than on the picture.

The overhang never vanishes until the last domino, and that is typical: a match is a branch that survives to the end, not one that keeps returning to zero.

Example 3 — the search counted two ways

With four dominoes there are 4 sequences of length one, 16 of length two and 64 of length three: 84 in total. Enumerating all of them is what an unpruned search does before it looks at length four.

With the prefix test the survivor counts are 2, 2, 4 for those levels, and the match is found at level four, so 12 nodes are examined in all.

Twelve against eighty-four. That is the entire value of the invariant, and it is a constant-factor saving: the tree is still infinite.

The model computes both counts from the instance and asserts the relation 4 × 12 < 84 rather than quoting a remembered figure.

Example 4 — an instance with no match, searched to depth 12

Instance: (a, ab), (b, bb), (ba, b). The model finds no match at any depth up to twelve.

The overhang lengths along the surviving branch are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and the frontier holds 2 branches at every level after the first. Nothing closes, and nothing dies.

So the run reports no match within twelve, which is true, and not no match, which it cannot know.

The difference between those two sentences is the most examined sentence on this topic.

Example 5 — MPCP to PCP, on a three-domino instance

MPCP instance with the first domino fixed: (a, aa), (ba, a), (aa, a). Its match is 1, 3: top a+aa = aaa, bottom aa+a = aaa.

Pad it: a star before every top letter, a star after every bottom letter, the first domino padded on both sides, and one closing domino (*@, @). The result has 5 dominoes.

The padded match is 1, 4, 5 and both rows read *a*a*a*@. Only domino 1 starts with the same symbol on both rows, so every match is forced to begin there.

Stripping the stars and dropping the closer returns 1, 3. The model checks that recovery, not just the forward direction.

Example 6 — the unary decider on three instances

(11, 1) and (1, 111): differences +1 and −2. Both signs present, so a match exists: two copies of the first and one of the second give 11111 on both rows, in 3 dominoes. Brute force agrees, and confirms no shorter match.

(11, 1) and (111, 1): differences +1 and +2. One sign, so no match: every sum is positive.

(11, 11) and (111, 1): a zero difference, so that domino alone is a match, of length one.

The decider and a brute-force search agree on all three, which is a check of the implementation. The proof of the decider is the three-case argument, not the agreement.

Boundary: one letter

Over Σ = {1} the problem is decidable, and the decider is three lines. A zero difference wins immediately; if every difference has the same sign, no sum can reach zero and the answer is no; otherwise one positive and one negative difference combine to zero.

This is a genuine counterexample to the statement “PCP is undecidable” stated without its hypothesis, not a quirk to be memorised separately.

Boundary: an empty string in a domino

Nothing in the definition forbids a domino whose top is empty. Such a domino can be appended without changing one row at all, which lets a match grow in one row only. Some textbook presentations assume all strings non-empty; the assumption changes which instances are legal, not the undecidability.

If a question says “non-empty strings”, that is a restriction on the input and worth noticing, because it can make an otherwise trivial match unavailable.

The trap: a match that repeats a domino

The worked match 1, 2, 3, 3 uses domino 3 twice. Any procedure that forbids repetition misses it, and would report the instance as having no match.

Repetition is allowed because the definition allows any sequence of indices. Removing it is a different problem, and an easier one.

The trap: a near miss that looks like a match

After 1, 2, 3 the rows are abaa and abaaa. One letter apart, and not a match. Comparisons are exact and letter by letter; there is no notion of closeness in the definition.

Questions that hand you a sequence are testing exactly this: read the two strings out fully before answering.

Boundary: how many dominoes an instance has

Verified fact Restricting the number of dominoes changes the answer: with at most two dominoes the problem is decidable, and it stays undecidable once the number is large enough.

Unverified claim The exact threshold is reported differently in different sources consulted this run — one survey gives decidability for two and undecidability from five upwards with the cases in between open, another reports a different bound. This page therefore states only the two ends and does not assert a threshold.

Verified fact The bounded version — is there a match using at most k dominoes — is NP-complete, so a brute-force search is not merely slow by accident.

A distinction that looks like one thing

Undecidable is about algorithms: no single procedure answers correctly on every instance. Unsolvable instance is about one input: this particular set of dominoes has no match. The first is a theorem about a class of inputs; the second is a fact about one input, which you may be able to prove by hand.

A decidable problem can have instances nobody can settle quickly, and an undecidable problem can have instances settled in one line. The words are not about difficulty.

One more distinction: the two directions of the padding

The MPCP-to-PCP padding works because the stars sit on opposite sides of the two rows. Pad both rows the same way and the first-domino requirement is not enforced, because another domino could still start a match. The construction is correct as a whole: the padded first domino, the star-interleaved middle, and the closing domino are one object, and dropping any part of it breaks the if-and-only-if.

“The search found no match, so there is none”

Correction. A bounded search proves a yes when it finds a match, and proves nothing when it does not.

Counterexample. The instance (a, ab), (b, bb), (ba, b) survives to depth twelve with two open branches at every level and no match. The search has not shown that no match exists; it has shown that none exists within twelve.

“Undecidable means this instance has no solution”

Correction. Undecidability is a statement about the absence of one algorithm covering every instance. It says nothing about any particular instance.

Counterexample. The instance (a, ab), (b, aa), (aa, a), (ab, b) is undecidable in the same sense as every other instance, and its match 1, 2, 3, 3 can be printed in one line.

“Checking a match is as hard as deciding whether one exists”

Correction. Checking is a walk along two strings: concatenate, compare, cost proportional to total length. Deciding existence is the undecidable question.

Why it feels right. Both are called “the problem” in casual speech. In an exam they are different questions with different answers, and the phrase “given a match, verify it” is the tell.

“PCP is undecidable, full stop”

Correction. Over a one-letter alphabet it is decidable, and the decider is three cases long. The alphabet hypothesis is part of the theorem.

Counterexample. Over {1}, the instance (11, 1), (1, 111) is decided by the sign test: differences +1 and −2, both signs present, so a match exists — two copies of the first domino and one of the second.

“Rice’s theorem settles PCP”

Correction. Rice’s theorem classifies properties of the language a machine recognises. PCP is not a property of a machine’s language, and it has its own reduction from the halting problem.

Why it gets said. Both are “undecidability by reduction” results, and naming the wrong theorem costs the marks even when the verdict is right.

“The prefix condition at every step means a match exists”

Correction. The invariant is necessary, never sufficient. It prunes branches; it never certifies a match.

Counterexample. In the growing-overhang instance every surviving branch satisfies the prefix condition at every level, and no branch ever matches. Satisfaction of the invariant is exactly what makes the search infinite there.

What all six have in common

Each is a claim whose scope has been widened by one word: found becomes exists, no algorithm becomes no solution, deciding becomes checking, over two letters becomes always, this theorem becomes that theorem, necessary becomes sufficient. The defence is the same every time: put the hypothesis back into the sentence and read it again.

How the material is examined

Four shapes cover almost everything this topic contributes to a paper:

  1. Decidable or undecidable. A named problem, one line of answer. Ambiguity of a context-free grammar: undecidable. Emptiness of a context-free language: decidable. Equivalence of two regular expressions: decidable.
  2. Is this sequence a match? Two concatenations and one comparison. Easy marks if you read the strings out in full.
  3. Which statements hold. An MSQ whose wrong options are the six misconceptions on the previous tab.
  4. Why is it undecidable? A short-answer or MCQ asking for the reduction, where the acceptable answer names the halting problem or the computation history and the unacceptable answer is “a search does not finish”.

The classification that is asked most

Is a CFG ambiguous?undecidable
Is L(G1) ∩ L(G2) empty?undecidable
Are G1 and G2 equivalent?undecidable
Is a CFL empty / finite?decidable
Does w ∈ L(G)?decidable
Is L(G) = Σ*?undecidable

Every “undecidable” in this table is a PCP reduction in disguise, which is why they are taught together.

The solving procedure, in order

  1. Read which question is being asked: existence, verification, classification, or reason.
  2. If it is verification, concatenate both rows and compare. Nothing else applies.
  3. If it is classification, name the class first — regular, context-free, general — then the question. The line between decidable and undecidable runs along the class.
  4. If it is a reason, name the reduction and the problem reduced from. “No algorithm has been found” is never the answer.
  5. If the alphabet size is stated, use it. A one-letter instance is decidable, and saying otherwise is a wrong answer however good the reasoning.

MCQ original

Q1 — which instance has a match

Exactly one of these instances over {a, b} has a match. Which one?

(a) (a, ab), (b, aa), (aa, a), (ab, b)
(b) (a, bb), (aa, b), (aab, bb)
(c) (aa, a), (ab, b), (bb, a)
(d) (a, ab), (b, ba), (a, bb)

(a). The sequence 1, 2, 3, 3 gives abaaaa on both rows.

(b) has no match by the first-letter test: every top begins with a and every bottom begins with b, so the two rows can never start with the same letter. (c) has no match by the length test: every top has length 2 and every bottom length 1, so a sequence of k dominoes gives top length 2k and bottom length k, which are never equal. (d) fails the length test the other way round: tops have length 1, bottoms length 2.

Each wrong option is ruled out by one line, not by a failed search. That is the difference between a proof and an observation, and it is what a well-set MCQ gives you.

NAT original

Q2 — the shortest match

For the instance (a, ab), (b, aa), (aa, a), (ab, b), the number of dominoes in a shortest match is ______.

4. The match is 1, 2, 3, 3.

It is shortest because every sequence of length 1, 2 and 3 was enumerated: 4 + 16 + 64 = 84 sequences, and none has equal rows. No domino has equal strings, so length 1 fails without searching.

Breadth-first order alone does not prove minimality — it finds a shortest match only if the search really is level by level. The exhaustive check is the proof.

MSQ original

Q3 — which statements hold

Select every statement that is true.

(a) If a search finds no match up to depth k, the instance has no match.
(b) A domino may be used more than once in a match.
(c) Over a one-letter alphabet the problem is decidable.
(d) Every instance whose branches satisfy the prefix condition at every level has a match.
(e) Deciding whether a context-free grammar is ambiguous would decide this problem.

(b), (c) and (e).

(b) holds by the definition: any sequence of indices is allowed, and the worked match uses domino 3 twice. (c) holds by the three-case decider. (e) holds by the LU ∪ LV construction.

(a) is the first slip: a bounded search proves only a bounded claim. (d) is the necessary-versus-sufficient slip: the growing-overhang instance satisfies the prefix condition at every level and never matches.

(a) and (d) are the two misconceptions this lesson exists to remove, and each has a computed counterexample on the Misconceptions tab.

MCQ original

Q4 — the one-letter instance

Over the alphabet {1}, the instance (11, 1), (1, 111) is given. Which is correct?

(a) It is undecidable whether it has a match.
(b) It has no match, because the lengths never agree.
(c) It has a match: two copies of the first domino and one of the second.
(d) It has a match only if the dominoes are used in the given order.

(c). Differences are +1 and −2. Two copies of (11, 1) and one copy of (1, 111) give 5 letters on each row: 11111 and 11111.

(a) is wrong because the alphabet has one letter, which makes the problem decidable. (b) is wrong because the sum of differences does reach zero. (d) invents an order requirement that only MPCP has, and only when it is stated.

The construction is the general one: with differences +a and −b, take b copies of the positive domino and a copies of the negative one.

MSQ original

Q5 — which questions are undecidable

For context-free grammars G1, G2 given as input, which of these are undecidable?

(a) Is L(G1) empty?
(b) Is L(G1) ∩ L(G2) empty?
(c) Is L(G1) = L(G2)?
(d) Is G1 ambiguous?
(e) Is L(G1) finite?

(b), (c) and (d). All three are PCP reductions: a shared word is a match, an equivalent grammar is a match, and an ambiguous grammar is a match.

(a) and (e) are decidable — convert to Chomsky normal form and check which nonterminals can derive a terminal string, and whether any can derive arbitrarily long strings.

Note that (a) is decidable while (b) is not, even though (b) contains (a) as a special case with G2 fixed. Decidability is not preserved by adding a second grammar.

Q6 — the padded instance size

An MPCP instance has 3 dominoes. After the padding construction used in this lesson, the resulting PCP instance has ______ dominoes.

5. One padded copy of the first domino, one star-interleaved copy of each of the three dominoes, and one closing domino: 1 + 3 + 1 = 5.

For n dominoes the count is n + 2, so it is linear in the input and the reduction is computable.

The instance on this page is exactly that case: (a, aa), (ba, a), (aa, a) becomes the five dominoes (*a, *a*a*), (*a, aa*), (*ba, a*), (*aa, a*), (*@, @).

MCQ original

Q7 — the theorem, stated correctly

Which statement is the theorem?

(a) PCP is undecidable.
(b) PCP is undecidable over any alphabet with at least two letters.
(c) PCP is undecidable over the one-letter alphabet.
(d) PCP is undecidable for instances with at least five dominoes and decidable below that.

(b). The alphabet hypothesis is part of the theorem.

(a) is the same sentence with its hypothesis removed, and it is false: over {1} the problem is decidable. (c) is false and is the exact counterexample. (d) asserts a threshold that the sources consulted this run do not agree on, so it cannot be stated as a theorem here.

An exam that offers (a) and (b) is testing whether you know that the hypothesis is load-bearing. Read both to the end.

NAT original

Q8 — counting the unpruned search

An instance has 5 dominoes. An unpruned search enumerates every sequence of length 1, 2 and 3. The number of sequences enumerated is ______.

155. 5 + 25 + 125 = 155.

In general it is n + n2 + n3 for three levels, and nk at level k alone. With four dominoes the same three levels give 4 + 16 + 64 = 84, which is the figure the animation uses.

This is why the prefix test matters in practice and why it still does not matter in theory: the count grows exponentially in the depth, whatever the pruning constant is.

MCQ original

Q9 — why a bounded search is not a decision procedure

A program searches every sequence up to length 100 and reports “match” or “no match”. Why is this not a decision procedure for PCP?

(a) Because 100 is too small a bound for real instances.
(b) Because the program may answer “no match” for an instance whose only match is longer than 100.
(c) Because checking a match is itself undecidable.
(d) Because the program does not use the prefix condition.

(b). A “no match” answer is only a claim about sequences of length at most 100, and some instances have their first match far beyond any fixed bound.

(a) is a statement about speed, not correctness: raising the bound to a million changes nothing in kind. (c) is false — checking is a linear walk along two strings. (d) is irrelevant: pruning makes the program faster and does not make it correct.

Adding pruning gives a faster semidecision procedure. The word that matters is “semidecision”.

Definitions in one line each

Instance — a finite list of pairs of strings over an alphabet of at least two letters.

Match — a non-empty sequence of indices whose top strings concatenated equal its bottom strings concatenated.

MPCP — the same, with the first domino of the sequence fixed by the input.

Overhang — the unmatched tail of the longer row at a partial match.

Prefix invariant — at every partial match, one row is a prefix of the other.

Results, with hypotheses

  • Verified fact PCP is undecidable over any alphabet with at least two letters (Post, 1946).
  • Verified fact PCP is decidable over a one-letter alphabet, by the three-case sign argument.
  • Verified fact MPCP reduces to PCP by padding, so a PCP decider would decide MPCP.
  • Verified fact Ambiguity of a context-free grammar is undecidable, by reduction from PCP.
  • Verified fact Emptiness of the intersection of two context-free languages is undecidable.
  • Verified fact Equivalence of two context-free grammars is undecidable.
  • Verified fact The bounded version, with a limit of k dominoes, is NP-complete.

  2. One instance: check the given sequence by concatenating both rows.
  3. All of them: name the class and then the question, and answer from the table on the GATE patterns tab.
  4. Is an alphabet size stated? One letter flips the answer to decidable.
  5. Is a reason asked for? Name the reduction, never the failed search.

Numbers to remember from this lesson

  • The worked match is 1, 2, 3, 3 and reads abaaaa.
  • 84 sequences unpruned against 12 nodes pruned, for levels 1 to 3.
  • The growing-overhang instance holds 2 branches at every level, with no match to depth 12.
  • The padded match is 1, 4, 5 and reads *a*a*a*@.
  • The unary construction for differences +1 and −2 uses 3 dominoes and gives 5 letters a side.

Every one of these is computed by the page’s model and checked against a second route, so they can be reproduced rather than memorised.

What to be careful about when revising

The two sentences that decide most marks are “a match exists” and “no match of length at most k exists”. They are not the same claim, and only the first is ever proved by a search. The second sentence that decides marks is the theorem: undecidable over at least two letters, with the hypothesis present. Revise those two sentences, the three-case unary argument, and the one-line classification of ambiguity, intersection emptiness and equivalence, and the topic is covered.

Primary source

Emil L. Post, “A variant of a recursively unsolvable problem”, Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 52 (1946), 264–268. Defines the problem over the two-letter alphabet {a, b} and proves it recursively unsolvable. Also states the two cheap no-conditions used in scene 5: every top longer than its bottom, and every top beginning with a different letter from its bottom.

Supports: the definition, the 1946 attribution, the undecidability result, and the first-letter test.

Textbook treatments

Michael Sipser, Introduction to the Theory of Computation, 3rd edition, section 5.2. The seven-part reduction from the halting problem to MPCP, and the padding construction that turns MPCP into PCP (the star-interleaved form used in scene 6).

Jean Gallier, Notes on the theory of computation, section 6.8. States PCP, sketches the reduction, and gives the application to grammar ambiguity as Theorem 6.8.2, with the LU ∪ LV construction reproduced in scene 8.

Supports: the padding construction, the computation-history idea, and the ambiguity reduction.

Course notes consulted

RPI, Post correspondence problem (course notes). The star-padding reduction, stated as “the only domino whose top and bottom start with the same symbol”, which is the fact the model checks on the padded instance in scene 6.

Brown University, Diagonalization and PCP (lecture notes). The same construction with worked examples, used to cross-check the form of the padding.

Supports: the shape of the padding and the “only one starter” property.

On the unary case

P. Rudnicki and G. J. Woeginger, “The Post correspondence problem over a unary alphabet”, Applied Mathematics Letters 16 (2003). States that the problem is decidable over a one-letter alphabet and studies the complexity of the shortest solution.

Sipser, exercise 5.17 solutions in circulation. The three-step decider reproduced in scene 10: a zero difference wins, one sign loses, both signs win by an explicit construction.

Supports: the decidability of the unary case and the three-case argument.

Reference entries

Wikipedia, “Post correspondence problem”. Used for the survey of variants: the bounded version is NP-complete, and the number of dominoes changes decidability. Where sources disagree on the exact threshold, this page says so rather than choosing one.

V. Halava, “Another proof of undecidability for the correspondence decision problem” (arXiv:1411.5197). Confirms the 1946 attribution and the binary-alphabet formulation, and gives the encoding argument that any finite alphabet reduces to two letters.

Supports: the variants paragraph, the encoding argument, and the historical note.
