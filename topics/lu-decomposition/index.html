Topic and objective

TOPIC: LU decomposition, the last clause of the Linear Algebra line of the GATE CS syllabus. The lesson builds the factorisation from elimination, reads the multipliers into a lower triangular matrix, solves a system with two triangular solves, takes the determinant off the diagonal, and states exactly when the factorisation fails.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Given a square matrix, produce A = L U by recording the multipliers, state which diagonal carries the ones, and confirm the pair by multiplying back. Solve A x = b as L y = b followed by U x = y. Read det(A) as the product of the diagonal of U, with a sign flip per row swap. Say when a no-pivot factorisation exists and repair it as P A = L U. Count the cost as (2/3)n cubed, and say why that is not cheaper than elimination for a single right-hand side.

I am reading this microtopic as one recording rule and four consequences: the rule is that each multiplier used to clear an entry is kept in the position it cleared, and the consequences are the factorisation, the two solves, the determinant, and the cost.

Level 1 — Intuition, and Level 2 — the formal definitions

Educational interpretation Elimination is a recipe you follow once and then throw away. If you write the recipe down as you go, you can re-run it on a different set of ingredients without redoing the chopping. L is the recipe; U is the chopped result; the two together are the original matrix.

Lower triangular. A square matrix with every entry above the diagonal equal to zero. Upper triangular. Every entry below the diagonal equal to zero.

LU decomposition. Square matrices L and U with A = L U, L lower triangular and U upper triangular. Doolittle fixes the diagonal of L to ones; Crout fixes the diagonal of U to ones. Without one of those conventions the pair is not unique.

Multiplier. In step k, the number m(i,k) = (current entry) / (pivot) that is subtracted from row i to clear column k. In Doolittle it is stored as L(i,k).

Level 3 — Visual understanding: the recipe, kept

Draw the matrix as a grid. Elimination clears the entries below the diagonal one column at a time. Every time an entry is cleared, the multiplier that cleared it is dropped into that same cell of a second grid. The first grid, once the clearing is done, is U; the second grid, with ones on its diagonal, is L.

That is the whole picture: U is what is left, L is what was used. Nothing else is computed, which is why the factorisation costs the same as elimination itself.

Level 4 — Worked examples, one line each

Integers only. A = [[2,1,-1],[-4,-1,3],[2,2,2]] gives L = [[1,0,0],[-2,1,0],[1,1,1]] and U = [[2,1,-1],[0,1,1],[0,0,2]]. The multipliers are -2, 1 and 1.

The 2022 system. L32 = -1/2, U33 = -1/2, x = (0, 2, -1).

The 2015 item. A = [[2,2],[4,9]] with the diagonal of U set to ones gives L = [[2,0],[4,5]], so l22 = 5.

A determinant. The 4x4 with pivots 4, 7/4, 12/7, 5/3 has det = 20, which cofactor expansion confirms.

Level 5 — Boundaries: when the factorisation exists

The theorem, with its assumption. A square matrix has an LU decomposition with unit diagonal on L, and it is unique, exactly when every leading principal minor D1, D2, ..., D(n-1) is nonzero. The minors are the determinants of the top-left k by k blocks. The last one, D(n), is the determinant itself and may be zero.

What the assumption is not. It is not invertibility. A matrix can be invertible and still fail, because D2 can vanish while D3 does not.

The repair. Allow row swaps and every square matrix has a P A = L U decomposition. The permutation P costs nothing to record and one sign flip in the determinant.

Level 6 — Edge cases

D(n) = 0 but D(n-1) nonzero. The factorisation still exists and is still unique; only the last pivot is zero, and the system has no unique solution. This is the case students wrongly report as "LU does not exist".

A zero pivot that is not a zero minor. After the first update the pivot is read from the trailing block, not from the original diagonal. The pivot can be zero even when the corresponding original entry is not.

Rectangular matrices. A non-square matrix has no LU decomposition in this sense; it has an L U with U trapezoidal, which is outside this syllabus clause.

Level 7 to 9 — what the exam asks, and what to keep

Level 7, GATE application. Three shapes recur: read an entry of L or U off a stated factorisation; solve a system by the two triangular solves; decide a structural statement about the factors. Each is a question about the recording rule, not about arithmetic speed.

Level 8, practice. The Practice tab holds eight items in learning mode, three of them verified previous-year questions.

Level 9, consolidation. U is what elimination leaves; L is what elimination used. Ones on L is Doolittle, ones on U is Crout. det is the product of the pivots, times -1 per swap. It exists when the leading minors up to n-1 are nonzero, and P A = L U always exists.

Example 1 — an integer factorisation, start to finish

Take A = [[2,1,-1],[-4,-1,3],[2,2,2]].

  1. Pivot a11 = 2. Clear column one. Justification: the multiplier is entry over pivot.
  2. m21 = -4/2 = -2. Row two becomes [-4 - (-2)(2), -1 - (-2)(1), 3 - (-2)(-1)] = [0, 1, 1].
  3. m31 = 2/2 = 1. Row three becomes [0, 1, 3].
  4. Pivot is now 1. Justification: the pivot comes from the trailing block, not the original diagonal.
  5. m32 = 1/1 = 1. The last entry becomes 3 - 1(1) = 2.

L = [[1,0,0],[-2,1,0],[1,1,1]], U = [[2,1,-1],[0,1,1],[0,0,2]]. The page multiplies them back in exact arithmetic and recovers all nine entries of A.

Example 2 — the GATE CSE 2022 system

Solve x1 + x2 - 2x3 = 4, x1 + 3x2 - x3 = 7, 2x1 + x2 - 5x3 = 7 by LU decomposition.

The factors. L = [[1,0,0],[1,1,0],[2,-1/2,1]], U = [[1,1,-2],[0,2,1],[0,0,-1/2]].

Forward. y1 = 4; y2 = 7 - 1(4) = 3; y3 = 7 - 2(4) + 0.5(3) = 0.5.

Back. x3 = 0.5 / (-0.5) = -1; x2 = (3 + 1)/2 = 2; x1 = 4 - 2 + 2 = 0.

Check. The residual A x - b is exactly (0,0,0), and det = 1 x 2 x (-1/2) = -1, which the cofactor expansion confirms.

Example 3 — the GATE CSE 2015 item, read the convention first

A = [[2,2],[4,9]], and the question fixes the diagonal of U to ones, so the pair is Crout: L = [[l11,0],[l21,l22]], U = [[1,u12],[0,1]].

  1. l11 = a11 = 2.
  2. u12 = a12 / l11 = 2/2 = 1.
  3. l21 = a21 = 4.
  4. l21 u12 + l22 = a22, so 4(1) + l22 = 9 and l22 = 5.

The same matrix under Doolittle gives L = [[1,0],[2,1]] and U = [[2,2],[0,5]], where the lower diagonal entry of L is 1. Both pairs multiply back to A; only one answers the question asked.

Example 4 — determinant and a structural decision

The determinant. For A4 with rows 4,3,2,1 / 3,4,3,2 / 2,3,4,3 / 1,2,3,4, the pivots are 4, 7/4, 12/7 and 5/3. Their product is 20. The cofactor expansion gives 20, and the fourth leading minor is 20. Three routes, one number.

The structural decision. Is it true that a symmetric P has symmetric factors? Test P = [[4,2],[2,3]]. The factors are L = [[1,0],[1/2,1]] and U = [[4,2],[0,2]]. L has a 0 where U has a 1/2, and U has a 2 where L has a 0, so neither is symmetric. One counterexample settles the statement.

Counterexample — invertible, yet no LU without row swaps

A = [[1,1,1],[1,1,2],[1,2,2]]. Its leading principal minors are 1, then 1(1) - 1(1) = 0, then -1.

The determinant is -1, so the matrix is invertible and the system has a unique solution. But D2 = 0, so after step one the second pivot is zero and the multiplier m32 would divide by it. Elimination stops.

The condition it violates: the leading-minor hypothesis of the LU theorem. Swapping rows 2 and 3 repairs it, and the page verifies P A = L U and then reads det = 1 x 1 x 1 x (-1) = -1, matching the cofactor value.

Boundary — the last pivot may be zero

If D1 to D(n-1) are all nonzero but D(n) = 0, the factorisation still exists and is still unique. Only u(nn) is zero, the product of the pivots is zero, and A is singular.

Example: [[1,2],[2,4]] factors as L = [[1,0],[2,1]] and U = [[1,2],[0,0]]. The diagonal of U carries the zero, which is exactly statement C of the 2025 item. Reading "det = 0" as "no factorisation" is the mistake this boundary catches.

Assumption — what "unique" needs

Uniqueness needs two things at once: the diagonal convention, and the nonzero leading minors. Drop the convention and any diagonal D gives a second pair, L D and D-inverse U. Drop the minors and there may be no pair at all.

A question that says "the LU decomposition" without naming a convention is relying on the Doolittle convention, which is the usual default. A question that names the diagonal of U is asking for Crout.

Edge case — the pivot is not the original entry

In step k the pivot is the current corner of the trailing block, after every earlier update has been applied. A matrix can have a nonzero entry in position (2,2) and still produce a zero second pivot.

So "a22 is not zero, therefore the second pivot is not zero" is not a valid argument. The valid one is "D2 is not zero, therefore the second pivot is not zero" — the pivot is the ratio D2/D1.

"An invertible matrix always has an LU decomposition"

Why it tempts: invertibility is the condition for solving A x = b uniquely, so it feels like the condition for factorising.

Minimal counterexample: [[1,1,1],[1,1,2],[1,2,2]] has det = -1 and no LU without swaps, because D2 = 0.

Correct rule: every square matrix has P A = L U. The plain A = L U needs the leading minors D1 to D(n-1) nonzero.

"det(A) is the product of the diagonal of L"

Why it tempts: the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal, and both L and U are triangular.

Minimal counterexample: under Doolittle the diagonal of L is all ones, so the product is 1 whatever A is. On the 2022 matrix that would give 1, not -1.

Correct rule: det = det(L) det(U) = 1 x (product of the diagonal of U), times -1 once per row swap.

"The LU decomposition is unique"

Why it tempts: the phrase "the LU decomposition" appears in questions as though one object were meant.

Minimal counterexample: A = [[2,2],[4,9]] has L = [[1,0],[2,1]], U = [[2,2],[0,5]] under Doolittle and L = [[2,0],[4,5]], U = [[1,1],[0,1]] under Crout. Both are valid.

Correct rule: unique only after a diagonal convention is fixed, and only when the leading minors allow it.

"L is the transpose of U"

Why it tempts: the two matrices look like mirror images in the diagrams, and one pair does satisfy it.

Minimal counterexample: in example 1, L has -2 in position (2,1) while U has 1 in position (1,2). They are unrelated.

Correct rule: L equals U-transpose only in the Cholesky case, for a symmetric positive definite matrix, where the factorisation is written A = L L-transpose.

"LU is faster than Gaussian elimination"

Why it tempts: the factorisation is described as the efficient method.

Minimal counterexample: at n = 10, one system costs 705 either way; the LU route costs 895 with its solves, which is more.

Correct rule: the same cubic per system. It wins from the second right-hand side onward, because the factorisation is not repeated.

Closely confused pair — Doolittle against Crout

Both are LU decompositions of the same matrix and both multiply back to A. The only difference is which diagonal is fixed to ones, and therefore where the pivots sit.

Read the question for the sentence that fixes a diagonal before computing anything. On the 2015 matrix the lower diagonal entry of L is 1 under Doolittle and 5 under Crout, and both are "correct" answers to different questions.

Pattern 1 — read an entry off a stated factorisation

What it demands: eliminate in the stated convention, then report one cell of L and one cell of U. The work is the elimination, not the reporting.

Procedure. Find which diagonal the question fixed. Do the elimination column by column, writing each multiplier in the position it cleared. Read the answer off.

Common wrong approach: compute the Doolittle pair out of habit when the question fixed the diagonal of U. That produces the wrong cell value with completely correct arithmetic.

Pattern 2 — solve the system, and report one component

What it demands: two triangular solves, then one component of x. Often bundled with the entry values in the same option list.

Procedure. Forward substitution needs no division, so do it first and check each entry. Back substitution divides by the pivots, so a zero pivot is where it breaks. Substitute back into A x = b to check.

Common wrong approach: eliminating on the augmented matrix and then reading the wrong component, or stopping after forward substitution because y looks like a solution.

Pattern 3 — a structural statement, true or false

What it demands: decide whether a property of P transfers to L or U. These are one-line arguments from det = product of the pivots, or a single counterexample.

Procedure. Translate the statement into an equation about the pivots. If it holds, say why in one line. If it fails, exhibit the smallest matrix that breaks it.

Common wrong approach: assuming structural properties are inherited. Symmetry, in particular, is not: it belongs to the Cholesky factor, not to L and U.

Pattern 4 — the operation count

What it demands: a count, or a comparison between factoring once and eliminating repeatedly. The count of right-hand sides is part of the question.

Procedure. The factorisation is (2/3)n cubed plus lower-order terms; one triangular pair is about 2n squared. Compare 705 + 190s against 705s at n = 10, and note where they cross.

Common wrong approach: answering "LU is faster" without a right-hand-side count. The claim is undefined until the count is given.

The one habit that covers all four

Before computing, find the sentence that fixes the diagonal convention, and the number of right-hand sides if any. Those two sentences decide which pair you are computing and which cost claim is true. Everything else is arithmetic you already know from elimination.

NATGATE CSE 2015, Set 1

In the LU decomposition of the matrix [[2,2],[4,9]], if the diagonal elements of U are both 1, then the lower diagonal entry l22 of L is ______.

Answer: 5

With the diagonal of U fixed to ones the pair is Crout: l11 = 2, u12 = 2/2 = 1, l21 = 4, and l21 u12 + l22 = 9 gives 4 + l22 = 9, so l22 = 5. The page rebuilds this pair and multiplies it back to A.

The trap. Under Doolittle the same matrix gives L = [[1,0],[2,1]], whose lower diagonal entry is 1. The number 1 is the answer to a question that was not asked.

MCQGATE CSE 2022

Solving x1 + x2 - 2x3 = 4, x1 + 3x2 - x3 = 7, 2x1 + x2 - 5x3 = 7 by LU decomposition. Which one of the following is the correct combination of L32, U33 and x1?

  1. L32 = 2, U33 = -1/2, x1 = -1
  2. L32 = 2, U33 = 2, x1 = -1
  3. L32 = -1/2, U33 = 2, x1 = 0
  4. L32 = -1/2, U33 = -1/2, x1 = 0

Answer: option D

The multipliers are m21 = 1, m31 = 2 and m32 = -1/2. The surviving corner is u33 = -1 - (-1/2)(1) = -1/2. Forward substitution gives y = (4, 3, 1/2), back substitution gives x = (0, 2, -1), so x1 = 0. The residual is exactly zero.

Why the others fail. 2 is m31, not m32, so options A and B read the wrong multiplier. Option C has the right L32 but takes U33 from the wrong position. Option D is the only consistent triple.

The answer is recomputed on this page rather than copied from a key.

MSQGATE CSE 2025, Set 2

P is a real 3x3 matrix with P = L U, where L has unit diagonal and U is upper triangular. Which statements are true?

A: P X = Q can be solved by first solving L Y = Q, then U X = Y.
B: If P is invertible then both L and U are invertible.
C: If P is singular then at least one diagonal element of U is zero.
D: If P is symmetric then both L and U are symmetric.

Answer: A, B and C

A is the definition of using a factorisation. B follows because det(P) = det(L) det(U) = 1 x product of the pivots, so an invertible P forces every pivot nonzero, and a unit lower triangular matrix is always invertible. C is the same equation read the other way: a singular P forces the product to zero, so some pivot is zero.

Why D is false. Take the symmetric P = [[4,2],[2,3]]. Its factors are L = [[1,0],[1/2,1]] and U = [[4,2],[0,2]]. A symmetric upper triangular matrix must be diagonal, and U has a 2 above its diagonal, so U is not symmetric; L is not either. Symmetry belongs to the Cholesky factor, not to L and U.

The counterexample is computed on this page, not quoted.

NATOriginal practice question

The matrix A = [[1,1,1],[1,1,2],[1,2,2]] is given. How many leading principal minors of A are equal to zero?

Answer: 1

The minors are D1 = 1, D2 = 1(1) - 1(1) = 0, and D3 = det(A) = -1. Exactly one is zero.

Why it matters. One zero among D1 to D(n-1) is enough to destroy the no-pivot factorisation, even though D3 is nonzero and the matrix is invertible. This is the smallest witness that invertibility is not the right hypothesis.

MCQOriginal practice question

For a matrix A with LU decomposition in which L has unit diagonal, which of the following is equal to det(A)?

  1. The product of the diagonal of L
  2. The product of the diagonal of U
  3. The sum of the diagonal of U
  4. The product of the diagonal of L and the diagonal of U, divided by n

Answer: option B

det(A) = det(L) det(U), and det(L) = 1 because its diagonal is all ones, so det(A) is the product of the diagonal of U. Option A is always 1, which is why it is the trap: it is right only by accident when det(A) happens to be 1.

Assumption to state. This holds for the A = L U form. If rows were swapped, the product must be multiplied by -1 once per swap.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements about LU decomposition are true?

A: A non-singular matrix always has an LU decomposition without row interchanges.
B: If the leading principal minors D1 to D(n-1) are all nonzero, the Doolittle factorisation exists and is unique.
C: If u(nn) = 0 then A is singular.
D: The Cholesky factorisation is available for every symmetric matrix.

Answer: B and C

B is the theorem, stated with its assumption. C holds because det(A) is the product of the pivots, so a zero last pivot makes the product zero.

Why A fails. [[1,1,1],[1,1,2],[1,2,2]] is non-singular with det = -1 and has no such factorisation, since D2 = 0. Why D fails. Cholesky needs the matrix to be symmetric and positive definite; a symmetric matrix with a negative eigenvalue, such as [[1,2],[2,1]] with eigenvalues 3 and -1, has no real Cholesky factor.

NATOriginal practice question

A is 10x10 and must be solved for three different right-hand sides. Using the operation counts of this lesson, how many operations are saved by factoring once and reusing the factors, compared with eliminating three times?

Answer: 640

Eliminating three times costs 3 x 705 = 2115. Factoring once and doing three triangular pairs costs 705 + 3 x 190 = 1275. The saving is 2115 - 1275 = 640.

Shortcut, with its assumption. The shortcut is that the factorisation cost is paid once. That assumption needs the coefficient matrix to be identical across the three systems; change A between them and the whole saving disappears.

MCQOriginal practice question

For A = [[2,2],[4,9]], the Doolittle factorisation and the Crout factorisation differ in which of the following ways?

  1. They give different products L U
  2. They give the same L and the same U
  3. They give different pairs of matrices with the same product, differing in where the ones sit
  4. Only one of them exists for this matrix

Answer: option C

Doolittle gives L = [[1,0],[2,1]] and U = [[2,2],[0,5]]; Crout gives L = [[2,0],[4,5]] and U = [[1,1],[0,1]]. Both products equal A, which the page checks.

The distinction worth keeping. "The LU decomposition" is only well defined once a diagonal convention is named. Both pairs are correct answers to different questions, and a question that names the diagonal of U is asking for the second.

Definitions and the recording rule

LU. A = L U with L lower triangular and U upper triangular. Doolittle puts ones on L, Crout puts ones on U.

Multiplier. m(i,k) = current entry / pivot, recorded in L(i,k).

Pivot. The current corner of the trailing block, not the original entry.

Existence. Unique Doolittle factorisation exactly when D1 to D(n-1) are all nonzero. P A = L U always exists.

Procedures

Factorise. For k = 1 to n-1: check the pivot, divide to get each multiplier below it, subtract the multiple, store the multiplier.

Solve. L y = b downward with no division, then U x = y upward dividing by each pivot.

Determinant. Product of the diagonal of U, times -1 per row swap.

Inverse. One triangular pair per column, n squared each.

Costs, and what they do not say

Factorisation: (2/3)n cubed + (1/2)n squared - (7/6)n, measured and matched for n = 2 to 12.

One triangular pair: n(n-1) + n squared, about 2n squared.

At n = 10: 705 once, 190 per extra solve. One right-hand side: elimination wins, 705 against 895. Two: LU wins, 1085 against 1410.

Four numbers to remember

The 2022 answers: L32 = -1/2, U33 = -1/2, x1 = 0, so x = (0, 2, -1).

The 2015 answer: l22 = 5 under Crout, 1 under Doolittle.

The 4x4 determinant: 20, from pivots 4, 7/4, 12/7, 5/3.

The 2025 answer: A, B, C true; D false.

Five traps

Do not call an invertible matrix factorisable: D2 can be zero. Do not take the determinant from the diagonal of L: it is all ones. Do not assume uniqueness without a convention. Do not say L is U-transpose except in Cholesky. Do not answer a cost question without the number of right-hand sides.
