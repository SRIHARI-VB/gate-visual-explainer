Topic and objective

TOPIC: First-order resolution — unification, the most general unifier, Skolemisation, and the refutation rule that replaces a variable by a term instead of by an object.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Read a term as a finite tree and a substitution as a finite map from variables to terms. Apply and compose substitutions, and say why composition is associative but not commutative. Run Robinson's unification algorithm as a walk to the first disagreement, and name the three ways it fails, including the occurs check. State what makes a unifier most general and test that property rather than quoting it. Convert a prenex sentence to clauses with Skolem constants and Skolem functions of the right arity, and say exactly what Skolemisation preserves. Resolve two clauses after unifying one complementary pair, apply the unifier to everything else, and read the result as a tree that ends in the empty clause. Name the three limits of the method, and decide a previous-year item on quantifier order with a two-element countermodel.

I read this topic as the first-order completion of the previous lesson rather than as a new subject: the propositional rule is one page, and everything added here is the word unification.

Prerequisites

  • First-order logic — quantifiers, scope, free and bound variables, and translation between English and formulae. Every sentence here is read with that vocabulary.
  • Resolution refutation (propositional) — clausal form, one pivot per step, the empty clause, soundness and refutation-completeness. This lesson adds exactly one thing to that rule.
  • Propositional logic — the connectives, truth tables and logical equivalence, used whenever a ground instance is checked.
  • Sets and functions — a function symbol denotes a function, and a Skolem function is an ordinary function with a name.

The five ideas, in one line each

  • A term is a tree. Two terms are equal when the trees match node for node.
  • A substitution replaces variables only. Constants and function symbols are fixed by the signature.
  • Unification walks to the first disagreement. A variable there is repairable; two symbols there are not; a variable inside its own value is refused.
  • Use the most general unifier. It commits to nothing the pair does not force, so it keeps the resolvents that a specific one would lose.
  • Skolemisation preserves satisfiability, not meaning. The arity of the Skolem symbol is the number of universals to its left.

1. Intuition — two forms that must be filled in the same way

Imagine two application forms, each with blank boxes and each with some boxes already printed. You want to know whether the two forms can be made identical by writing something in the blanks — the same word in every box with the same name. That is unification: the printed entries are constants and function symbols, the blanks are variables, and the filled-in version is the substitution. Educational interpretation

Three things can go wrong, and they are the three failures of the lesson. Two printed entries can disagree in the same box, and no amount of writing fixes that. A blank can be asked to contain a word that itself contains that blank, which no finite word does. Or the first filling can change what a later box says, so a form that looked fillable turns out not to be.

Why not simply fill the blanks with real objects and use the propositional rule? Because a first-order sentence promises something about every object at once, and there may be infinitely many. Unification asks a question about the forms themselves, so no object is ever named.

2. Formal definitions

A term is a constant, a variable, or a function symbol applied to terms. An atom is a predicate applied to terms; a literal is an atom or its negation; a clause is a disjunction of literals, read as universally quantified over its variables.

A substitution is a finite map from variables to terms. Applying it replaces every occurrence of each variable in its domain. A substitution unifies a set of terms when applying it makes them all identical. A most general unifier (MGU) is a unifier σ such that every other unifier θ can be written θ = σ ∘ θ′ for some substitution θ′.

Skolemisation replaces each existentially quantified variable in a prenex sentence by a fresh function symbol applied to the universally quantified variables to its left; with none to its left, the symbol is a constant. First-order resolution: from clauses C₁ and C₂, renamed apart, with a positive literal of C₁ and a negative literal of C₂ whose atoms have MGU σ, infer σ applied to all remaining literals.

3. Visual understanding — what the drawing has to show

Unification is not a string operation, and a string is the wrong picture. The animation draws two trees and walks them in lockstep, so the first disagreement is a place on the canvas rather than a line in a trace. The binding that follows is drawn as a substitution printed under the trees, and the walk continues on the updated trees — which is exactly the step that people skip when they do this by hand.

The refutation scene uses the other natural picture: a proof is a tree whose leaves are the clauses and whose root is the empty clause. The count of leaves and steps is then a fact about a shape, not a claim to memorise.

4. Worked example — one walk, two bindings

Unify f(x, g(y)) with f(a, g(b)). The roots are both f with two arguments, so descend. First arguments: x against a — a variable against a constant, so bind x to a. Apply that: the terms become f(a, g(y)) and f(a, g(b)). Second arguments: g(y) against g(b) — same symbol, same arity, so descend again. y against b binds y to b. Nothing remains to compare.

The MGU is {x/a, y/b}. The page prints the two steps its engine actually took, including the running substitution after each one, so the answer is read off the computation rather than remembered.

5. Boundaries — when the method applies

Resolution applies to clauses, so the input must first be put in clausal form, which needs prenex form and Skolemisation. It is a refutation method: it proves a set unsatisfiable, and a goal is tested by adding its negation. It has no built-in equality, so a set that needs a = a requires the equality axioms or an extension such as paramodulation.

And it is only semi-decidable: by Church's theorem there is no algorithm that decides first-order validity, so a search that has not found the empty clause has proved nothing. Verified fact

Example 1 — a walk that succeeds

  1. Compare f(x, g(y)) with f(a, g(b)). Roots: f and f. Same symbol, same arity — descend.
  2. First arguments: x against a. A variable against anything is repairable, so bind x to a. Justification: no substitution can help a clash, but a variable is exactly what a substitution may change.
  3. Apply the binding before continuing. The terms are now f(a, g(y)) and f(a, g(b)).
  4. Second arguments: g(y) against g(b). Same symbol, same arity — descend again.
  5. y against b binds y to b. The disagreement set is empty, so {x/a, y/b} is a unifier.

The page checks the result the only way that settles it: it applies the substitution and compares the two terms.

Example 2 — a walk that starts well and dies

Unify f(x, x) with f(a, b).

  1. Roots agree; first arguments x against a give the binding x/a.
  2. Apply it. The first term becomes f(a, a) and the second is already f(a, b).
  3. Second arguments: a against b. Both are constants, and they differ, so this is a clash.
  4. No substitution fixes it: a and b are fixed names in the signature.

Why this is worth a card. The first step succeeded. A learner who stops after one successful binding reports success, and the answer is wrong.

Example 3 — Skolemise two sentences that look alike

∀x∃y R(x, y) — every x has some y. One universal sits to the left of the existential, so the witness may depend on x: the Skolem form is ∀x R(x, f(x)), and the clause is R(x, f(x)).

∃y∀x R(x, y) — one y serves every x. No universal sits to the left, so the witness depends on nothing: the Skolem form is ∀x R(x, c), and the clause is R(x, c).

The two clauses are not instances of each other. The first is satisfied by R = {(0,0), (1,0)} with f the constant-0 function; the second needs R(x, c) for the named c, and choosing c badly breaks it. The page counts the models where the sentence and its Skolem form disagree.

Example 4 — one resolution step, with the unifier written out

Clauses: ¬Man(x) ∨ Mortal(x) and Man(s).

  1. Rename apart: ¬Man(x₁) ∨ Mortal(x₁) and Man(s). The renaming changes nothing here, because the second clause has no variables.
  2. Choose the positive atom Man(s) and the negative atom ¬Man(x₁). Same predicate, same arity.
  3. Unify Man(s) with Man(x₁): the MGU is {x₁/s}.
  4. Apply it to the remaining literals: Mortal(x₁) becomes Mortal(s).

The resolvent is Mortal(s). One more step against ¬Mortal(s) gives the empty clause, with no substitution needed at all.

The occurs check, and what dropping it costs

x against f(x) must fail. Suppose x stood for some finite term t. Then t would have to be f(t), which is strictly larger than t, so no finite term qualifies. Without the check, the algorithm would either loop or build an infinite term.

The page searches the term universe up to nesting depth four and finds no value for x that satisfies the equation — which is what turns "the algorithm refused" into "the refusal is correct".

Two clauses that share a variable letter

¬P(x) and P(f(x)) ∨ Q(x) look as if they cannot resolve, because unifying P(x) with P(f(x)) needs x = f(x). But the two x's are different variables that happen to share a letter.

Renaming one clause apart gives ¬P(x₁) with P(f(x₂)) ∨ Q(x₂), which unifies with {x₁/f(x₂)} and yields Q(x₂). The page computes both routes and prints the count: one resolvent with renaming, none without. This is a completeness loss, not a soundness one — the wrong answer is "no", not "a false clause".

A factor is stronger than its clause

P(x) ∨ P(y) ∨ ¬R(x, y) factors to P(z) ∨ ¬R(z, z). The factor is entailed by the clause — instantiate x and y both to z — but the clause is not entailed by the factor: a model with R true only off the diagonal and P empty satisfies the factor and falsifies the clause.

So factoring is a strengthening step, not a rewriting step. It is sound because a consequence is all a resolution step needs, and it is required for completeness. The page prints the model that separates the two.

Skolemisation is not equivalence

∀x∃y R(x, y) and ∀x R(x, f(x)) are equisatisfiable and not equivalent. Take a domain {0, 1} and R = {(0,0), (1,0)}: the sentence is true, because every x has a witness. Now interpret f as the identity: f(1) = 1 and R(1,1) is false, so the Skolem form fails in that expansion.

The page counts exactly these models. The moral is directional: a Skolem form may be used to refute, never to claim that two sentences say the same thing.

A satisfiable set never reaches the empty clause

The search is run on 481 clause sets and compared with an exact truth table computed over the six ground atoms of a two-constant signature. The two agree on every set: 68 are unsatisfiable and produce the empty clause, and no satisfiable set produces it.

That comparison is the operational content of soundness. It is evidence, not a proof — the proof is the one-case argument that a resolvent is entailed by its parents, which the page also checks on all 1,024 interpretations of the two-element domain.

Matching is not unification

x against a succeeds: the variable takes the constant. a against f(y) fails: a constant cannot take a term, and f(y) is not a constant. So the same pair of symbols gives different answers depending on which side is allowed to move.

Pattern matching in a functional language is the one-way case, and it is why a pattern may contain variables but the value it is matched against may not.

"Most general unifier means the biggest one"

Why it tempts: "most" sounds like "maximum".

The correct rule: most general means least specific — it commits only to what agreement forces, so every other unifier is this one followed by a further substitution. For f(x,y) against f(y,a) the MGU is {x/a, y/a}; the unifier {x/a, y/a, z/b} says strictly more and is an instance of it.

Why it matters: a specific unifier throws away resolvents. Completeness is stated for the most general one.

"Resolution requires the two atoms to be identical"

Why it tempts: the propositional rule cancels P with ¬P, and P and P are identical.

The correct rule: they need a most general unifier, not identity. Man(s) and ¬Man(x) are different atoms and they resolve, because {x/s} makes them equal. That single relaxation is the whole difference between the propositional and the first-order rule.

The check: ask whether a substitution exists, not whether the strings match.

"Skolemisation gives an equivalent formula"

Why it tempts: the transformation is presented as a rewriting, and rewritings usually preserve meaning.

The correct rule: it preserves satisfiability only, and the Skolem form is strictly stronger because it names a witness. So it may be used to refute a set; it may not be used to assert that two sentences are equivalent.

The witness: ∀x∃y R(x,y) with R = {(0,0),(1,0)} and f the identity — true sentence, false Skolem form.

"A failed search means the set is satisfiable"

Why it tempts: in propositional logic the closure is finite, so a closed set without the empty clause really does mean satisfiable.

The correct rule: in first-order logic the term universe is infinite, so the search space is infinite and a search that has stopped has decided nothing. Only a model proves satisfiability, and only a refutation proves unsatisfiability.

The name for it: semi-decidability. A refutation is a proof; silence is not.

"Two quantifiers of different kinds can be swapped"

Why it tempts: two universals commute, and two existentials commute, so the habit generalises.

The correct rule: ∃y∀x R(x,y) implies ∀x∃y R(x,y), never the reverse. The first says one witness serves all; the second says each x gets its own.

The witness: on two elements, R = {(0,1),(1,0)} satisfies ∀x∃y and fails ∃y∀x. The page finds this model by search and prints it.

"Substitutions commute, because numbers do"

Why it tempts: composition is written with a small circle, which looks like multiplication.

The correct rule: composition is associative and not commutative. {x/y} then {y/a} makes x into a; the other order leaves x untouched, because the binding to y is applied after x has already been rewritten.

What still works: associativity is enough for the algorithm to carry one running substitution, which is why the convention must be fixed and then respected.

Pattern 1 — which of these is implied by F?

The question gives one quantified sentence and four candidates, and asks which follow. The work is not computation but a decision for each candidate: is there a model of F in which this candidate is false?

The reasoning it demands: a swap of two universals is free; a swap of two existentials is free; an existential pulled out of a universal is a strictly stronger claim; and a candidate that is F rewritten by pushing a negation inward is not a new claim at all.

The common wrong approach: testing candidates on one convenient model. One model can show that a candidate is not implied; it can never show that it is.

Pattern 2 — which formula is not valid?

The candidates differ only in where a quantifier sits relative to a subformula that does not mention the bound variable. The decision is a two-element countermodel, and the examinable rule is the scope rule: a quantifier may move past a subformula only when the variable is not free in it, and the direction of the move matters when a negation or an implication is involved.

The shortcut and its assumption: "test with one element first". With one element every quantifier collapses, so a one-element model can confirm invalidity but never validity. That assumption is why the page searches domains of size one and two before answering.

Pattern 3 — translate an English sentence with "exactly one"

"Everyone has exactly one mother" needs three pieces: existence of a mother, and uniqueness, which is written as a conditional over every other candidate. The usual wrong answers state at most one, or state existence twice with different witnesses and no constraint between them.

The reasoning it demands: exactly one is "at least one" and "at most one", and the second half is a universal with an implication, never an existential with a conjunction.

Pattern 4 — model size and quantifier prefix

A sentence whose prefix is ∃s∃t∃u∀v… and which has a model at all has a model with at most three elements: take the three witnesses the existentials name, and note that the quantifier-free part mentions nothing else.

The shortcut and its assumption: count the leading existentials, and remember that they are Skolem constants. The assumption is that the matrix is quantifier-free and mentions no other objects, which is exactly what the question states.

MCQGATE CSE 2017 Set 1

Consider the first-order logic sentence F: ∀x(∃y R(x, y)). Assuming non-empty logical domains, which of the sentences below are implied by F?

  1. I. ∃y(∃x R(x, y))
  2. II. ∃y(∀x R(x, y))
  3. III. ∀y(∃x R(x, y))
  4. IV. ¬∃x(∀y ¬R(x, y))

Options: (A) IV only (B) I and IV only (C) II only (D) II and III only

Answer: B — I and IV only

I follows. ∀x∃y R(x,y) with a non-empty domain gives some x, hence some y with R(x,y); two existentials commute, so ∃y∃x R(x,y) holds.

II does not follow. It demands one y that works for every x, which is a strictly stronger claim. Countermodel on two elements: R = {(0,1), (1,0)}. Every x has a y, but no single y serves both x. The page finds this model by search.

III does not follow. It reverses the relation. Countermodel on two elements: R = {(0,0), (1,0)}. Every x has a y, but element 1 has no preimage, so ∀y∃x R(x,y) fails.

IV follows. ¬∃x(∀y ¬R(x,y)) is ∀x¬(∀y¬R(x,y)) = ∀x∃y R(x,y), which is F itself. A rewritten sentence is not a new claim.

The trap. Swapping two quantifiers of different kinds. Two universals commute and two existentials commute; a universal and an existential do not.

MCQGATE CSE 2020

Which one of the following predicate formulae is NOT logically valid? Note that W is a predicate formula without any free occurrence of x. (The option order differs between archives, so the statements are given rather than the letters.)

  1. ∀x(p(x) ∨ W) ≡ ∀x p(x) ∨ W
  2. ∃x(p(x) ∧ W) ≡ ∃x p(x) ∧ W
  3. ∃x(p(x) → W) ≡ ∀x p(x) → W
  4. ∀x(p(x) → W) ≡ ∀x p(x) → W

Answer: the fourth statement, ∀x(p(x) → W) ≡ ∀x p(x) → W, is the one that is not valid

Why it fails. Rewrite the left side: ∀x(¬p(x) ∨ W) = ∀x¬p(x) ∨ W = ¬∃x p(x) ∨ W = ∃x p(x) → W. So the left side is equivalent to the existential form, not the universal one. A one-element countermodel with p true and W false separates them.

Why the other three hold. W has no free x, so the quantifier distributes over the connective: a disjunction under ∀ and a conjunction under ∃ both pass through, and the third statement is the derivation just given.

The trap. Distributing ∀ over an implication whose consequent does not mention the variable. The negation inside the implication silently turns the quantifier around.

NATOriginal practice question

Unify the two terms f(x, g(y)) and f(a, g(b)). How many bindings does the most general unifier contain?

Answer: 2

The walk descends through the agreeing root f, binds x to a at the first arguments, applies that binding, then descends through the agreeing g and binds y to b. Two disagreements, two bindings, and the MGU is {x/a, y/b}.

Why not more. A binding is created only where the two trees disagree, and there are exactly two such places. Descending through a node that agrees is not a binding.

Edge case. If the terms had been f(x, x) and f(a, b), the first binding would still be one, but the pair would fail — the count of bindings before failure is not an answer.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following pairs of terms have a most general unifier? Select all that apply.

  1. f(x, b) and f(a, y)
  2. f(x, x) and f(a, b)
  3. x and f(x)
  4. g(h(x)) and g(y)

Answer: A and D

A: two disagreements, both repairable — {x/a, y/b}. D: the roots agree, so y takes h(x) and the MGU is {y/h(x)}.

B fails after the first binding: x/a makes the second arguments a against b, which is a clash. C fails the occurs check: no finite term equals f of itself.

Why "most general" is in the question. If a pair has any unifier it has a most general one — the two questions are the same question, and the phrase is there to make you say so.

MCQOriginal practice question

Skolemise the sentence ∀x ∀z ∃y R(x, z, y). Which replacement is correct?

  1. R(x, z, c) with c a fresh constant
  2. R(x, z, f(x, z)) with f a fresh binary function symbol
  3. R(x, z, f(y)) with f a fresh unary function symbol
  4. R(x, z, f(x)) with f a fresh unary function symbol

Answer: B

The witness for y may depend on every universally quantified variable to its left, and there are two of them, x and z. So the Skolem symbol takes two arguments.

Why A is wrong. A constant says one witness serves every pair (x, z), which is the quantifier swap the sentence does not permit. Why C is wrong. y is the variable being replaced; it cannot appear in its own replacement. Why D is wrong. It forgets z, so the witness cannot vary with z.

Rule to carry: arity equals the number of universals to the left, and each existential gets its own fresh symbol.

MCQOriginal practice question

Resolve ¬P(x) ∨ Q(f(x)) with P(g(y)). Which clause is the resolvent?

  1. Q(f(g(y)))
  2. Q(f(x)) ∨ P(g(y))
  3. Q(f(g(y))) ∨ ¬P(x)
  4. No resolvent exists, because P(x) and P(g(y)) are not identical

Answer: A

Rename apart, then unify P(x₁) with P(g(y₂)): the MGU is {x₁/g(y₂)}. Apply it to the only remaining literal, Q(f(x₁)), which becomes Q(f(g(y₂))).

Why B fails. It keeps the cancelled pair — the pivot disappears, it does not move into the answer. Why C fails. It keeps the other parent's literal as well. Why D fails. Requiring identity instead of unifiability is exactly the propositional habit this lesson is about.

Shortcut and its assumption. Substitute the unifier into everything except the pivot, then drop the pivot. The assumption is that the unifier is most general, which it is.

NATOriginal practice question

The set {¬Man(x) ∨ Mortal(x), Man(s), ¬Mortal(s)} is refuted. How many resolution steps does the shortest refutation use?

Answer: 2

Step one resolves the first clause with Man(s) on the unifier {x/s}, giving Mortal(s). Step two resolves Mortal(s) with ¬Mortal(s) on the empty substitution, giving the empty clause.

Why not 3. A refutation uses each of the three clauses once and needs two joins; three clauses and a binary rule give two steps.

Edge case. If the goal had been added without its negation, no refutation would exist, because the set would be satisfiable.

MSQOriginal practice question

Which of the following statements about first-order resolution are true? Select all that apply.

  1. A resolvent is entailed by its two parents.
  2. If a search finds no empty clause, the set is satisfiable.
  3. Renaming the two clauses apart is needed for completeness.
  4. The most general unifier is unique up to renaming of variables.

Answer: A, C and D

A is soundness, checked here on all 1,024 interpretations of a two-element domain. C is why ¬P(x) with P(f(x)) ∨ Q(x) resolves only after the renaming: sharing the letter makes the occurs check fire. D is the uniqueness of the MGU.

B is false. That is the semi-decidability boundary. In first-order logic the search space is infinite, so a search that has stopped has decided nothing; only a model proves satisfiability.

The distinction being tested. Refutation-completeness (a refutation exists if the set is unsatisfiable) versus decidability (an algorithm that always answers). Resolution has the first and cannot have the second.

Definitions in one line each

  • Term: constant, variable, or function symbol applied to terms.
  • Substitution: finite map from variables to terms.
  • Unifier: a substitution that makes the terms identical.
  • MGU: a unifier σ such that every unifier θ equals σ ∘ θ′.
  • Skolem symbol: a fresh function symbol, arity = universals to its left; a constant when that count is zero.
  • Resolvent: the MGU applied to all non-pivot literals of two clauses renamed apart.

The procedure, in five lines

  1. Negate the goal; put everything in prenex form.
  2. Skolemise; drop the universals; read the matrix as a set of clauses.
  3. Rename two clauses apart; pick one positive and one negative atom of the same predicate.
  4. Take the MGU; apply it to the remaining literals; keep the resolvent if it is not a tautology and not already present.
  5. Stop at the empty clause (unsatisfiable) or when nothing new can be made (no refutation found — which proves nothing).

The traps, ranked by how often they are made

  • Swapping ∀ and ∃ of different kinds.
  • Treating a Skolem form as equivalent instead of equisatisfiable.
  • Reading a failed search as a model.
  • Resolving without renaming apart, so the occurs check refuses a legal step.
  • Forgetting to apply the unifier to the remaining literals.
  • Calling a factor equivalent to its clause.
  • Using the occurs check as an optional optimisation.

Four questions that decide almost every item

  • Is the input clauses? If not, prenex and Skolemise first.
  • Which unifier? The most general one, or the answer loses resolvents.
  • How many universals to the left? That is the Skolem arity.
  • What was preserved? Satisfiability, or meaning. Skolemisation preserves only the first.

One-sentence summary

First-order resolution is the propositional rule plus one idea — cancel a complementary pair after applying the substitution that makes them identical — and everything difficult about it lives in that substitution: when it exists, which one to choose, and what it costs when it is applied to a quantified sentence.

Textbook and university material

  • J. A. Robinson, "A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle", Journal of the ACM 12(1), January 1965, pp. 23–41. Supports: the disagreement set, the unification algorithm, the definition of a most general unifier, factoring, and the resolution theorem (a finite clause set is unsatisfiable if and only if it has a refutation).
  • J. Gallier, First-Order Logic (resolution chapter), University of Pennsylvania. Supports: the two fatal disagreements (a clash and the occurs check), the repairable case, the definition of MGU as θ = σ ∘ θ′, the lifting lemma, soundness and completeness, and Church's theorem that no algorithm decides first-order validity.
  • M. Vardi, COMP409 Lecture 25, Rice University. Supports: prenex normal form, Skolem normal form, and that Skolemisation is an equisatisfiable transformation rather than an equivalence.
  • N. Immerman, Herbrand's theorem lecture, UMass. Supports: the Skolem constant versus Skolem function distinction and the arity rule.

Reference entries

  • Skolem normal form, Wikipedia. Supports the statement that the Skolem form is equisatisfiable with the original and not necessarily equivalent to it, and the second-order reading of the transformation.
  • Resolution principle, Wolfram MathWorld. Supports the factoring definition: two or more unifiable same-sign literals are replaced by their instance.
