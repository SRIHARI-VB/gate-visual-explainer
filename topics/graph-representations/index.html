Topic and objective

TOPIC: Graphs as a data structure — how a graph is stored: the adjacency matrix, the adjacency list, the edge list and the incidence matrix. This is the word graphs in Section 4 of GATE 2027, and the representation step that every graph algorithm assumes.

LEARNING OBJECTIVE: Write one graph down three ways and read each back. Recover the edge count from the sum of a matrix and a degree from a row sum. Count the entries a list needs, for undirected and directed graphs. Compute the memory each representation costs under a stated machine model and find the density at which the list overtakes the matrix. Price the two operations that decide the choice — testing one edge, and walking every neighbour. Read A² as a walk counter and trace(A³)/6 as a triangle counter. See the cycle count appear as the nullity of the incidence matrix. And know why a degree sequence is not a graph.

I read this topic as a price list, not a format. The graph is one object; the matrix, the list and the incidence table are three prices for the same object. Almost every exam question here is either "what does this register say about the graph" or "which register is cheap for this operation", and almost every lost mark comes from quoting a cost without saying which representation it belongs to.

Prerequisites

  • Sets and ordered pairs. A graph is a set of vertices and a set of two-element subsets. Built in Sets and set operations.
  • Relations. An undirected edge is a symmetric relation; a directed edge is any relation. Built in Relations and their properties.
  • Functions, injective and bijective. Needed to say what a relabelling of the vertices does to a matrix. Built in Functions and their properties.
  • Arrays and linked lists. A head array plus a chain of nodes is exactly the list representation, and a two-dimensional array is exactly the matrix. Built in Arrays, stacks and queues and Linked lists.
  • Matrix multiplication. Powers of the adjacency matrix are ordinary products. Built in Matrices, systems and eigenvalues.
  • Big-O, Theta and exact counts. The whole cost half of this lesson is that vocabulary applied once. Built in Asymptotic complexity.

Nothing about traversal algorithms is assumed. BFS and DFS are mentioned only to price them; their mechanics live in Graph algorithms.

Visualization plan

Ten scenes, each one argument, all on the same six-vertex graph so that every difference you see is a difference in bookkeeping:

  1. Three registers. The graph drawn, then the matrix, the list and the edge table named beside it.
  2. The matrix fills. One edge sets two cells; seven edges later the grid is symmetric with a zero diagonal.
  3. Counting. A row-sum column appears, then the grand total, then the handshake lemma.
  4. The list. Chains fill vertex by vertex; C appears on four of them.
  5. Space. A three-row table of bytes, then the crossover at n = 1000.
  6. Two costs. One cell against a short scan, then 36 cell reads against 20 list steps.
  7. A squared. The product computed, its diagonal lit as the degree sequence, its off-diagonal as common neighbours.
  8. A cubed. The diagonal of A³ against triangles at each vertex, and why the divisor is six.
  9. Incidence. A vertex-by-edge grid, its column sums, and its nullity as the cycle count.
  10. Choosing, and the trap. Four questions, then K3,3 beside the prism with identical degrees.

The five ideas to leave with

  1. An undirected edge costs two cells in a matrix and two entries in a list. A directed edge costs one of each.
  2. The matrix is sized by vertices, the list by vertices and edges. That single sentence decides every space question.
  3. Constant-time edge test is what the matrix sells; cheap neighbour enumeration is what the list sells. You cannot buy both.
  4. Powers of the adjacency matrix count walks, and the trace of A cubed counts triangles six times over.
  5. A degree sequence is necessary for isomorphism and nowhere near sufficient. K3,3 and the prism prove it in six vertices.

Intuition: the class register and the noticeboard

A school has thirty students, and some pairs of them are friends. You want a record that answers two different questions. Question one: are Asha and Bina friends? Question two: who are all of Asha's friends?

The class register answers the first question beautifully: thirty rows, thirty columns, a tick in the box where the row's student and the column's student are friends. To answer, find the box. But to list Asha's friends you must read all thirty boxes in her row, even though most are empty. The noticeboard answers the second question beautifully: one card per student, and on Asha's card the names of her friends and nothing else. To answer, read one card. But to check whether Asha and Bina are friends you must read the whole card. Neither record is more correct. Each makes one question free and the other expensive, and the emptiness of the register — the boxes with no tick — is what you pay for when you choose it.

Level 1 — Intuition, in one sentence each

  • Graph. A set of vertices and a set of edges, each edge joining two vertices.
  • Simple graph. No loops and at most one edge between a pair.
  • Adjacency matrix. An n × n grid; a 1 where the two vertices are joined.
  • Adjacency list. One chain per vertex, holding only its neighbours.
  • Edge list. The m pairs, written out one per line.
  • Incidence matrix. An n × m grid; a 1 where a vertex touches an edge.
  • Degree. How many edges touch a vertex.
  • Representation. A choice of what to make cheap.

Level 2 — Formal definitions

Verified fact A graph is G = (V, E) with V a finite set of vertices and E a set of edges. In an undirected simple graph each edge is a two-element subset of V; in a directed graph each edge is an ordered pair. The number of vertices is written n, the number of edges m.

Verified fact The adjacency matrix A is the n × n matrix with A[i][j] = 1 when {i, j} is an edge and 0 otherwise. For an undirected graph A is symmetric and A[i][i] = 0 when there is no loop.

Verified fact The adjacency list is an array Adj of n lists; Adj[u] holds exactly the vertices v with an edge from u to v. Summing the list lengths gives 2m for an undirected graph and m for a directed one.

Verified fact The incidence matrix B is n × m, with B[i][e] = 1 when vertex i is an endpoint of edge e. Each column of an undirected incidence matrix has exactly two ones.

Level 3 — The structure the animation computes

One graph carries the whole lesson: vertices A to F, and seven edges — A-B, B-C, C-A, C-D, D-E, E-F, F-C. That is a triangle A-B-C and a four-cycle C-D-E-F, sharing the vertex C. The page builds all three registers from that edge list before anything is drawn, then checks each register against its own definition.

The matrix. Fourteen ones, symmetric about the diagonal, zero diagonal. Row sums give degrees 2, 2, 4, 2, 2, 2. The page checks that the entries above the diagonal number exactly m, that the row sums equal the degrees, and that the degrees sum to 2m.

The list. Six chains holding fourteen entries between them, with C appearing four times and every other vertex twice. The page checks that a vertex appears on exactly as many chains as its degree.

The incidence matrix. Six rows and seven columns, forty-two cells, fourteen of them set. Every column sums to 2. Over GF(2) the rank is 5 = n − c and the nullity is 2 = m − n + c, computed here by elimination. The page also eliminates over the reals and gets rank 6, not 5 — because this graph has a triangle, and the unsigned incidence matrix has real rank n − c only when the graph is bipartite. That discrepancy is checked against a bipartite example as well.

Level 4 — Worked example: building the matrix by hand

Take the edge list in order and set two cells for each edge:

A-B → A[0][1] and A[1][0]
B-C → A[1][2] and A[2][1]
C-A → A[2][0] and A[0][2]
C-D → A[2][3] and A[3][2]
D-E → A[3][4] and A[4][3]
E-F → A[4][5] and A[5][4]
F-C → A[5][2] and A[2][5]

Seven edges, fourteen cells. Row A holds ones at columns 1 and 2, so deg(A) = 2. Row C holds ones at 0, 1, 3 and 5, so deg(C) = 4. Every row sum is a degree, and the fourteen cells are the seven edges counted twice.

Level 4 — Worked example: the same graph as chains

A : B, C
B : A, C
C : A, B, D, F
D : C, E
E : D, F
F : E, C

Fourteen entries. Note the shape of the work: to test A-D you scan A : B, C and answer no after two reads; to test C-F you scan C : A, B, D, F and answer yes on the fourth. The cost of an edge test is the degree of the first endpoint, not a constant.

Level 5 — Boundaries: what each register can and cannot tell you

The matrix is indexed by vertices, so it is sized by n alone: it stores an empty graph and a complete graph in exactly the same space. The list is indexed by incidence, so it is sized by n and m together. That is the whole space argument, and everything else is arithmetic on top of it.

The other boundary is what a register loses. A matrix forgets nothing, which is why powers of A are meaningful. A list forgets the ordering of neighbours and, for a digraph, forgets in-edges unless you keep a second set of chains. An edge list forgets adjacency entirely: to find a vertex's neighbours you scan all m pairs. So "which representation" is really "which of my questions do I need to be cheap".

Level 6 — The counterexample that matters

Both of these are 3-regular on six vertices with nine edges:

K3,3: two groups of three, every cross pair joined. Degrees 3,3,3,3,3,3. Triangles: none, because a triangle would need two vertices from the same group.

The triangular prism: two triangles joined by three matching edges. Degrees 3,3,3,3,3,3. Triangles: two.

What it breaks: the belief that a degree sequence identifies a graph. The page computes both degree sequences, both edge counts and both triangle counts, and shows them equal on the first two and different on the third. Any argument of the form "the degrees are the same, so the graphs are the same" is dead at six vertices.

Level 6 — Closely confused: A² and A³

A² counts walks of length exactly 2. Its diagonal entry A²[i][i] counts closed walks of length 2, which are edges out and back, so it equals the degree — a fact, not a definition.

A³ counts walks of length exactly 3. Its diagonal entry counts closed walks of length 3, which are triangles walked round, and each triangle gives 2 at each of its three corners.

Why they get merged: both are "powers of A count walks". The difference is which power answers which structural question, and the divisor. For triangles the trace of A³ must be divided by 6; for degrees the diagonal of A² is already the answer, with no division.

Level 7 — How it is examined

Four shapes recur. A cost question names a representation and asks for the tightest bound on an operation — 2014 Set 1 is exactly this. A property question gives you A and asks which statements hold — 2022 Q10. A count question asks for the number of triangles, or of walks, in terms of a power of A — 2022 Q12. A structure question asks what a matrix condition forces the graph to be — 2024 Set 2 Q7.

None of them needs an algorithm. All of them need the two directions of the translation: register to graph, and graph to register.

GATE CSE 2014 Set 1 — the tightest bound on DFS

"Let G be a graph with n vertices and m edges. What is the tightest upper bound on the running time of Depth First Search on G, when G is represented as an adjacency matrix?"

Read the representation first. The question says adjacency matrix, so every vertex forces a full row read to find its neighbours.

Count the work. n vertices, each reading a row of n cells: n² cell inspections, whatever m is.

Answer: Θ(n²). Θ(n + m) is the adjacency-list figure, and it is the trap. Θ(n) is wrong because the row read is unavoidable, and Θ(m²) is wrong because the work does not depend on m at all. "Tightest" matters: n³ is also an upper bound and is not the answer.

GATE CSE 2016 Set 2 Q41 — twin pointers

Each edge of an undirected graph appears twice in the adjacency list, once in each endpoint's chain. Those two entries are twins. With no memory limit, how fast can every twin pointer be set?

The naive way. For each of the 2m entries, search the other endpoint's chain for the partner. Each search costs up to O(n), so O(mn) — and O(m²) on a dense graph.

The linear way. Walk all 2m entries once, keying each by the unordered pair {u, v} in a table. The first entry of a pair is stored; the second finds it and the two are linked.

Answer: Θ(n + m) (option B). The n term is the scan over the head array; the m term is the 2m entries. Memory is free by the question's own words, which is what makes the table legal.

GATE CSE 2022 Q10 — which properties hold (MSQ)

A is the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected unweighted graph on n vertices. Which statements hold?

(a) The diagonal entries of A² are the degrees. True. A²[i][i] = Σk A[i][k]·A[k][i] = Σk A[i][k]² = Σk A[i][k], which is the row sum, which is the degree.

(b) If the graph is connected then no entry of An−1 + I is zero. False. Take the path on three vertices: A² has zero entries, and adding I does not remove them.

(c) If the sum of all entries of A is at most 2(n−1) then the graph is acyclic. False. A triangle plus an isolated vertex has n = 4, m = 3, and 2m = 6 = 2(n−1), yet it has a cycle.

(d) If every row and column holds at least one 1 then the graph is connected. False. Two disjoint edges on four vertices satisfy the condition and are disconnected.

Answer: (a) only. Each false option is refuted by a graph small enough to draw in ten seconds, which is the intended method.

Example 1 — the space of three graphs

One bit per matrix entry; a 4-byte index per head; an 8-byte node per chain entry; undirected, so 2m entries.

n = 6, m = 7 → packed 5 B, 32-bit 144 B, list 136 B
n = 1000, m = 2000 → packed 125,000 B, 32-bit 4,000,000 B, list 36,000 B
n = 1000, m = 400000 → packed 125,000 B, 32-bit 4,000,000 B, list 6,404,000 B

The middle row is why lists exist; the first row is why "lists are smaller" is not a fact about lists. At six vertices the packed matrix wins outright, because 36 bits is less than 136 bytes no matter how sparse the graph is.

Example 2 — walks and triangles by matrix

For this graph the page computes A² and A³ in full and compares both against a brute-force walk enumeration.

Walks of length 2: the sum of all entries of A² is 36, and 2²+2²+4²+2²+2²+2² = 36. Both counts agree, which is the identity Σk deg(k)².

Walks of length 3: the sum of A³ is 88, checked by enumerating every three-step walk.

Triangles: trace(A³) = 2 + 2 + 2 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 6, and 6 / 6 = 1 triangle. The brute-force count over all triples also gives 1. Dividing by 3 would give 2, which is the number of directed triangles.

Example 3 — one degree sequence, two graphs

Take the degree sequence 3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3. The complete bipartite graph K3,3 realises it, and so does the triangular prism.

K3,3: groups {0,1,2} and {3,4,5}, all nine cross pairs joined. Nine edges. Triangle count 0, because two vertices of a triangle would have to lie in the same group and no edge does that.

The prism: triangles 0-1-2 and 3-4-5 plus the matching 0-3, 1-4, 2-5. Nine edges. Triangle count 2.

Same n, same m, same degree sequence, different graph. The page checks all three claims and then checks that the triangle counts differ — which is the proof that no degree-based test can decide isomorphism.

Boundaries, and what each one breaks

Each card names a condition the rules above assume, then shows the smallest case where the condition fails and what the answer becomes. Several of these are questions in their own right, because the edge case is the exam item.

A self-loop breaks the column sum of 2

In an undirected incidence matrix a loop touches one vertex twice. Depending on the convention the column holds a single 2, or two ones in the same row.

What it breaks: the sentence "every column has exactly two ones". It is true of simple graphs and only of simple graphs. In an adjacency matrix a loop is a 1 on the diagonal in some texts and a 2 in others, because a loop adds two to the degree.

A directed graph halves the list and doubles the asymmetry

For a digraph the out-chains hold m entries, not 2m, because an edge (u,v) is stored only in u's chain.

What it breaks: "the list holds 2m entries". Also the adjacency matrix is no longer symmetric, the row sum is the out-degree and the column sum is the in-degree. To find in-neighbours in a list you either scan everything or keep a second set of chains, which restores 2m.

A multigraph makes the matrix non-Boolean

Two parallel edges between the same pair are stored as a 2 in the matrix, and twice in each chain.

What it breaks: the assumption that entries are 0 or 1, and therefore that A²[i][i] equals the degree. With a 2 in the row the diagonal of A² counts 4 for that pair. Read whether the graph is simple before applying any identity that squares entries.

Labelling is part of the data

The matrix is defined only after the vertices are numbered. Reordering the vertices permutes the rows and the columns by the same permutation, so A becomes P·A·PT.

What it breaks: any claim about "the" matrix of a graph. Properties invariant under that conjugation — the trace, the eigenvalues, the number of ones — are properties of the graph; the entries themselves are properties of the labelling. That is exactly why the trace of A³ can count triangles.

An empty graph and a complete graph cost the same

Both have n vertices, so both take an n × n matrix. One holds no ones at all; the other holds n(n−1) of them.

What it breaks: the intuition that a structure's size should reflect how much it holds. The matrix's size reflects how many pairs could be joined. This is the whole reason the list exists, and the reason a matrix for a large sparse graph is a memory question rather than a time one.

Θ(n²) is not automatically "slow"

For a dense graph m is Θ(n²), so a matrix traversal in Θ(n²) is linear in the size of the input. For a sparse graph the same traversal is quadratic in the input size.

What it breaks: the slogan "the list is faster". Compare like with like: Θ(n²) against Θ(n+m), and then ask which of those is smaller for the graph you actually have. On the graph in this lesson they are 36 and 20.

n = 1, and the matrix of a single vertex

One vertex, no edges: a 1 × 1 matrix holding 0, one head, one empty chain, and an incidence matrix with one row and no columns.

What it breaks: formulas written without a base case. The packed matrix is ceil(1/8) = 1 byte, not 0; the rank of a 1 × 0 incidence matrix is 0, and n − c = 0, so the identity survives. Check the degenerate case before trusting a formula you derived on a picture.

A tree is the sparsest connected graph

Connected means m ≥ n − 1, and equality holds exactly for a tree. So for a connected graph the list is Θ(n) and the matrix is Θ(n²), and the gap grows without bound.

What it breaks: the idea that the crossover is a fixed m. It is a ratio: at n = 1000 the list wins while m < 7,562, which is m/n under about 7.6. A tree has m/n under 1, so a tree is always list territory.

Seven beliefs that produce the wrong option

Each card states the wrong belief, why it is tempting, the smallest case that refutes it, and the rule that replaces it. The refutations are computed on this page, not asserted.

1. "The adjacency matrix takes n² bytes"

Why it tempts: n² cells sounds like n² bytes, and every rough analysis says n².

Minimal counterexample: six vertices need 36 cells, which pack into 5 bytes, not 36. Stored as 32-bit integers the same matrix needs 144 bytes.

The rule: n² bits is the honest minimum; the word-per-entry implementation is a constant factor above it. Say which you mean, because the crossover with the list moves by that factor.

2. "The adjacency list holds m entries"

Why it tempts: there are m edges, so surely m entries.

Minimal counterexample: seven undirected edges give fourteen entries here, because each edge is written on both of its endpoints' cards. For a digraph the same seven edges give seven.

The rule: 2m for undirected, m for directed, plus n heads in both cases. State directed or undirected before you count.

3. "A degree sequence identifies a graph"

Why it tempts: the degree sequence is invariant under relabelling, so it feels like a fingerprint.

Minimal counterexample: K3,3 and the triangular prism are both 3-regular on six vertices with nine edges. One has no triangle; the other has two.

The rule: the degree sequence is necessary for isomorphism and not sufficient. Use triangle counts, the trace of A³, connectivity, or the number of spanning trees to separate candidates.

4. "The diagonal of A is the degree"

Why it tempts: the row sums are degrees, and the diagonal is right there in the middle.

Minimal counterexample: the diagonal of A here is all zeros while the degrees are 2, 2, 4, 2, 2, 2. The degrees are the row sums of A and the diagonal of A².

The rule: row sum of A = degree; diagonal of A² = degree; diagonal of A = loop or nothing. Three different objects, and the exam moves between them.

5. "Triangles are the trace of A³ divided by 3"

Why it tempts: a triangle has three corners, so dividing by three feels right.

Minimal counterexample: here trace(A³) = 6 and there is one triangle. Dividing by 3 gives 2, which is the number of directed 3-cycles, not the number of triangles.

The rule: divide by 6 — three starting corners times two directions. GATE CSE 2022 Q12 offers 2, 3 and 6 precisely to separate these.

6. "DFS on an adjacency matrix is Θ(n + m)"

Why it tempts: Θ(n + m) is the figure everyone memorises, and it is correct for the list.

Minimal counterexample: a path on n vertices has m = n − 1, yet DFS on its matrix still reads every row in full: n² cells for n − 1 edges.

The rule: Θ(n + m) with a list, Θ(n²) with a matrix. The representation is part of the question, not an implementation detail you may choose silently.

7. "The incidence matrix is just the matrix transposed"

Why it tempts: both are grids of zeros and ones describing the same graph.

Minimal counterexample: the adjacency matrix is n × n and its rows and columns are both vertices; the incidence matrix is n × m and its columns are edges. Transposing it gives m × n, not n × n.

The rule: adjacency is vertex-to-vertex, incidence is vertex-to-edge. They answer different questions, and the incidence matrix is the one whose nullity is the cycle count.

How the topic is asked, and the procedure that answers it

Four question shapes cover every item this lesson has seen. The procedure below is ordered so that each step can be done on the rough sheet in under a minute.

The procedure, in five steps

1. Fix the representation. Matrix, list, edge list or incidence. Every later sentence depends on this, and the question usually names it in one clause.

2. Count what it stores. 2m cells for an undirected matrix, 2m entries for an undirected list, m of each for a digraph, n + 2m for the list including heads.

3. Price the operation named. Edge test: O(1) on a matrix, O(deg) on a list. Enumerate neighbours: Θ(n) on a matrix, Θ(deg) on a list. Whole traversal: Θ(n²) against Θ(n + m).

4. If a power of A appears, translate it. Ak[i][j] is walks of length k; the diagonal of A² is degrees; trace(A³)/6 is triangles.

5. If degrees are given, look for a second invariant. Degrees alone never settle a structure question; the triangle count or the connectivity usually does.

The four question shapes

Costtightest bound for an operation, given a representation
Propertywhich statements about A hold — often an MSQ
Counttriangles, walks, or entries, from a power of A
Structurewhat a matrix condition forces the graph to be

A cost question is answered by counting one operation, not by recalling a table. A property question is answered by a counterexample small enough to draw. A count question is answered by the identity plus the right divisor. A structure question is answered by translating the matrix condition into a degree or a walk statement, then reasoning on the graph.

The four traps, and the phrase that signals each

  • "tightest upper bound" — a looser bound is a wrong option, so do not stop at the first bound you can prove.
  • "represented as an adjacency matrix" — the representation is given, so the list figures are traps.
  • "must necessarily be true" — one counterexample kills an option, and disconnected or degenerate graphs are usually the cheapest counterexample.
  • "at least three vertices" or "simple undirected" — these are the conditions that make an identity valid. Check them before applying trace(A³)/6 or A² diagonal = degree.

Two worked procedures on this graph

Cost. "How long to test every pair for adjacency?" Matrix: n² pairs, one read each, Θ(n²). List: for each vertex, scan its chain per pair, Σu deg(u)·deg(u) = Σ deg² = 36 here — the same 36, by a different route, and equal to the sum of A².

Structure. "A is its own inverse — what follows?" A symmetric and A·A = I means (A²)[i][i] = 1 for all i. But (A²)[i][i] is the degree, so every degree is 1, so the graph is a perfect matching. Answer: the graph is a perfect matching (GATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q7), and the derivation took two lines because the identity was already in hand.

MCQGATE CSE 2014 Set 1

Let G be a graph with n vertices and m edges. What is the tightest upper bound on the running time of Depth First Search on G, when G is represented as an adjacency matrix?

  1. Θ(n)
  2. Θ(n + m)
  3. Θ(n²)
  4. Θ(m²)

Answer: C — Θ(n²)

With an adjacency matrix, finding the neighbours of a vertex means reading its whole row of n cells. Doing that for all n vertices is n² cell inspections, independent of m.

Why the others fail. Θ(n + m) is the adjacency-list bound and is the most tempting wrong answer, because it is the figure usually memorised. Θ(n) cannot be right: no algorithm can find a vertex's neighbours without looking at its row. Θ(m²) is wrong in the other direction — the work does not depend on m at all, so a sparse graph is not cheaper here.

The distinction being tested. The same algorithm has two different bounds, and the representation decides which. Read the representation clause before reading the options.

MCQGATE CSE 2016 Set 2 Q41

In an adjacency list representation of an undirected simple graph G = (V, E), each edge (u, v) has two adjacency list entries: [v] in the list of u, and [u] in the list of v. These are called twins of each other. A twin pointer is a pointer from an entry to its twin. If |E| = m and |V| = n, and memory is not a constraint, what is the time complexity of the most efficient algorithm to set the twin pointer in every entry?

  1. Θ(n²)
  2. Θ(n + m)
  3. Θ(m²)
  4. Θ(n⁴)

Answer: B — Θ(n + m)

There are 2m entries. Walk them once, keying each entry by the unordered pair {u, v} in a table. The first entry of a pair is recorded; when its twin arrives it is found in O(1) and the two are linked. Total work is proportional to the 2m entries plus the n heads.

Why the others fail. Θ(m²) is what you get by searching the other endpoint's chain for the twin each time — correct but not the most efficient, and the question asks for the most efficient. Θ(n²) is a plausible-looking bound that is neither necessary nor tight, and Θ(n⁴) is not reachable by any sensible method.

What makes it linear. The clause "memory size is not a constraint". It licenses the table; without it, the extra n² space would be a cost the question did not allow.

MSQGATE CSE 2022 Q10

Which of the properties hold for the adjacency matrix A of a simple undirected unweighted graph having n vertices?

  1. The diagonal entries of A² are the degrees of the vertices of the graph.
  2. If the graph is connected, then none of the entries of An−1 + In can be zero.
  3. If the sum of all the elements of A is at most 2(n − 1), then the graph must be acyclic.
  4. If there is at least a 1 in each of A's rows and columns, then the graph must be connected.

Answer: A only

(A) A²[i][i] = Σk A[i][k]·A[k][i] = Σk A[i][k]² = Σk A[i][k], which is the row sum, which is the degree.

Why the others fail. (B) fails on the path on three vertices: A² has zero entries and adding I does not remove them. (C) fails on a triangle plus an isolated vertex: n = 4, m = 3, sum of entries 6 = 2(n − 1), and the graph has a cycle. (D) fails on two disjoint edges: every row and column holds a 1, and the graph is disconnected.

The pattern. Three of the four options are refuted by a graph you can draw in ten seconds. On a "must necessarily be true" MSQ, build the smallest counterexample first and only then try to prove.

MCQGATE CSE 2022 Q12

Consider a simple undirected unweighted graph with at least three vertices. If A is the adjacency matrix of the graph, then the number of 3-cycles in the graph is given by the trace of

  1. A³
  2. A³ divided by 2
  3. A³ divided by 3
  4. A³ divided by 6

Answer: D — trace(A³) / 6

A closed walk of length 3 from a vertex back to itself is a triangle walked round. Each triangle has three corners, and from each corner it can be walked in two directions, so one triangle contributes 2 to each of three diagonal entries: 6 in the trace.

Why the others fail. trace(A³) alone counts every directed closed 3-walk, so it is six times too large. Dividing by 2 or by 3 under-corrects; dividing by 3 gives the number of directed triangles. On this lesson's graph, trace(A³) = 6 and the answer is 1; dividing by 3 would give 2, which is visibly not the number of triangles in a picture with one triangle.

The condition. "Simple undirected" is what makes the count exact: a loop or a parallel edge would add closed walks of length 3 that are not triangles.

MCQGATE CSE 2024 Set 2 Q7

Let A be the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph G. Suppose A is its own inverse. Which one of the following statements is always TRUE?

  1. G is a cycle
  2. G is a perfect matching
  3. G is a complete graph
  4. There is no such graph G

Answer: B — G is a perfect matching

A = A−1 means A·A = I, so (A²)[i][i] = 1 for every i. But the diagonal of A² is the degree sequence, so every degree equals 1. A graph in which every vertex has degree 1 is a perfect matching.

Why the others fail. A cycle C₄ has A² ≠ I: its diagonal entries are 2. A complete graph Kₙ with n ≥ 3 has degrees n − 1. And option D is refuted by the smallest example: two disjoint edges on four vertices, whose A² is the identity.

The shortcut and its assumption. "A² = I, and the diagonal of A² is the degree, so all degrees are 1" needs the graph to be simple — without that, a loop or a 2 in a row would break the second step.

NATOriginal

An undirected simple graph has 8 vertices and 12 edges. How many entries of its adjacency matrix are equal to 1?

Enter a single integer.

Answer: 24

Each undirected edge sets two cells, one on each side of the diagonal. Twelve edges therefore set 24 cells, and the sum of all entries of A is 24 = 2m.

The trap. Answering 12 forgets the second cell. Answering 96 multiplies by 8, which is not a quantity in this problem. And if the graph were directed with 12 arcs, the answer would be 12 — the word "undirected" is doing the work.

NATOriginal

For the cycle graph C₄ on four vertices, what is the sum of all entries of A², where A is its adjacency matrix?

Enter a single integer.

Answer: 16

The sum of all entries of A² is Σk deg(k)². Every vertex of C₄ has degree 2, so the total is 4 × 4 = 16.

Checking it directly. The diagonal of A² is 2, 2, 2, 2. Each off-diagonal entry is the number of common neighbours, which is 1 for a pair at distance 2 and 0 for adjacent pairs, so the off-diagonal sum is 8. Total 8 + 8 = 16.

The trap. Reading A² as a 0/1 matrix and counting ones: A² is not Boolean here, and its entries are counts. The question asks for the sum of the entries, not the number of non-zero entries.

MSQOriginal

Which of the following statements about the vertex-edge incidence matrix B of a connected simple undirected graph with n vertices and m edges are true?

  1. Every column of B has exactly two ones.
  2. Every row sum of B equals the degree of that vertex.
  3. The rank of B over GF(2) is n.
  4. The nullity of B over GF(2) is m − n + 1.

Answer: A, B and D

(A) Each edge has two endpoints, so its column holds two ones. (B) Row i counts the edges touching vertex i, which is its degree. (D) Since the rank is n − 1 and there are m columns, the nullity is m − (n − 1) = m − n + 1, the cycle-space dimension.

Why C fails. Over GF(2) every column sums to 2 = 0, so the all-ones vector is in the left null space and one row is always redundant. The rank is n − 1 for a connected graph, never n. On this lesson's graph that is 5, computed on the page by elimination in GF(2).

The distinction. The field matters. Over GF(2) the rank is n − c for every graph; over the reals the unsigned matrix has rank n − c only when the graph is bipartite, and n otherwise — which is what this lesson's own page reports, since its graph has a triangle. An oriented incidence matrix restores n − c over the reals.

MCQOriginal

A connected undirected graph is stored as an adjacency list with n heads and one 8-byte node per entry. Under a model of 4 bytes per head, which of these graphs uses the most memory?

  1. A tree on 1000 vertices
  2. A cycle on 1000 vertices
  3. A graph on 1000 vertices with 2000 edges
  4. A complete graph on 100 vertices

Answer: D — the complete graph on 100 vertices

Memory is 4n + 16m. (A) 4,000 + 16(999) = 19,984 B. (B) 4,000 + 16(1,000) = 20,000 B. (C) 4,000 + 32,000 = 36,000 B. (D) n = 100, m = 100·99/2 = 4,950, so 400 + 79,200 = 79,600 B.

Why the others fail. The three options on 1000 vertices look bigger because n is ten times larger, but the list is sized by m as well. The complete graph on 100 vertices has 4,950 edges against at most 2,000 for any option on 1000 vertices.

The lesson. "More vertices" is not "more memory" for a list. Count m before you compare.

MCQOriginal

You are given only the degree sequence 3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3 of a simple undirected graph on six vertices. Which of the following can you conclude?

  1. The graph contains no triangle.
  2. The graph contains exactly two triangles.
  3. The graph has exactly nine edges.
  4. The graph is bipartite.

Answer: C — exactly nine edges

The handshake lemma gives m = (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3)/2 = 9. That is the one quantity the degree sequence fixes.

Why the others fail. (A) is refuted by the triangular prism, which is 3-regular with two triangles. (B) is refuted by K3,3, which is 3-regular with none. (D) is refuted by the prism, which has odd cycles. Both counterexamples are computed on this page.

The distinction being tested. A degree sequence fixes the number of edges and nothing else of interest. Any option that names a structure is a claim about all realisations, and one counterexample settles it.

One-screen revision sheet

Everything on this page, compressed to what you would write on the rough sheet in the first minute of the paper. State the representation and the machine model before quoting a cost or a byte count.

The four registers, in one line each

  • Adjacency matrix: n × n; 2m ones undirected, m directed; symmetric; zero diagonal for a simple graph.
  • Adjacency list: n heads plus 2m entries undirected, m directed; neighbours only.
  • Edge list: m pairs; adjacency costs a full scan.
  • Incidence matrix: n × m; two ones per column; rank n − c; nullity m − n + c.

Costs, side by side

edge testO(1) matrix · O(deg) list
enumerate neighboursΘ(n) matrix · Θ(deg) list
whole traversalΘ(n²) matrix · Θ(n+m) list
spacen² bits · 4n+16m bytes
transposeΘ(n²) matrix · Θ(n+m) list
in-degrees (digraph)Θ(n²) matrix · Θ(n+m) scan, or 2× space

The byte figures assume one bit per matrix entry, a 4-byte head and an 8-byte node. Change the model and the constants move; the orders do not.

Three identities, and what each needs

Row sum of A = degree. Needs an undirected graph; for a digraph it is the out-degree.

Diagonal of A² = degree. Needs a simple graph, so that every entry is 0 or 1 and squaring does nothing.

trace(A³)/6 = triangles. Needs a simple undirected graph; a loop or a parallel edge adds closed 3-walks that are not triangles.

Six traps, six answers

  • Counting list entries? 2m undirected, m directed.
  • Quoting n² bytes? It is n² bits; bytes are a model choice.
  • DFS on a matrix? Θ(n²), not Θ(n + m).
  • Triangles from A³? Divide by 6.
  • Same degree sequence? Not the same graph — check triangles.
  • Rank of an incidence matrix? n − c over GF(2); over the reals only if bipartite.

Where this leads next

Every algorithm in Graph algorithms is written against one of these registers, and its bound is decided by which. A traversal's Θ(n + m) versus Θ(n²), Kruskal's use of an edge list, Prim's use of a priority queue over adjacency lists, and Dijkstra's relaxation step are all representation choices in disguise. The triangle count returns in Counting and combinatorics, and the incidence matrix's nullity is the same cycle-space argument that appears in Relations as a kernel computation.
